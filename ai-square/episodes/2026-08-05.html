Full transcript

Maya: What if AI agents were becoming both our most powerful tool and our biggest vulnerability at the same time? Welcome to AI Square, the podcast that breaks down artificial intelligence in your everyday world. Today, we're diving into a landscape where AI agents are taking over insurance, cybersecurity, and software development, and where the question of governance is becoming absolutely critical.

Maya: Ryan, hey, good to have you here! Let's kick things off with our big story of the day, and honestly there's no shortage of options this morning. But the thing that's really grabbing my attention is this wave of solutions specifically designed to secure AI agents. We had three major announcements drop within just a few hours of each other. What's going on?

Ryan: Hey Maya! You're right, that is the headline signal of the day. And it's no coincidence that all of this is hitting at once. We are right in the middle of Black Hat USA 2026, the biggest cybersecurity conference in the world. And this year, AI agents have taken center stage in the conversation. Airlock Digital used the event to unveil a control and governance solution for AI agents, specifically built for endpoints. Now that might sound a little technical, but the core idea is simple: when an AI agent is running on your company's machines, what exactly is it doing? Who's watching its every move in real time?

Maya: And up until now, the honest answer to that question was basically nobody, right?

Ryan: Exactly. AI agents have gotten really capable, really fast. They can execute commands, access files, spin up processes. And for a long time, security teams had almost zero visibility into what was happening at the level of each session, each individual command. Airlock Digital is offering granular visibility: you can see precisely what an agent is doing, command by command, and you can set centralized policies to allow or block specific actions.

Maya: Got it. And then in that same day, KnowBe4 announces something along the same lines?

Ryan: Yeah, but with a complementary angle. KnowBe4 is integrating Claude, Anthropic's model, into what they're calling their Agent Risk Manager. The idea is to use one AI to watch another AI. Claude continuously analyzes the actions, the outputs, and the tool usage of agents deployed inside a company. And their stated goal is to prevent an agent from becoming what they call a backdoor into sensitive assets. In plain terms: if an attacker compromises an AI agent, they should not be able to use that agent to freely roam around your entire system.

Maya: Which actually leads us right into our cybersecurity story of the day, because Zero Networks is also in the mix here.

Ryan: Yes, Zero Networks is launching what they call Least Agency Enforcement. Think of it as borrowing the well-established principle of least privilege from traditional security and applying it to AI agents. With least privilege, a human user should only have access to the resources they absolutely need to do their job. Zero Networks applies that exact same logic to AI agents. An agent that handles billing has no business touching HR data. A customer support agent has no reason to be executing system commands. By capping what an agent is allowed to do at the bare minimum, you dramatically shrink the attack surface.

Maya: And this ties directly into the whole autonomous ransomware threat, right? Because we're hearing more and more about these scenarios where a compromised AI agent becomes the actual attack vector.

Ryan: One hundred percent. This is the unsettling frontier that the security community is starting to seriously explore. A compromised or misconfigured AI agent could, in theory, carry out malicious actions completely on its own, with no human in the loop. It could encrypt files, exfiltrate data, spread to other systems. And that's exactly why all of these governance solutions are rushing out the door right now, while enterprise AI agent deployments are exploding. The window to lock these systems down before they get massively exploited is narrow.

Maya: Alright, let's shift over to our tools and frameworks segment. And again, today was packed. Let's start with Snyk, because what they're announcing is pretty groundbreaking.

Ryan: Snyk is a really well-known name in code security, and they're taking a significant step forward with what they're calling continuous AI pentesting and agent red teaming. For listeners who aren't deep in the security world: pentesting, short for penetration testing, is when security experts try to hack your own system to find the vulnerabilities before the real bad guys do. Red teaming takes it further by simulating a full-blown attack with realistic scenarios. Snyk is now applying both of those methods to AI agents running in production.

Maya: So in practice, they're literally trying to hack your own AI agents to see what happens?

Ryan: Exactly. And this is crucial because AI agents have emergent behaviors that are incredibly hard to predict in a standard testing environment. An agent can pass every unit test with flying colors and then behave in unexpected, even dangerous ways once it hits real-world conditions. Snyk's solution lets you visualize AI components as they run in production, but more importantly, it models how an attacker could actually exploit them. It's a real paradigm shift: you're not just testing whether the agent does what it's supposed to do, you're also testing how it could be hijacked.

Maya: We also have Microsoft publishing a framework aimed at cutting the cost of training AI agents. Is that a big deal?

Ryan: It's a huge deal for democratization. One of the biggest barriers to deploying AI agents is cost. Training an agent, fine-tuning it to be effective in a specific business context, can run into the tens or even hundreds of thousands of dollars. Microsoft Research is putting out a framework designed to meaningfully reduce those costs. We don't have all the technical details yet, but the implications are clear: if training an AI agent becomes something a small or mid-sized business can afford, not just a Fortune 500 company, adoption is going to accelerate dramatically.

Maya: And that loops us right back to the security concerns we were just talking about, because more agents deployed means more potential attack surface.

Ryan: That's exactly the connection to draw. And it's why you really have to read the security announcements and the tooling announcements together. There's also Sinch launching their Agent Tools for developers, a suite that lets you build and deploy applications by plugging directly into Sinch's infrastructure, especially around communications. The idea is that developers can stay in their familiar environments, their usual coding tools and AI coding assistants, and connect everything to Sinch without any friction.

Maya: Okay, let's move to our research segment. And I came across two studies today that I think are really eye-opening. The first one is from Dovetail Software, and they're predicting that 40 percent of AI agent projects are going to fail. That's a pretty grim forecast.

Ryan: Grim, but well-supported. Dovetail Software analyzed failure patterns in agentic AI projects and identified three main causes. The first one is not having a clear definition of success. A lot of companies kick off an AI agent project with fuzzy goals, something like "improve operational efficiency." But how do you actually measure that? Without precise metrics, you never really know if the project is working or not, and you keep pouring money into something that isn't really delivering.

Maya: What's the second cause?

Ryan: Data access. And this might be the most underestimated one. You build an agent in a controlled environment with clean, well-structured data. Then you deploy it in production and suddenly realize that the real data lives across seven different systems, with incompatible formats, fragmented access rights, and data silos everywhere. The agent simply cannot do its job because it can't get to the information it needs in the first place.

Maya: And the third?

Ryan: No designated owner. When an AI agent makes a mistake in production, who is responsible? The AI team? The business unit? The IT department? In a lot of organizations, that question doesn't have a clear answer. And without an identified owner, problems pile up without getting solved, fixes don't get prioritized, and the project slowly dies on the vine.

Maya: When you lay it out like that, these are really human problems, not technical ones.

Ryan: That's exactly right. The AI agent technology itself is mature enough at this point. The failures aren't coming from bugs in the models. They're coming from governance, organizational structure, and project management issues. And the other notable research today points in the same direction. HackerNoon is publishing a clarifying analysis aimed at cutting through the terminology confusion that's been running rampant in the developer community.

Maya: The confusion between RAG, AI agents, and agentic AI. Because those terms get thrown around pretty interchangeably, don't they?

Ryan: And that's a real problem. An AI agent in the strict sense is a large language model combined with tools it can use and a memory. RAG, or Retrieval Augmented Generation, is a technique for giving an LLM access to an external knowledge base, like a company's internal documents. And agentic AI is the orchestration of multiple agents collaborating to accomplish complex tasks. These three concepts often get layered together, but they are not synonyms. And when a developer conflates all three, they end up making bad architectural decisions right from the start.

Maya: Let's talk industry impact now, because the Guidewire announcement feels really concrete and tangible.

Ryan: Guidewire is a dominant player in software for insurance companies. And their announcement around the Quasar release is genuinely significant for that sector. They're launching a full agentic framework that lets insurers build and deploy AI agents with secure access to the critical data that makes insurance run: policy data, claims, billing. What's really interesting is their workflow-based approach. They're not pitching one general-purpose agent that does everything. They're offering specialized agents, each one a master of a specific process.

Maya: Like one agent that only handles claims filings, and a separate one that only deals with billing?

Ryan: Exactly. And that specialization comes with several real advantages. First, much better performance. An agent focused on a narrow domain is far more reliable than a generalist agent. Second, simpler governance. You know precisely what each agent does, what data it looks at, what actions it can trigger. And third, clear accountability, which directly addresses that third failure mode Dovetail Software flagged.

Maya: Insurance is also a sector where compliance stakes are enormous. Are regulators keeping up?

Ryan: That is the question in the industry right now. Regulators are playing catch-up. In Europe, the AI Act sets strict requirements for high-risk AI systems, and insurance absolutely falls into that category. Guidewire has clearly made regulatory compliance a central pillar of their framework, with granular access controls and full audit trails. That's not a nice-to-have, it's a requirement for their customers to be able to deploy these agents without running afoul of the law.

Maya: Zooming out and looking at everything we've covered today, what's the one big takeaway you're walking away with?

Ryan: The big takeaway is that the industry is crossing over into a maturity phase for AI agents. For the past two years, we've been talking about potential, impressive demos, theoretical use cases. Today, agents are in production. They're doing real things, with real data, inside real companies. And naturally, that raises real questions: how do you govern them, how do you secure them, how do you measure their success and manage their failures? Every single announcement today, whether it's Airlock, KnowBe4, Zero Networks, Snyk, or Guidewire, is answering the same fundamental question: how do you actually trust an AI agent in production?

Maya: And the answer right now is that you don't trust blindly. You verify, you monitor, you constrain.

Ryan: Exactly. Trust gets built through control mechanisms, not through wishful thinking. And honestly, that's encouraging, because it shows the industry is taking the risks seriously instead of brushing them under the rug.

Maya: And that brings us to the end of today's episode. Here's the recap of a packed day: AI agents are moving into new sectors, with Guidewire pushing into insurance and Microsoft working to cut training costs and open the door for broader adoption. But at the same time, a whole governance and security industry around AI agents is being built out at a rapid pace, with tools from Airlock Digital, KnowBe4, Zero Networks, and Snyk. And researchers are reminding us that 40 percent of projects are at risk of failure, not because of the technology, but because of human decisions: no metrics, no data access, no designated owner. Agentic AI is powerful. Making it reliable, secure, and governable is the defining challenge of this year. Ryan, thanks so much for breaking all of this down with me today.

Ryan: Always a pleasure, Maya. There is a lot more coming on all of these topics, I don't think we're even close to being done talking about this.

Maya: Not even close. Thanks so much for listening to AI Square.
