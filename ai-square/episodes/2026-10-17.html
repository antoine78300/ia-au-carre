Emma: Good morning and welcome to AI Squared, your daily dose of artificial intelligence. It is Saturday, the weekend team is here, the coffee is hot, and the AI news absolutely did not take the day off. I am Emma, joined as always by Liam, our go-to AI analyst. We have got some genuinely fascinating stories for you this morning, so get comfortable and let us dive right in.

Emma: Liam, let us kick things off with the biggest story of the week, and it comes all the way from Australia. Prime Minister Anthony Albanese's government put forward a policy on using creative works to train AI models. And the reaction from the creative community was, let us just say, anything but lukewarm.

Liam: That is putting it mildly. Ninety percent of Australia's creative coalition rejected the plan. Ninety percent. That number really deserves a moment. The government wanted to allow AI companies to use copyright-protected Australian music to train their models, and artists, record labels, authors, journalists, they all said no, in overwhelming numbers.

Emma: But hold on, was the Australian government actually saying companies could just take creative works without asking?

Liam: And this is where it gets interesting, because Albanese's own position is actually pretty nuanced. The Prime Minister himself has stated that AI companies must get permission from creators and pay them fair compensation. He is even planning to legislate on this in 2027, covering music, books, visual arts, and journalism. It is the first government initiative of this scale that tries to clearly define what authorization and fair pay actually mean in the context of AI training.

Emma: So the government is essentially siding with creators here, and yet ninety percent of the creative sector is still saying no?

Liam: Exactly, and that tells you everything about the level of distrust that exists right now in the creative world. Even when a government tries to put guardrails in place, the response is, we do not trust that this will be enough, or that it will actually be enforced. And honestly, that is understandable. This is an industry that can already see massively funded AI music generators coming down the road.

Emma: And speaking of which, let us talk about that. Because while Australia is having this debate, the corporate side of things is moving fast, and two very different visions are emerging.

Liam: Yeah, and the contrast is striking. On one side, you have players like Suno, and major labels Universal, Sony, and Warner, who have jointly invested seventy-six million dollars in Stability AI to develop autonomous music generators. We are talking tools that produce a complete song from start to finish, with no human musician in the loop whatsoever.

Emma: And on the other side?

Liam: On the other side, you have companies like Moises going in the completely opposite direction. Their whole philosophy is building tools that assist the musician rather than replace them. Stem separation, contextually aware instrument suggestions, creative building blocks that the musician themselves assembles. They are talking about giving artists Lego bricks, not handing them a finished house.

Emma: That is almost like two philosophies of AI in general, right? The AI that does it for you versus the AI that gives you superpowers.

Liam: Exactly. And YouTube Music just jumped into this debate in a very concrete way. They have started rolling out labels on songs to flag when a track was created primarily by generative AI.

Emma: Oh, like nutrition labels on food?

Liam: That is exactly it. And it raises a genuinely tricky technical question, because the line between a fully synthetic song and a hybrid one, part human, part AI, can get really blurry really fast. YouTube has to make that call. And to put the scale in context, ninety thousand new tracks are uploaded to music platforms every single day. Ninety thousand, every day.

Emma: That is just mind-bending. Alright, let us shift gears because there is another story this week that genuinely stopped me in my tracks. We are talking humanoid robots, and things are getting real.

Liam: Very real. Faraday Future and FF EAI Robotics announced on October 15th the FF Nova. It is a consumer-grade AI humanoid robot, and it is priced at under two thousand five hundred dollars in the US.

Emma: Two thousand five hundred dollars. That is like, the price of a high-end smartphone.

Liam: That is exactly the point. We are crossing a threshold here, moving from an era where humanoid robots are lab prototypes costing tens of thousands of dollars, to something you can actually start to think of as a consumer product. They also announced the FF Nova Mini, an educational version designed for kids, with courses and training resources for teachers included.

Emma: A robot in the classroom is something I would have placed way further into the future.

Liam: And running alongside that, there was another announcement that I think is even more exciting for the developer and research community, dropping on October 16th. RoboParty launched the RP1, nicknamed Roboto 01. And this one is fully open source, top to bottom.

Emma: Fully open source, what does that actually mean for a robot?

Liam: It means researchers can examine, modify, and adapt both the hardware and the software. Every layer of the system is open. That is a radical break from the black-box logic that has dominated this space up until now. They unveiled it at the IROS 2026 conference, and it is now officially available to the entire research community.

Emma: So on one hand, a low-cost robot for everyday consumers, and on the other, a fully open-source robot for researchers. It has been a packed week for humanoids.

Liam: And we are not done yet. Because if we turn to the more academic side of things, Europe dropped a massive announcement this week.

Emma: Yes, RAISE! Tell us about it.

Liam: On October 15th, the European Commission launched RAISE, which stands for Resource for AI Science in Europe. The idea is to give more than three million European researchers access to high-powered AI infrastructure. They are building on thirty-five supercomputers currently in development across Europe. And NVIDIA has committed to supporting over two hundred and fifty open models and datasets to fuel the whole thing.

Emma: Three million researchers. That is democratization of AI access in science at a genuinely massive scale.

Liam: And it connects directly to what University College Dublin is doing. They just created a new multidisciplinary AI institute bringing together medicine, agriculture, climate science, and the humanities, all working side by side with AI specialists. And they already have concrete projects underway: digital twins for pediatric oncology, diagnostic tools for preeclampsia, weather forecasting models.

Emma: This is what I love about these stories. AI is no longer just the territory of computer science labs. It is becoming a cross-cutting tool for researchers across every field.

Liam: And this is a global movement. Google.org just announced a five-million-dollar grant to Georgia Tech with exactly the same ambition: expanding access to AI research tools to mid-sized universities, not just the big well-funded institutions.

Emma: So you have got Europe with RAISE, Ireland with UCD, the US with Georgia Tech. The same wave, breaking everywhere at once.

Liam: It is a convergence that says something really profound about where scientific research is heading right now.

Emma: Alright Liam, we have made it to my favorite part of the show. The What If. And with everything we have covered today, there is a question that has been sitting in the back of my mind all week.

Liam: Let us hear it.

Emma: What if the two big battles we saw this week, the copyright fight in music and the open-source push in robotics, are actually the same battle?

Liam: Now that is a great question. Walk me through it.

Emma: In Australia, creators are fighting to hold onto control over what they make, to keep it from being absorbed without their consent into opaque, closed systems. And in robotics, RoboParty is doing the exact opposite: opening everything up, refusing the black-box model entirely. In one case people are fighting against the loss of control, and in the other they are building systems where control is shared by design.

Liam: That is a fundamental tension. And I think you have put your finger on something really important. The question of who controls what inside AI systems, whether we are talking about training data or robot code, might just be the defining question of this decade. And what this week shows us is that there is no single answer. Governments are trying to legislate, companies are splitting on their models, researchers are pushing for openness. And all of it is happening at the same time, on every front simultaneously.

Emma: We are genuinely at an inflection point.

Liam: I think we really are. And the good news is there are still choices to be made. None of this is written in stone.
