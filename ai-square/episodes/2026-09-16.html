Full transcript

Maya: Welcome to AI Squared, your daily dose of everything you need to know about artificial intelligence. Today we are diving into a trend that should genuinely have all of us paying attention: AI agents are being deployed everywhere, at breakneck speed, but the guardrails meant to keep them in check are nowhere close to keeping up. And when a former researcher from both Anthropic and OpenAI decides to walk out the door very publicly, that is someone worth listening to. This is your episode for Wednesday, September 16, 2026.

Maya: Ryan, good to have you here. Let's kick things off with what is probably the most unsettling story of the day. A former researcher who worked at both Anthropic and OpenAI just went very public with some serious concerns. Walk us through what happened.

Ryan: Hey Maya, good to be here. Yeah, this one made a lot of noise today. Jacob Coxon, who spent time working on pretraining at both Anthropic and OpenAI between 2023 and 2026, announced his resignation in a piece published by Time magazine. And this was anything but a quiet exit. He is openly calling out what he describes as a race toward self-improving superintelligence being run with no real accountability whatsoever.

Maya: So when we say self-improving superintelligence, let's make sure our listeners know exactly what we are talking about.

Ryan: Absolutely. The basic idea is that at some point in its development, an AI system could become capable of improving itself, rewriting its own code, optimizing its own algorithms, all without any human involvement. The danger Coxon is flagging is that labs like Anthropic and OpenAI are locked in such an intense competitive race that they are charging toward that goal without having put in place the control mechanisms that would ensure a system like that stays aligned with human values.

Maya: And he talks about internal tensions. So people inside these companies are raising these concerns and just not being heard?

Ryan: That is exactly what he is saying. And honestly, that is probably the most troubling part of his account. This is not the first time we have seen high-profile departures from AI safety teams at these organizations. We have seen a number of very loud exits from OpenAI over the past few years. What Coxon is saying is that commercial pressure and competitive pressure consistently win out over safety concerns whenever the two come into conflict. And he reached a point where he did not want to be complicit in that anymore.

Maya: That is a pretty powerful statement. And his account lands on the same day as a study from EY that, in a way, validates those same concerns, but now at the level of companies deploying AI rather than building it.

Ryan: Right, and the parallel is really striking. EY published the results of a survey of 202 senior AI decision-makers in the United States, we are talking C-suite, VP level and above. And the finding is pretty stark: autonomous AI deployment is moving significantly faster than the governance frameworks that are supposed to manage it.

Maya: There was one number in that study that really jumped out at me.

Ryan: Yeah, the one on IT approval. In just one year, the share of organizations that require sign-off from their IT department before deploying an AI system dropped from 45 percent to 38 percent. So in six out of ten companies, you can roll out an autonomous AI agent with zero technical validation required. And that number is moving in the wrong direction.

Maya: So we are heading the wrong way on this.

Ryan: Clearly heading the wrong way. And what makes that especially concerning is that the AI agents we are talking about are not just chatbots answering questions anymore. These are systems that make decisions, execute actions, access sensitive data, sometimes interact with critical infrastructure. Deploying that kind of capability without oversight is a serious risk.

Maya: And speaking of AI agents, there was also a really interesting analysis published today that highlights a blind spot we probably have not thought enough about. Ryan, what is that about?

Ryan: This came from a piece in Eurasia Review, and it raises a question that sounds simple on the surface but is actually pretty thorny. We have gotten pretty good at figuring out how to stop AI agents. The EU, Singapore, several other jurisdictions now have frameworks that spell out how to deactivate an autonomous agent, how to trigger what is called a human override and take back control. But nobody has defined the rules for restarting them.

Maya: And why is that a problem?

Ryan: Imagine an AI agent gets suspended because it started behaving abnormally. You investigate, you think you have identified the issue, and you go to restart it. But on what basis? In what state? With what verification steps? Was the context it was operating in properly preserved when it was shut down? Are the data points it had in memory still valid? Is the fix you applied actually sufficient? No current framework answers those questions in any systematic way. That is what experts call full lifecycle governance, and right now we only have half of it.

Maya: That is a slightly unsettling analogy, but it is kind of like we invented the light switch without ever writing the rules for when it is actually safe to flip it back on.

Ryan: That is exactly it. And in high-stakes environments like finance, healthcare, or critical infrastructure, that gap could have very real consequences.

Maya: So is the industry actually responding to any of this? Because we did see several tools and frameworks launch today that seem to be pointing in that direction.

Ryan: Yeah, and it is interesting because you can clearly see an entire category of solutions emerging that are specifically focused on agent governance. The biggest structural move today is the public beta launch of the Agent Manager from WSO2. WSO2 is a company that specializes in enterprise integration platforms and has pretty solid roots in large organizations.

Maya: What does the Agent Manager actually do in practice?

Ryan: It is a centralized platform for managing the complete lifecycle of AI agents across an organization. It comes with more than 40 built-in automated guardrails, including PII masking to protect personal data, rate limiting to prevent an agent from consuming too many resources or making too many calls, and multi-level controls at the agent layer, the language model layer, and the MCP protocol layer, which is becoming a standard for communication between agents. And there is one feature I think is particularly relevant given everything we just talked about: the ability to suspend an agent with a single click inside a sandboxed environment.

Maya: So you can isolate it and shut it down fast if something goes sideways.

Ryan: Exactly. That is the partial answer to the problem we were just describing. The shutdown side is covered. The restart side, as I mentioned, still needs to be defined at the regulatory level.

Maya: Google also put something out today on the security side of agents that is worth talking about.

Ryan: Yeah, Google published a pretty dense post on their developer blog laying out what they call a zero-trust approach for AI agents. The core idea is that securing an agent cannot just mean checking whether a request is correctly formatted. You have to evaluate the intent behind each action.

Maya: How do you actually evaluate intent in practice?

Ryan: Google proposes three layers. The first is Model Armor, which functions like an inline firewall. It blocks prompt injection attacks, meaning attempts to manipulate an agent by hiding instructions inside data it processes, and it prevents sensitive data from leaking. The second layer is what they call Semantic Governance Policies, which is a policy engine built on a language model that evaluates every tool call an agent makes and checks whether it is actually consistent with what the user originally asked for. And the third is Agent Anomaly Detection, which monitors an agent's overall behavior over time to catch any drift.

Maya: That is a much more sophisticated approach than anything we were seeing even a year ago.

Ryan: It really is. And it shows how fast the AI security space is professionalizing. What was academic research eighteen months ago is now being shipped in actual products.

Maya: Let's shift to a big signal from the funding and research side, because there was a pretty significant raise announced today in a space we keep a close eye on: AI model auditing and certification.

Ryan: Yes, this is the startup AIUC, which stands for Artificial Intelligence Underwriting Company. They announced a 40 million dollar raise to expand their auditing work to frontier AI models. Up until now, AIUC had been focused on auditing agents built on top of those models. Now they want to go deeper and evaluate the underlying models themselves.

Maya: Who are the founders?

Ryan: John Furrier and Dave Vellante, two very well-known names in the American tech world, particularly for founding SiliconAngle and TheCUBE. These are people with real industry credibility and very strong networks. A 40 million dollar raise in this space is a strong signal that AI certification is becoming a legitimate industry in its own right.

Maya: Which makes total sense given everything we have covered today. If companies are rolling out agents without enough validation, someone needs to step in and play the role of independent auditor.

Ryan: Exactly. And there is actually a useful analogy with financial services here. Rating agencies like Moody's and Standard and Poor's were born out of the need for trusted third parties capable of evaluating complex products that buyers could not evaluate on their own. AIUC is trying to play that role for AI models. The difference is that certifying frontier AI models is even more complex because these systems are constantly evolving.

Maya: On the industry partnership side, there was also a notable announcement from Salesforce and Nvidia today.

Ryan: Right, they launched a model they are calling Koa. It is Salesforce's first reasoning model, co-developed with Nvidia on top of the open-weight Nemotron model. What is interesting here is the specialization: Koa is built specifically for sales, marketing, and customer support tasks. This is not a general-purpose model.

Maya: What is the strategy behind that move?

Ryan: The strategy is to stop being fully dependent on large general-purpose models like GPT or Claude for business applications. By building their own model, Salesforce keeps control over performance, over costs, and most importantly over data. And the Nvidia partnership is not incidental: it gives them access to top-tier training infrastructure. This is actually a very strong trend we are seeing across major enterprise software companies right now, building their own intelligence layer rather than just plugging in someone else's AI.

Maya: And what about Arm? Because they had announcements today too around their strategy for the agentic era.

Ryan: Arm, for anyone who needs a refresher, is the company behind the chip architectures powering essentially every smartphone on the planet. And they are actively positioning their technology as the foundational infrastructure for AI agents, all the way from edge devices out in the field to the cloud. They announced two new architectures, CSS for Mobile 2 and CSS N4, and they are talking about what they call physical AI, meaning AI agents embedded directly in hardware devices. The vision is to create a ubiquitous computing platform that agents can run on anywhere.

Maya: That is a pretty sweeping ambition.

Ryan: Very sweeping. And what is strategically interesting is that Arm does not make AI models, does not build agents, but by controlling the silicon that everything runs on, they potentially hold enormous structural leverage. It is a classic platform play: whoever owns the infrastructure has a structural advantage over everyone else in the ecosystem.

Maya: We should also quickly touch on the Crusoe and Perplexity partnership, which fits right into that same theme.

Ryan: Yeah, it is a multi-year deal built around vertically integrated infrastructure. Perplexity, which is one of the most widely used AI search engines right now, will be training its frontier models on Crusoe Cloud's GPU clusters, running on Nvidia's new GB300 chips. The appeal is covering the entire model lifecycle on a single platform: training, fine-tuning, and production inference all in one place. It is part of this broader trend toward consolidating AI infrastructure and reducing dependencies across multiple vendors.

Maya: Ryan, we are wrapping up here. If you had to pull out the through-line connecting everything we covered today, what is your big takeaway?

Ryan: I keep coming back to this fundamental tension that runs through every story we touched today. On one side, you have massive acceleration: AI agents being deployed inside companies, inside infrastructure, inside everyday devices. Models are getting more specialized, more powerful, more deeply integrated. And on the other side, you have governance running to catch up and not quite getting there. The EY study shows it at the company level. Jacob Coxon's testimony shows it inside the research labs themselves. And the analysis on agent restart rules shows it at the regulatory level. The good news is that awareness is building. Governance tools like the one from WSO2, Google's layered security approach, funding rounds going into certification like AIUC, all of that suggests the industry is starting to treat safety as a real priority. The question is whether that catch-up will happen fast enough.

Maya: And that is a question we do not have the answer to yet. What we do know is that we will keep tracking it for you every single day.

Maya: Alright, that is a wrap on today's episode of AI Squared. A huge thank you to Ryan for the sharp analysis as always. If you got something out of today's show, share it with someone who needs to hear it, drop us a comment, it genuinely helps us grow. And we will see you tomorrow for another look at what is happening in the world of artificial intelligence. See you tomorrow!
