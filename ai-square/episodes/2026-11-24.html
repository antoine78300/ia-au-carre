Maya: Welcome to AI Squared, your daily deep dive into everything artificial intelligence. Maya: Let's kick things off with the biggest story of the day. Ryan, Cisco and OpenAI just dropped something called Shirudo. What's the deal?

Ryan: So Shirudo actually means shield in Japanese, and honestly, the name nails it. It's an open-source framework that uses AI agents to detect and block critical vulnerability exploits in real time. The core idea is to fight AI-speed attacks with AI-speed defense.

Maya: And we're talking about real AI attacks here, not just theoretical scenarios?

Ryan: Absolutely. Since July 2026, we've been seeing autonomous attack frameworks in the wild, things with names like ARTEX and CyberStrikeAI, that can scan systems, find vulnerabilities, and exploit them with zero human involvement. Shirudo has actually been operationally tested against two specific flaws: Java4shell and certain PHP vulnerabilities. And the results are solid.

Maya: What really stands out to me is that it's Cisco, one of the biggest names in enterprise networking, teaming up with OpenAI on this. That's not an obvious pairing, right?

Ryan: It's very telling, actually. Cisco has this unique vantage point because they're seeing network traffic across thousands of Fortune 500 companies. When you pair that visibility with the reasoning capabilities of OpenAI's models, you get a defensive agent that can actually understand the context of an attack, not just match it against a known signature. That's what sets Shirudo apart from your traditional antivirus tools.

Maya: And the fact that it's open-source, does that actually matter?

Ryan: It matters a lot. It means the global security community can audit the code, contribute to it, adapt it to their own infrastructure. It's also a kind of political statement: we don't want agentic cyber defense locked up in the hands of a few private players. And that's especially important when you consider that the autonomous attacks it's defending against are already spreading freely.

Maya: Alright, staying with stories that affect big business, let's talk about what I'd call the Fortune 500 paradox. Ryan, major companies are going all in on agentic AI, but there's a catch.

Ryan: A pretty significant one. A new analysis from Emergent.sh found that 73 percent of Fortune 500 companies are running agentic systems today. That's a huge number. But only 12 percent have actually restructured their approval hierarchies to match the level of autonomy those agents operate at.

Maya: What does it even mean in practice to restructure an approval hierarchy?

Ryan: Picture an AI agent that needs to sign off on a supplier contract. In a traditional organization, that approval goes through a procurement manager, then a CFO, maybe a full committee. The agent can draft the contract in 30 seconds, but then it sits waiting on a human signature for three days because nobody redesigned the workflow. The efficiency gain just gets swallowed up by the org chart.

Maya: So the problem isn't the AI, it's people not willing to change how they work?

Ryan: It's more complicated than that. A lot of this comes down to governance and accountability. If the agent makes a bad call, who's on the hook? Pre-agent org structures were built to trace human responsibility. When an agent acts autonomously, that whole accountability framework breaks down, and companies don't have a replacement model yet.

Maya: And that connects directly to something happening on the regulatory front, because California just floated a pretty bold proposal on exactly this question.

Ryan: Right. Governor Newsom is proposing what you could call a state-level kill switch. The idea is to give California the authority to immediately shut down any AI model that poses a catastrophic risk, with developers having the option to pursue legal recourse afterward.

Maya: Afterward. So you pull the plug first and ask questions later.

Ryan: That's the principle. And this is unprecedented at this level of specificity in any US state. We saw similar frameworks come out of Singapore and Malaysia between January and November 2026, but nothing like this in the United States. And given that Silicon Valley sits in California, this proposal would hit the world's biggest AI labs directly.

Maya: I have a feeling the reception in San Francisco is going to be, let's say, mixed.

Ryan: The debate is going to be fierce, no question. But what's really interesting is that this proposal is surfacing at exactly the moment when frameworks like Shirudo are demonstrating that AI can attack and defend autonomously. The question of human control is no longer abstract. It's very, very real.

Maya: Let's get into tools and frameworks now, because there are two big announcements today. Ryan, let's start with what Microsoft just did with USB4. Yeah, you heard that right, USB4.

Ryan: I know, it sounds like a hardware press release, but stick with me because the implications run deep. Microsoft announced in developer preview a feature called RDMA over USB4. RDMA stands for Remote Direct Memory Access. It's a technology that lets one computer directly access another computer's memory without going through the main processor.

Maya: Okay, and what can you actually do with that?

Ryan: You can connect two or more Windows PCs with a USB4 or Thunderbolt 4 cable, pool their GPU memory and compute power, and run AI models together that would be way too large for any single machine. The numbers they put out are pretty impressive: GPT-OSS-120B, a 120-billion-parameter model, hits 49 tokens per second across three Intel Core Ultra PCs with 64 gigabytes each. And Qwen3-235B, a 235-billion-parameter model, runs at 18.5 tokens per second across three AMD mini-PCs.

Maya: So you no longer need a supercomputer to run a large model?

Ryan: That's exactly the paradigm shift. Up until now, if you wanted to do distributed inference, you either needed cloud infrastructure or specialized hardware with a complex network setup. Now we're talking about linking desktop PCs with a cable. This is what decentralized edge computing looks like in practice, and it puts serious local AI within reach for small businesses, research labs, independent developers.

Maya: The other tool story today is a comparison between two AI agent evaluation frameworks: Promptfoo and DeepEval. Why is evaluating an AI agent fundamentally different from evaluating a traditional model?

Ryan: Great question, and it really gets at something important. When you were evaluating a traditional LLM, you'd prompt it and measure the quality of its responses, things like perplexity scores or performance on fixed benchmarks. But an agent doesn't just respond. It makes decisions, calls external tools, hands off subtasks to other agents. The failure point could be anywhere along that chain.

Maya: So how does DeepEval address that?

Ryan: The key distinction highlighted in this comparative breakdown is that DeepEval evaluates what the agent actually does in production, not what the underlying model claims it can do. You're evaluating the full trajectory, the tool calls, the handoffs between agents. It's an approach that's much closer to how these systems actually behave in the real world.

Maya: Now let's get into research. Ryan, Georgia Tech just published work on a problem we all know: LLMs have no idea what happened after their training cutoff date.

Ryan: It's a fundamental issue, and the researchers at Georgia Tech, Alan Ritter and his team, wanted to quantify it rigorously rather than just describe it qualitatively. They built a benchmark that tests how well LLMs handle post-cutoff events by using a web search function.

Maya: And what model did they test this on?

Ryan: They used GPT-6 Astra, which has a training cutoff of September 3rd, 2026. That means the model has zero knowledge of the November 3rd US elections. The benchmark feeds it probabilistic scenarios: predictions about the 2034 World Cup, the 2032 British elections. And the core question is: does the model correctly recognize when it should rely on a web search rather than its own internal knowledge?

Maya: And what did they find?

Ryan: The benchmark lets you precisely quantify the bottleneck between the training window and dependence on real-time search. That matters a lot in agentic systems, because an agent that confuses its frozen knowledge with current facts can make seriously bad decisions. This work from Georgia Tech gives us a tool to measure that risk and start mitigating it.

Maya: And speaking of academic recognition, there's something significant happening on that front too, right?

Ryan: There is, and it's a big deal. The Journal of Management Information Systems, one of the most influential academic journals in information systems, is launching a special issue titled Agentic Organizations. The timeline is concrete: extended abstracts due by December 15th, 2026, full papers by February 1st, 2027.

Maya: What does it mean when a journal of that caliber dedicates an entire issue to this topic?

Ryan: It means the field has hit critical mass. Up to now, most research was documenting industry adoption in a qualitative way. This special issue formalizes something important: AI that takes actions with bounded autonomy, in other words, agents, requires a complete rethinking of traditional information systems theory. It's academic recognition that this isn't just an evolution of existing AI. It's a change in kind.

Maya: Alright, let's close out with industry impact. Ryan, two stories worth your attention. First up, something out of China: a model called GLM-5.4.

Ryan: GLM-5.4 is developed by ZHIPU AI, deployed on November 23rd. What makes it stand out is the architecture: the Loop Transformer. Instead of passing information through successive layers just once the way a classic transformer does, the Loop Transformer cycles back through existing layers to enable iterative reasoning.

Maya: And what does that deliver in terms of performance?

Ryan: ZHIPU AI is claiming intelligence equivalent to models in the two-to-three-trillion-parameter range. And the coalition is serious: MIT Media Lab on the research side, Mozilla for open web standards, the ITU-T for international telecom norms. This isn't just a press release. It's a genuine attempt to define the protocols for a web where an agent is a first-class citizen, right alongside humans.
