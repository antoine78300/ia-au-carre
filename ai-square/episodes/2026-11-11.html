Maya: Welcome back to AI Squared, your daily deep-dive into everything artificial intelligence. And yes, Ryan and I are back hosting the show starting today, and we are so glad to be here with you again. Now, buckle up, because we have got a packed episode: a model that can coordinate a thousand agents at the same time, a major IBM and Microsoft alliance bringing serious firepower to the enterprise, and an entire infrastructure layer being built around AI agents at breakneck speed. Let's get into it.

Maya: Ryan, let's kick things off with the biggest story of the day, and honestly it kind of picks itself. Anthropic just dropped two new models, Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2. Walk us through it.

Ryan: Yeah, this one is absolutely worth slowing down for. Anthropic is launching Fable 5.2 today, and the thing that jumps out immediately is a capability we really have not seen at this scale. This model can coordinate more than a thousand agents simultaneously. We are not talking about a chatbot helping you draft an email. We are talking about an orchestrator that can run an entire army of AI agents in parallel, all at once.

Maya: A thousand agents at the same time. That is genuinely hard to wrap your head around. What does that actually look like in practice?

Ryan: Okay, picture a complex software engineering project. Right now you have a team of developers splitting up the work. With Fable 5.2, you could theoretically spin up hundreds of agents running in parallel, each one handling a different chunk of the codebase, while others are running tests, others are doing security checks, and Fable is orchestrating all of it in real time. And Anthropic is not being vague about the target use cases either. They explicitly call out advanced software engineering tasks and robotics control as the core applications here.

Maya: And then there is Mythos 5.2, which is a different beast entirely?

Ryan: Right, Mythos 5.2 is in restricted access with a verification process. It relaxes certain guardrails specifically for fields like cybersecurity and life sciences. It is designed for professionals who need to push further in sensitive domains, within a controlled access framework. On the pricing side, Fable 5.2 drops from ten to eight dollars per million input tokens, so a twenty percent reduction, and it comes with a thirty percent speed boost. And if you need even faster throughput, there is a turbo option that is twice as fast at twice the price.

Maya: So faster, cheaper, and dramatically more capable for agentic workloads. This is clearly a direct play against the wave of specialized agent models we are seeing pop up everywhere right now.

Ryan: Exactly. Anthropic is positioning Fable as a head-on alternative to those specialized models. And the timing is not accidental. With Anthropic's IPO expected in the coming months, this launch is sending a very deliberate signal to the market.

Maya: Let's stay in the world of frameworks and tooling, because there is a lot happening today on the infrastructure side, the stuff that actually makes all these agents run. Ryan, where do we start?

Ryan: We start with Red Hat, who was at KubeCon this week. They are presenting what they describe as a complete open-source stack for running AI agents in a cloud-native environment. Concretely, it comes down to three pieces that fit together: OpenShift, which is their Kubernetes foundation, OpenShell for agent execution with a security layer co-developed with NVIDIA, and llm-d 1.0, which launched on November fifth and handles inference distribution natively inside Kubernetes.

Maya: Why does the open-source piece matter so much here?

Ryan: Because it completely changes the equation for enterprises. A proprietary stack locks you into one vendor. An open-source stack built on Kubernetes, which has become the industry standard for container orchestration, gives you flexibility, portability, and a whole community contributing to it. What Red Hat is demonstrating here is that you can run agents in production, at scale, with serious governance, without being trapped inside a closed ecosystem.

Maya: And GoodData.AI also made an announcement today that is in a similar vein?

Ryan: Yeah, and this one is interesting because GoodData is tackling a very specific problem: how do you run AI agents on data at high concurrency, meaning when a lot of agents are hitting your data simultaneously, with different tenants who absolutely cannot see each other's information. They call it an Agentic Serving Plane, essentially a governed execution layer. The idea is to isolate the context and permissions for each agent and each tenant. And Gartner just named them a Visionary in their 2026 Magic Quadrant for analytics, which signals that this new infrastructure category is being taken seriously at the analyst level.

Maya: So you have got Red Hat handling how agents run in the cloud, and GoodData handling how they access data securely.

Ryan: Exactly. And that illustrates something really important. Agentic infrastructure is not a single product. It is an ecosystem of specialized layers being built out right now, in real time.

Maya: Let's talk tools now, because there are two stories today that get at very real, very concrete problems in agent deployment. First up is a tool designed to defend against AI-assisted reverse engineering. Ryan, explain that one for us.

Ryan: Here is a problem that most people did not see coming. AI agents have gotten so good at analyzing code that someone can hand them a compiled binary, a file with no readable source code, and within minutes get a detailed picture of exactly what that software does. That is reverse engineering. And it creates an obvious problem for intellectual property protection and software security.

Maya: So what is Blind LLM Agents, and how does it push back on that?

Ryan: It is an open-source tool built by an independent developer specifically to make life harder for AI agents trying to analyze code. It sits within a growing defensive ecosystem alongside Cisco's Project Quarry. What is really notable here is that this is the first time we are seeing defensive tools built specifically against the capabilities of AI agents, not just against human reverse engineers or traditional decompilation tools. That is a meaningful shift.

Maya: And there is also a roundup published today on observability tools for agents, which touches on a similar security angle?

Ryan: Yes, and this one really caught my attention. The report covers ten major agent observability and evaluation tools: Promptfoo, LangSmith, Braintrust, Langfuse, Arize Phoenix, Datadog, DeepEval, Weave, Galileo, and AgentOps. What is shifting is the philosophy behind these tools. They are no longer just about measuring performance. They now bake in adversarial security testing, meaning you are actively checking whether an agent can be manipulated through prompt injection, whether its tool access permissions are properly scoped, and whether it can leak data. And critically, these tests happen at every stage of development, not just after you ship.

Maya: That is actually a pretty fundamental mindset shift. You are no longer extending default trust to the agent.

Ryan: Exactly. We are industrializing skepticism, if you want to put it that way. And honestly, that is a really healthy thing for the reliability of production systems.

Maya: Alright, let's get to the notable research of the day. And this one comes from a company you might not immediately associate with AI: Texas Instruments.

Ryan: Yeah, this is one of those announcements that shows just how deep agentic AI is reaching into highly specialized, highly technical domains. Texas Instruments unveiled major upgrades to their CCStudio development environment at electronica 2026. CCStudio is the tool embedded engineers use to program microcontrollers. They have integrated an autonomous agentic AI that has deep, specific knowledge of Texas Instruments devices: microcontrollers, connectivity chips, radar sensors.

Maya: What does that actually mean for an embedded engineer on the ground?

Ryan: They are claiming that tasks that used to take months can now take days. Now, yes, that is classic marketing language, but it is pointing at something real. Configuring a microcontroller, debugging firmware, navigating dense technical documentation, that is highly specialized work that requires deep expertise. Having an agent that knows the entire Texas Instruments catalog and can walk an engineer through every step is a genuinely significant accelerator.

Maya: And this is the opposite of a general-purpose assistant. This is very domain-specific.

Ryan: That is exactly what makes it interesting. We are watching agentic AI go vertical, specializing by industry and by domain, with embedded knowledge of very precise hardware. That is so much more useful than a general model with a vague awareness of electronics.

Maya: Time for the industry impact segment, and today the list is long. Let's start with the biggest alliance of the day: IBM and Microsoft launching something called Imagine Enterprise.

Ryan: This one carries real weight. IBM and Microsoft are combining six thousand pre-built agents from the IBM Consulting catalog with Microsoft's cloud infrastructure. The goal is a unified agentic platform organized by industry vertical, with joint support from both companies delivered directly to enterprise customers.

Maya: Six thousand pre-built agents. That is a staggering catalog. But it does raise the question: does any company actually need six thousand agents?

Ryan: Fair point, and the real logic here is verticalization. Every industry, every business process has its own nuances. A claims management agent for an insurance company looks nothing like a logistics planning agent for a manufacturer. By offering six thousand pre-built agents, IBM and Microsoft are trying to cut deployment time for each specific use case. It is a platform play through and through.

Maya: Google Cloud is also making a move in healthcare today.

Ryan: Gemini for Healthcare is a purpose-built agentic solution for clinical, operational, and administrative systems. What stands out in this announcement is native governance: every agent gets a distinct identity with granular access controls. Blue Cross Blue Shield of Massachusetts is cited as a validated early adopter, which gives the solution real credibility.

Maya: Healthcare is a sector where data governance and security are completely non-negotiable.

Ryan: One hundred percent. And that is exactly why Google is leading with per-agent identity and fine-grained access control. In a hospital setting, there is absolutely no scenario where an administrative agent should be touching detailed clinical records. Governance is not a nice-to-have, it is table stakes.

Maya: Adobe and Google also deepened their partnership today, on a very different front: creative work.

Ryan: Yeah, Gemini is being integrated directly into Adobe Creative Suite and Acrobat, with connections into Google Workspace, Drive, and Gmail. What is significant here is that this marks the arrival of agentic orchestration inside mainstream creative workflows. Up to now the conversation around agents has mostly been about productivity and data. Now we are talking about Photoshop, Illustrator, PDFs.

In an industry where rivalries run deep, seeing Meta deploy on Google's infrastructure rather than keeping everything on their own servers tells you that the priority is reaching developers where they already are.
