Maya: Forty-three compromised tools, an AI model that uncovered nearly a thousand Linux vulnerabilities, and US banks finally getting a regulatory framework for their autonomous agents. Welcome to AI Squared, your daily pulse on everything artificial intelligence. Maya: Root access, meaning the highest level of privileges you can have on a computer system. Practically speaking, if you have root access on a machine, you own it — you can do anything.

Ryan: Exactly. And what's especially striking here is that these vulnerabilities go beyond anything previously documented. This isn't a tool that's replaying known attacks from some database. This is an agent that reasons, that explores, that finds pathways human researchers hadn't identified yet.

Ryan: One point two million dollars in API infrastructure credits. That gives you a real sense of just how computationally intense this kind of automated cybersecurity pipeline actually is. It's a massive investment, but the output — 948 critical vulnerabilities — starts to paint a picture of the potential cost-effectiveness if this capability gets industrialized.

Maya: And that's where things get a little dizzying. Because if an AI agent can find these vulnerabilities, you have to assume that other actors — ones with less honorable intentions — are working on similar capabilities.

Ryan: That's precisely why this demonstration cuts both ways. On one hand, it validates the value of AI agents in offensive cyber defense — finding vulnerabilities before the attackers do. On the other hand, it's a pretty clear illustration of the exact kind of threat that bad actors could look to deploy. And this lands at the same time as another really troubling story, which we'll get to in just a moment.

Ryan: Absolutely. But honestly, what's even more interesting than the fundraise is what Together AI is announcing alongside it: Together Compiler. This is a tool that uses teams of AI agents to automatically rewrite the GPU code of AI models.

Ryan: Exactly. This is what's known as recursive self-improvement. And it's not theoretical anymore — it's running in production. It's an open-source inference platform that tackles a very real-world problem: when you're running AI agents in production, demand spikes are unpredictable. One second there's nothing, and the next you've got thousands of requests hitting at the same time. llm-d handles those spikes while keeping latency stable, whether you're on cloud infrastructure or on-premise servers.

Maya: And it brings costs down?

Ryan: That's the core goal, yes. By optimizing inference management, you avoid over-provisioning expensive resources just to handle peak loads. For companies running AI agents at scale, that's potentially a very significant economic win.

Maya: We also have STMicroelectronics coming in with something that feels a little unexpected in this landscape.

Ryan: Yeah, and it's an important signal. STM is launching STM32Cube for Agentic AI, a tool built directly into its development stack for STM32 microcontrollers. The idea is to give AI-assisted coding tools the right context and directives to generate accurate, optimized code for these extremely resource-constrained microcontrollers.

Maya: Why does that matter?

Ryan: Because it validates agentic AI adoption beyond servers and the cloud. Microcontrollers are the heart of embedded edge computing — connected devices, industrial systems, automotive. Maya: Let's shift to the research side now. And this is where we need to come back to that story you flagged earlier. The 43 compromised tools.

Ryan: Right, and this is a serious alert. Researchers discovered that 43 AI agent tools contain backdoors — hidden entry points — introduced through the software supply chain.

Maya: The software supply chain, for those less familiar, is the whole ecosystem of libraries, dependencies, and third-party components that any piece of software relies on. When one of those components is compromised, everything built on top of it potentially is too.

Ryan: Exactly. And what makes this discovery especially alarming is the scope. Forty-three tools — that's not an isolated incident. That's a systemic compromise of a portion of the AI agent ecosystem. It's also worth noting that Anthropic confirmed the compromised version that referenced them was not distributed through their official channels — which is good news for their direct users, but it underscores that the problem is coming from reused third-party components.

Maya: And this echoes a similar incident from late October involving LiteLLM, right?

Ryan: Yes, that October 26th incident had already raised flags about supply chain risks in the AI ecosystem. These new findings put a real number on the problem: 43 tools impacted. That's a figure that should push every team deploying AI agents to urgently audit their dependencies.

Maya: And in that context, the announcement from JFrog makes a lot of sense.

Ryan: Completely. And CrowdStrike is moving in the same direction with new tools unveiled at Fal.Con Europe.

Ryan: They're launching threat detection tools built specifically with AI agents in mind. All of this is integrated into their Falcon platform. Additional capabilities are planned for later this year and early 2028. What we're seeing take shape is an entire cybersecurity industry that's actively repositioning itself around the specific risks posed by autonomous agents.

Banks that are deploying AI agents in their operations — loan processing, fraud detection, customer service — now know they'll be audited against specific criteria.
