Maya: So they're being deliberate about not grabbing the scientific authorship, but they are confirming that their models did reasoning work that a human couldn't have pulled off alone in any reasonable amount of time.

Ryan: Exactly. And that shift is what's historic here. We're moving from AI that helps verify proofs to AI that actually generates new ones. They call it an operational validation of agentic AI as a producer of mathematical theorems.

Maya: And what was the malware actually doing?

Maya: And this could have been prevented?

Ryan: Completely. The post-mortem shows that LiteLLM had a master key sitting exposed for months before the attack, and they weren't able to revoke it in time. This is what's called a supply chain risk. When you're using a third-party library to connect your AI agents to models, that library becomes a critical trust point. If it's compromised, everything flowing through it is compromised too.

Maya: A harsh reminder that software supply chain security is a massive deal for AI systems running in production.

Maya: Let's shift to something more technical but really fascinating, coming out of Tsinghua University.

Ryan: Right, researchers at Tsinghua published and open-sourced a tool called AKV. It's a KV cache optimization server for LLM-based agents. Let me try to make this approachable.

Maya: Please do, because KV cache is not exactly intuitive.

Ryan: So when an AI agent is handling a long conversation, it needs to remember everything that was said earlier. To avoid recomputing all of that from scratch on every step, LLMs use a cache, called a KV cache for key-value. The problem is that in environments with lots of simultaneous requests, managing that cache gets expensive and slow. What AKV does is let agents reposition that cache dynamically, instead of rewriting the entire history every time.

Maya: And the results are impressive.

Ryan: Really impressive. The researchers report a 27.7% cost reduction, and a dramatic latency improvement. Under high concurrency, with 16 simultaneous requests, 95th percentile latency drops from 229 seconds to just over 30 seconds. That's roughly seven times faster.

Maya: Seven times is huge for anything running in production. What models does it support?

Ryan: It uses SGLang as the inference engine, and it's compatible with models like Qwen, open-source GPT variants, and MiniMax-M2. All of it is available open source.
