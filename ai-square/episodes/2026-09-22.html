On the program: autonomous medical AI in healthcare, AI agents regulation and safety, FDA authorization for clinical AI systems.

Full transcript

Maya: Welcome to AI Squared, your daily breakdown of everything happening in the world of artificial intelligence. Today, AI agents are everywhere — in your hospitals, your banks, your cybersecurity systems. And the question is no longer whether they're going to change our lives. The question is whether we can get a handle on them before they get ahead of us. That's what we're diving into today.

Maya: Ryan, we're jumping straight into the deep end. There's a massive new investment just announced for medical AI in the US. Walk us through it.

Ryan: Yeah, this is a really significant one. ARPA-H, the federal agency that's basically the DARPA equivalent for healthcare, just launched a program called ADVOCATE. We're talking a 62.7 million dollar commitment over four years. The goal is to develop the first fully autonomous clinical AI that gets official FDA authorization to manage heart failure directly with patients.

Maya: Hold on. An AI that talks directly to cardiac patients, with no doctor in the loop?

Ryan: That's exactly what we're talking about. And here's where it gets really interesting. This program isn't just building a medical tool. It's going to establish the very first official FDA standard for authorizing AI agents in a clinical setting. So for the first time, regulators will be defining the precise criteria an agentic AI has to meet to be considered safe in a medical context.

Maya: So it's almost like a regulatory sandbox as much as a medical one?

Ryan: Exactly. And the ripple effects for the whole industry are enormous. Right now, regulatory ambiguity is one of the biggest blockers to deploying AI agents in healthcare. Companies working in medical AI are watching this very closely, because what the FDA validates in the US has a way of becoming the global benchmark. If the ADVOCATE standard takes hold, it's going to shape what gets approved in Europe too.

Maya: But let's talk about the actual medicine here. Can an AI really manage heart failure on its own? That's a serious condition.

Ryan: It is, but it's also a chronic condition that requires daily monitoring — tracking weight, blood pressure, adjusting medications. These are repetitive, protocol-driven tasks where a well-trained AI can genuinely add value, especially in areas where cardiologists are hard to come by. That said, the liability question is still wide open. If the AI gives bad advice, who's responsible? That's precisely what ADVOCATE is going to have to figure out.

Maya: Yeah, that's a question we're going to be wrestling with for a long time. Alright, let's shift to tools and frameworks. Ryan, what's catching your eye?

Ryan: A few things. First, AWS just made Strands Harness generally available. It's an open-source agent framework that launched back in September 2026, and what sets it apart is its flexibility. It can do web research, execute commands, maintain persistent memory, and hand off subtasks — and it doesn't lock you into any specific AI model. You can run it with Amazon Bedrock models, but also with Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, or even a locally hosted model through Ollama.

Maya: So it's kind of like a Swiss Army knife for deploying agents in production?

Ryan: That's a great way to put it. And governance is baked in from the start, which is critical. Because on that note, there's also a new study from Dresner Advisory that just confirmed something people in this industry have been sensing for a while now: in 2026, generative AI and agentic AI are completely inseparable in real production environments. You just can't treat them as two different things anymore.

Maya: And that creates management headaches?

Ryan: Major ones. The study highlights a critical need for what's called ModelOps — basically all the practices and tooling required to govern dozens, sometimes hundreds of AI agents running in parallel inside a company. Knowing who's doing what, keeping behavior in check, catching things when they go sideways. It's as much an organizational challenge as a technical one.

Maya: And there's also a new tool in the mix called Jev. What's the deal with that?

Ryan: So Jev is a startup founded by one of the original people behind ChatGPT. The concept is pretty elegant. Instead of using a large language model to decide which tool to call or what action to take within an agent pipeline, they built a highly specialized transformer model just for routing and monitoring decisions. It responds in under 500 milliseconds and costs roughly 26 times less than current alternatives.

Maya: That sounds almost too good. I'm guessing there's a catch?

Ryan: There's always a catch. VentureBeat documented a serious vulnerability: Jev is susceptible to prompt injection attacks. That means malicious data injected into an agent's environment can manipulate its routing decisions, even when a human is supposed to be validating the actions. And this isn't theoretical — real-world cases have been documented, including cybercriminal groups exploiting this vulnerability to automate attacks at scale.

Maya: We're going to dig into the security angle in a bit. But first, the New York AI law is also in the news today, right?

Ryan: That's right. Governor Hochul just announced that the first requirements of the RAISE Act are going into effect — that stands for Responsible AI Safety and Education Act. Starting November 2026, AI models serving users in New York will have to meet new safety and transparency obligations. It's the first state-level standard of this scope in the US. And because New York is the financial capital of the world, this is going to have a serious pull effect on European companies operating there.

Maya: Alright, let's get into the research side of things, and Ryan, this next part is genuinely alarming.

Ryan: Yeah, I'd call this the most serious security alert of the day. Security Affairs just published an analysis showing that attack reconnaissance — the phase where a threat actor identifies targets, maps out vulnerabilities, and gathers intelligence — is now being executed by AI agents, automatically, and at massive scale.

Maya: So you're talking about AI agents doing the prep work for hackers?

Ryan: Exactly. And two concrete cases have been documented. The first is called EchoLeak, tracked under CVE-2025-32711. This was a vulnerability that allowed an AI agent to exfiltrate data from Microsoft Copilot, the AI assistant built into Microsoft's suite of tools. The second is called CurXecute, which has two CVEs associated with it, and it enabled remote code execution in development environments using the MCP protocol.

Maya: And what's new here is the speed at which these vulnerabilities are being exploited?

Ryan: That's the key point. Traditionally, there was a fairly long lag between academic research describing a vulnerability and that vulnerability being exploited in the wild. That window has shrunk to a matter of weeks. AI agents are automatically reading research publications, identifying exploitable flaws, and turning them into attack tools. The line between theoretical research and operational weapon is essentially disappearing.

Maya: And what does NVIDIA have to do with all of this? You mentioned something about agent evaluation.

Ryan: So NVIDIA is releasing Nemotron 3.5 Lightning with benchmarks specifically designed to measure agent performance across complete end-to-end scenarios, not just isolated tool calls. They're working with banking use cases — things like customer refund workflows — and validating results with human reviewers. This is actually a big deal, because right now, assessing whether an agent is genuinely reliable in production is an unsolved problem. We know how to measure whether an AI answers a question correctly. We're a lot worse at measuring whether an agent successfully completes a complex multi-step mission over several hours.

Maya: Now let's talk about enterprise impact. And the numbers here on agent security are pretty jaw-dropping.

Ryan: A survey from a company called Gravitee published this year paints a pretty alarming picture. 85 percent of organizations deploying AI agents have no one formally in charge of security for those agents. And only 7.2 percent have named a specific individual with a clear role to manage these risks.

Maya: 85 percent with nobody responsible. That's staggering. How is that possible when these agents are out there making real decisions?

Ryan: It's the reality on the ground. AI agents tend to come in through the side door in a lot of companies. One team adopts it to save time, it spreads, and governance never catches up. The survey identifies five critical risks that need to be addressed before any deployment. First, identity: every agent needs a unique identity scoped specifically to its purpose. Second, credentials, meaning access keys, which need to be temporary rather than permanent. Third, permission inventory: actually knowing what each agent has access to, which is rarely done. Fourth, the tool supply chain: an agent uses third-party tools, and each one is a potential attack vector. And fifth, that designated owner — the specific named person who is missing in 93 percent of cases.

Maya: That list sounds a lot like what we were saying about classic cybersecurity twenty years ago.

Ryan: The parallel is spot on. And just like with traditional cybersecurity, we may end up learning the hard way. There's also Hamming AI, which is doing something interesting specifically for banks — red-teaming dedicated to voice agents. They essentially simulate attacks, manipulation attempts, and stress scenarios to pressure-test a voice agent's robustness before it goes live in a banking context.

Maya: What about movement on the model side of things?

Ryan: Two notable updates. Moonshot AI, a Chinese startup, is deploying its Kimi K3 model on Amazon Bedrock. This is a strategically significant move — it's basically a test of whether a Chinese open-weight model can monetize through Western cloud platforms. What we see play out over the next few months will tell us a lot about the geopolitics of AI.

Maya: And what about Grok?

Ryan: xAI is launching Grok 4.7, available inside the Cursor development environment and through the API. Pricing comes in at two dollars per million input tokens and six dollars per million output tokens, with a faster variant that's twice as quick for twice the price. Nothing earth-shattering, but it confirms xAI's strategy of embedding directly into developer tools to capture that audience.

Maya: And before we wrap up, there's an initiative I really want to make sure we give time to. A global coalition around minority languages.

Ryan: Yes, and this one is meaningful at a different scale. Sixty organizations — including frontier research labs, governments, and the Gates Foundation — have committed to a five-year goal: making AI tools genuinely useful for the 3.4 billion people who speak languages that are essentially invisible in today's models. We're talking about languages from sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia, Indigenous communities — languages with very little training data to work from.

Maya: This is a fundamental equity issue. If AI only works for populations that are already connected and English-speaking, it deepens inequality instead of reducing it.

Ryan: Absolutely. And there are very concrete practical implications too. Voice agents being deployed in healthcare, education, and financial services have zero value if the person on the other end can't communicate in their own language. This coalition is an encouraging signal, even if five years is a long runway and the targets are ambitious.

Maya: Ryan, as we close out today's episode, if you had to pull one common thread from everything we covered, what would it be?

Ryan: AI agents are not on the horizon anymore. They are in production, right now, in hospitals, in banks, in offensive cyber operations. And the central question of 2026 is a simple one: are we building governance at the same pace as capability? Honestly, the answer right now is no. 85 percent of companies with no designated owner, exploitation timelines shrinking to weeks, medical agents operating without a fully defined regulatory framework. We are in a race. And initiatives like ADVOCATE, like New York's RAISE Act, like the minority language coalition, show that awareness is growing. But we need to move faster.

Maya: Ryan, thank you as always for breaking all of this down. And thank you to everyone listening to AI Squared today. If this episode gave you something to think about, share it with someone who needs to hear it — understanding AI shouldn't be reserved for insiders. We will see you tomorrow for another deep dive into what's happening in artificial intelligence. See you then.
