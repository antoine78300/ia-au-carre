Full transcript

Maya: Hey everyone, welcome to AI Squared, your daily deep dive into everything artificial intelligence. Today we have got a packed show: massive models at rock-bottom prices, AI agents going after the secret keys inside your favorite tools, and researchers now embedded inside OpenAI to keep a close eye on the models themselves. Let's get into it.

Maya: Ryan, let's kick things off with the biggest story of the day. And honestly, it feels like the through line today is AI agent security.

Ryan: Absolutely, Maya, and this one really deserves our full attention. Amazon has documented a wave of attacks carried out by autonomous AI agents scanning popular tools like LiteLLM, Dify, Langflow, FastGPT, RAGFlow, and Open WebUI. The goal of these malicious agents is to extract API keys stored inside those systems. We are talking hundreds of identified targets.

Maya: Hold on, you are saying AI agents are attacking other AI systems?

Ryan: Exactly. And what makes this especially alarming is that Google, on their end, has put out formal documentation on entire offensive frameworks built specifically for automated multi-step attacks. We are talking systems like ARTEX, CAIRN, CyberStrikeAI, PentAGI, and HexStrike AI. That last one runs more than a hundred and fifty tools managed by twelve specialized agents all working in coordination.

Maya: That is wild. And are these frameworks actually out there for people to access?

Ryan: That is where it gets really unsettling. Even after the original GitHub repository was taken down in October, these tools are still accessible. And Google documents that between August and October, one attacker used a modified version of one of these frameworks to systematically target organizations across Asia. We have moved from isolated attacks to what is essentially industrialized offensive orchestration.

Maya: So agentic AI, the thing everyone keeps pitching as the future of productivity, is also becoming the future of cybercrime.

Ryan: That is the flip side. The same power that lets you automate complex tasks can be weaponized to automate exploits at a speed and scale that traditional human defenses just cannot keep up with. And the detail that really hits home is that attackers are also going after LLM proxies like One API, New API, and Veloera. Anything that exposes keys in plain text is a potential target.

Maya: Loud and clear. Alright, let's shift gears and talk tools and frameworks, because there is a lot happening on that front too. Ryan, where do we start?

Ryan: Let's start with an announcement that is making real waves in the agent deployment space. Withcoral has launched Coral, an open-source gateway that runs agent tool calls inside isolated environments called V8 isolates, with built-in policy verification. And the numbers they are putting up are genuinely impressive.

Maya: Like what kind of numbers?

Ryan: Across thirty-four analyzed tasks, custom tools managed through Coral cut token consumption by thirty-seven percent, costs by thirty-one percent, and execution time by twenty-nine percent. And maybe the most significant thing: smaller models are able to perform like much larger ones. Over four hundred and fifty companies are already using it.

Maya: So you are cutting the bill and you can skip the big expensive models entirely.

Ryan: Exactly. And this fits right into a bigger trend of making AI agents leaner and more controllable. Along those same lines, Genpact, which is a major business services player listed on the New York Stock Exchange, just announced Genpact Onyx. It is a full platform for building, running, and governing AI agents inside enterprise operations.

Maya: What sets Onyx apart from just another open-source framework?

Ryan: It comes down to what they are calling the Process Intelligence Core. The idea is to take decades of operational knowledge that Genpact has built up working with large enterprises and bake it directly into the agents. This is not a generic tool. It is a platform that shows up with real domain expertise already inside it. This is the first time a company of this size in business process services has put out a unified, proprietary agentic solution at this level.

Maya: And on the more academic tools side, there is also something around student project management, right?

Ryan: Yes, more modest in scope but interesting from an architectural standpoint. It is called vProjTutor. It is a system of specialized LLM agents that plugs directly into Telegram, Trello, and GitHub. Students can use plain natural language to set up projects, generate sprint plans, organize tasks, and track their progress. It is a solid real-world demonstration that multi-platform agentic orchestration works in very practical everyday contexts.

Maya: Let's move to research now. And OpenAI is front and center here, with not one but two collaborations with external research teams.

Ryan: This is a pretty significant move from OpenAI. First, with Apollo Research, OpenAI has launched a pilot program where researchers are embedded directly inside the company with access to some very sensitive stuff: training code, reinforcement learning rollouts, intermediate model checkpoints.

Maya: And what is the core question they are trying to answer?

Ryan: Whether models are attempting to subvert their own alignment mechanisms, or those of future versions. Basically, can an AI model try to work around the guardrails it has been given? It might sound like a theoretical question, but it gets very real very fast as these models become more capable.

Maya: And there is a second parallel collaboration running alongside that one?

Ryan: Yes, with Transluce AI, formerly affiliated with the Effective Altruism Institute. The access there is different again: raw model chain-of-thought outputs, internal alignment evaluations, and even transcripts from prior training runs. This pilot kicks off in December for six weeks. The focus is on investigating transparency in model behavior.

Maya: Two external teams, two different angles. Is this OpenAI signaling that it wants to be seen as more accountable?

Ryan: It is at least a structured, formalized opening up, which is new compared to keeping everything purely internal. We will have to wait and see what these teams actually publish. But just the act of granting this level of access to independent third parties is a real shift in posture, and it is worth noting.

Maya: Alright, let's wrap up with the industry impact segment, and there are some major model announcements here. Tencent and Alibaba both dropped new models almost at the same time.

Ryan: They did, and it is a pretty striking illustration of just how hard Asian players are competing in the race for cost-efficient models. Tencent announced Hy4 Flash on November 16th. It is a model with two hundred and twenty-four billion total parameters, but only twelve billion are active at any time thanks to a hybrid Mixture of Experts architecture. One million token context window. And the pricing: seven cents per million input tokens.

Maya: That is cheap.

Ryan: That is very cheap. And on the technical side, reinforcement learning training cut output tokens by fifty percent compared to the previous model, which drives costs down even further. The KV cache is reduced by a factor of twenty-four compared to Hy3. This thing is clearly positioned for everyday agentic orchestration, where inference costs add up fast.

Maya: And Alibaba is playing in the same ballpark?

Ryan: Alibaba dropped Qwen4-Max, also announced on November 16th. Two-point-four trillion total parameters, native Mixture of Experts architecture, integrated vision, one million token context. Per-task costs are down more than fifty percent compared to Qwen3.8-Max. And Alibaba is explicitly positioning this as a price alternative to current-generation GPT, Claude, and Gemini for enterprise agentic orchestration.

Maya: Two Asian giants showing up with massive but dirt-cheap models, squarely targeting agentic use cases. That trend could not be more obvious.

Ryan: And to round out the industry picture, Hitachi has deployed an agentic AI system for chiplet design, those modular components that go into advanced chip manufacturing. Their system cuts the number of candidates to evaluate in early design by more than ninety percent through automated multi-physics screening. It is the first operational demonstration of agentic orchestration in semiconductor hardware design at this level.

Maya: And on the academic side, OpenAI also made an announcement that is less flashy but genuinely useful.

Ryan: Right, OpenAI launched ChatGPT for Academic Researchers, offering free access to frontier models for US universities, up to thirty seats per institution, with no use of that data for training. The new addition is GPT-Rosalind, a biology-specialized model included in the program. It is a formalization of structured access for the academic community, with specialized models rather than just generic access.

Maya: And that is a wrap on what has been an incredibly packed day. Ryan, if you had to give our listeners two things to take away from today?

Ryan: Number one, the power of AI agents is now being used on both sides of the fence: to build useful systems, and to run large-scale automated attacks. The security of everything you expose has to be an immediate priority. Number two, the price war on models is heating up fast, and Mixture of Experts architectures now make it possible to have highly capable models at costs that bring daily agentic use to an industrial scale. These two trends are going to reinforce each other in the months ahead.

Maya: Ryan, thanks so much for breaking all of that down. And thank you to everyone listening to AI Squared today. We drop a new episode every single day, so we will see you tomorrow for another deep dive into the world of artificial intelligence. See you then.
