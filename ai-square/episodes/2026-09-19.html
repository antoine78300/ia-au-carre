Maya: Good morning and welcome to AI Squared! It is Saturday, the coffee is hot, and honestly... we are pretty happy to be here. This morning, secret biology labs, robots taking over factory floors, and a question that might make you a little uncomfortable: is AI really coming for musicians' jobs? Let's get into it!

Ryan: Good morning Maya! It's Saturday, so I've got my coziest sweater on. Ready for what is shaping up to be a fascinating rundown.

Maya: Perfect. So Ryan, what's the story of the week for you? What's leading the conversation?

Ryan: No hesitation: Anthropic. The company behind Claude, the AI competing head-to-head with ChatGPT, just revealed that it has quietly been building an in-house biology lab. A real lab, with actual researchers, actual equipment, the whole operation.

Maya: Wait, quietly? One of the most talked-about companies in Silicon Valley opened a biology lab and nobody saw it coming?

Ryan: That's exactly it, and that's what makes it so fascinating. The news came out through Reuters, not through some big splashy press release. The idea is to speed up drug discovery using what they call AI agents, meaning AI systems that don't just answer questions but actually conduct research on their own, autonomously.

Maya: So what does that actually look like in practice? Is the AI playing chemist?

Ryan: Pretty much! They're specifically targeting extremely complex molecules, like bispecific antibodies, which are basically molecules that can go after two threats in the body at the same time. And on top of that, they're taking aim at diseases that medicine currently considers untreatable. The ones where the best answer we have right now is simply: we don't know how to fix this.

Maya: That is ambitious. Maybe even a little scary?

Ryan: Honestly, both at once. And here's what makes the timing really interesting: this internal lab is launching at the same moment as another big piece of news. Novo Nordisk, the company behind Ozempic, the drug everyone has been talking about for the past two years, is announcing a partnership with Anthropic to deploy Claude directly into their research workflows.

Maya: So in the same week, Anthropic opens its own lab AND partners with one of the biggest pharmaceutical companies on the planet?

Ryan: Exactly. And this isn't Novo Nordisk's first move in this space. They already had a deal with OpenAI back in April. Now they're adding Anthropic to the mix. What I find really interesting about that strategy is this: rather than betting everything on one single AI to do it all, they're picking different tools for different jobs.

Maya: Kind of like how we all use different apps depending on what we're trying to get done.

Ryan: Exactly right. And what Novo Nordisk is going after goes way beyond Ozempic. They want to fundamentally transform how molecules are discovered in the first place. Not just make existing processes faster, but potentially find treatments for diseases where we are completely stuck right now.

Maya: Okay, that is a huge promise though. Do we have any concrete results yet, or is this still mostly in the dream phase?

Ryan: Honestly, we're still very early days. Approved drugs that came directly from AI-driven research, we're not there yet. But what is already changing is the speed of exploration. An AI can test millions of molecular combinations in a matter of days, where human researchers would need years. The goal isn't to replace scientists, it's to go places they've never had the time to go before.

Maya: Alright. Let's move into our experiments and original projects segment, and this week it's all about robotics!

Ryan: Yes, and this is a space that is just exploding right now. We've got two pieces of news that actually complement each other really well. First, a platform called Tairos has just launched, backed in part by Tencent and other partners, with the goal of making it easier to deploy robots in real industrial environments.

Maya: And for anyone who's not familiar, Tencent is one of China's biggest tech giants, kind of like if you combined Meta and Amazon into one company.

Ryan: That's a good way to put it. And the numbers here are really striking. The embodied robotics market, meaning physical robots acting in the real world guided by AI, was worth 14 billion dollars in 2025. By 2030, we're talking 61 billion.

Maya: The market quadruples in five years. That is a staggering trajectory.

Ryan: And this isn't theoretical. A company called Geek Plus already has 72,000 robots deployed across warehouses, factories, and logistics centers around the world. These robots are on the job right now, not sitting in a lab somewhere as prototypes.

Maya: 72,000 robots. That's basically an entire city's worth of robots, all clocking in.

Ryan: A pretty productive city at that! And the whole idea behind Tairos is to lower the barrier to entry. Up until now, deploying smart robots was extremely expensive and required very specialized expertise. With a shared open-source platform, even mid-sized companies can start accessing this technology.

Maya: And there was also something about a major robotics summit in China this week?

Ryan: Right! In Hangzhou, the 9th China Robot Summit brought together more than 2,000 experts. What's interesting is that the conversation wasn't just about rolling more robots out the door. They're working on standardizing how we evaluate robot capabilities, kind of like how we have safety standards for cars or elevators.

Maya: Making sure the robot can actually do the job before you let it loose in a real factory.

Ryan: Exactly. And they identified three critical challenges. One: fast information processing, because a robot needs to react in real time. Two: environmental perception, meaning it has to understand what's around it even when things change. Three: dynamic physical control, moving with precision in an unpredictable environment.

Maya: And then the last piece of this segment is the founder of Spirit AI, a Chinese startup, making a pretty bold claim about 2027?

Ryan: Yes, he's saying that robots have already evolved from executing simple, isolated tasks to handling complex, continuous workflows across large areas. And he's predicting a major breakthrough by 2027 on what he's calling robot brains, the cognitive capabilities built directly into the robots themselves.

Maya: Should we believe him?

Ryan: With the healthy skepticism you should always bring to a startup founder's announcements... let's say it's plausible. The growth curve is real. But between making the announcement and actually delivering the breakthrough, there's usually a gap of a few years.

Maya: Fair and wise. Alright, let's move into the segment I really care about this week: AI, society, and creativity. And this one is not a feel-good story.

Ryan: No, it really isn't. UNESCO has just published a study called Reshaping Policies for Creativity Projects. And some of the numbers are pretty sobering.

Maya: Lay it on us.

Ryan: Generative AI is projected to reduce musicians' revenues by 24 percent by 2028. And the audiovisual industry, movies, series, all of it, by 21 percent. In less than two years.

Maya: That is massive. We're talking about one in four dollars of a musician's income, just gone.

Ryan: And what's really notable about this report is the tone. Tim Curtis, UNESCO's regional director for South Asia, is not saying AI is the villain here. He's saying that if we don't act right now with clear policy frameworks and real technological guardrails, the transformation is going to happen faster than anyone can adapt to.

Maya: There's a big difference between being dragged through a change and actually steering it.

Ryan: Exactly. And that connects directly to another story out this week, this one from the advertising world. A creative director at VML in Riyadh is sounding the alarm about what she calls AI slop.

Maya: I love that phrase.

Ryan: Content generated at massive scale that looks finished, clean, professional... but has absolutely no soul. No emotional authenticity. She says algorithmically produced ads are all starting to look the same, and they end up saying nothing to anyone.

Maya: That is so real, because it's exactly what you feel sometimes when you're scrolling. You see a stunning image and you just... don't care at all.

Ryan: That's the paradox. The technical quality is there, but something is missing. And her argument is that what protects human creativity is precisely what AI struggles to replicate: judgment, genuine emotion, the ability to imagine something that does not exist yet.

Maya: Do you actually buy that distinction? Or do you think AI will eventually learn that too?

Ryan: I think AI is going to get more and more convincing emotionally. But there's a difference between simulating an emotion and actually living one. And in creativity, that difference matters. The real question is whether the audience will notice. And more importantly, whether they'll care.

Maya: That might be the defining philosophical question of our era. And on that note, we have arrived at our favorite segment.

Ryan: The What If!

Maya: The What If! Ryan, take it away.

Ryan: Okay, here is my open question for this week. We talked about Anthropic building a biology lab so that AI can discover treatments for diseases that are incurable today. At the same time, we're seeing robots take up more and more space in industry, in healthcare, in logistics. And on the other side, we have creators watching their livelihoods get threatened by AI systems that can replicate their style.

Maya: Yeah, that's basically our three big stories this week, wrapped up in one.

Ryan: And my question is this: what if, twenty years from now, AI has genuinely eliminated some of today's incurable diseases, but in exchange, entire creative professions have disappeared? Would that be progress? Would we take that deal?

Maya: Ooh. That is a beautifully cruel question.

Ryan: Because if AI cures cancer but kills the music industry, do we say thank you, or do we grieve?

Maya: My instinct is to say both things can coexist. That we can have incredible medical breakthroughs and still protect space for human creativity. But that takes some really strong, deliberate policy choices.

Ryan: And that is exactly what UNESCO is saying. This is not inevitable. It is a question of collective choices. We get to decide how these technologies are deployed, where we let them run freely, and where we put guardrails in place.

Maya: And that decision is being made right now. Not in twenty years.

Ryan: Right. Because in twenty years, it may be too late for some of these things.

Maya: There's something to sit with on a quiet Saturday morning. Maybe while listening to some human-made music, just for the contrast.

Ryan: Highly recommended.

Maya: Ryan, thank you for another fascinating and slightly dizzying tour through the week, as always.

Ryan: My pleasure! And honestly, for a Saturday, that was a pretty solid one.

Maya: This has been AI Squared, the podcast that makes artificial intelligence accessible to everyone, every single day. Thanks so much for listening, take care of yourselves, enjoy the rest of your Saturday, and we will see you tomorrow for a brand new episode. See you tomorrow!
