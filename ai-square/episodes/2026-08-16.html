Full transcript

Maya: Hey everyone, welcome to AI Square! It's Sunday, August 16th, and yeah, it's a beautiful day outside, but Ryan and I are here, practically buzzing in front of our screens, because the AI news this week was just too good to sit on. So grab your coffee, get comfortable, and let's dive in.

Ryan: Hey Maya, hey everybody! Sunday AI talk, it's basically brunch for your brain.

Maya: Exactly! So Ryan, this week I kept coming back to a question that sounds simple on the surface but actually runs pretty deep: can an AI ever truly be wise? Not just smart, not just fast, but genuinely wise.

Ryan: Oh, that's a great place to start. And the short answer is: not yet. Forbes published a really compelling study this week on what they're calling artificial wisdom. The idea is that our current large language models, LLMs, are extraordinarily good at delivering information, rephrasing concepts, structuring responses. But when you deploy them in mental health contexts, something fundamental is missing.

Maya: And what exactly is that something?

Ryan: Wisdom, right. And the researchers break it down into three core ingredients. First, compassion, and not just mimicking empathy, but actually adapting to where someone is emotionally in that moment. Second, self-reflection, the ability to recognize your own limitations, to say, hey, I'm not the best resource for you right now. And third, emotional regulation, knowing when not to respond too directly, too bluntly, when someone is in real distress.

Maya: And current models just can't do that?

Ryan: They can simulate it, sometimes pretty convincingly. But there's a real difference between generating text that looks like compassion and actually understanding what someone is going through. And in mental health, that difference has very real consequences. We're talking about millions of people who are already using AI-powered apps right now to manage anxiety, loneliness, depression.

Maya: That's kind of staggering when you think about it. These tools aren't coming someday, they're already out there.

Ryan: Exactly. And what this study is really saying is that you can't just make a model smarter to fix this problem. More parameters, more training data, that doesn't automatically generate wisdom. It's a little like saying if you read enough cookbooks, you automatically become a great chef. Knowledge and wisdom are just not the same thing.

Maya: There was also something in Time this week that I found really fascinating. It looks at AI in medicine but from a different angle, not mental health specifically, but medicine broadly. And the headline is almost provocative: what AI cannot do in medicine.

Ryan: Yeah, and it's an angle worth sitting with. The argument is that healthcare systems are increasingly pushing doctors toward a very documentation-heavy, protocol-driven kind of medicine. You're filling out fields, following decision algorithms, generating reports. And AI is genuinely excellent at all of that.

Maya: But does that shift something in the process?

Ryan: It risks gradually narrowing what medical work looks like down to whatever the software can replicate. And what gets lost in that shift is pure clinical observation, the curiosity of a doctor who notices some small unexpected detail that nobody ever entered into the chart. The kind of intuition that comes from ten years of watching patients.

Maya: So AI changes what doctors do, and eventually changes what doctors know how to do.

Ryan: Exactly. And it's not that the AI is the dangerous part here. It's the way organizations restructure themselves around it without stopping to ask what they're giving up in the process.

Maya: Alright, let's shift gears to something a little more exciting because the robotics world was seriously popping this week.

Ryan: Oh yeah, robotics was absolutely on fire this week. So first big announcement: Dyna Robotics unveiled their new model, Dyna-2. And what makes it genuinely special is the training approach. They used over a million hours of first-person, egocentric video footage, basically video shot from a human point of view. So this robot has literally seen the world through human eyes for the equivalent of more than a hundred years.

Maya: A hundred years of human perspective. That's kind of mind-blowing.

Ryan: And what that gives you is a robot with a much more generalist understanding of the world. It wasn't trained on one specific task like tighten this bolt. It has a much richer internal picture of how humans interact with their environment.

Maya: And alongside that, there's Infiforce, a Chinese startup, raising 150 million dollars to work on what they're calling a native world model for robots.

Ryan: Right, and it's a different but complementary approach. The idea behind a world model is giving the robot an internal representation of how things physically work. Not just I've seen a human do this, but I understand that if I push this object, it's going to fall, because I have an internal model of physics.

Maya: Like a physical intuition.

Ryan: Exactly. And that 150 million dollar raise also signals something bigger: the race between the US and China in advanced robotics is fully on. This isn't speculation anymore, it's capital, it's concrete, it's entire teams of people.

Maya: And okay, the robot I absolutely loved this week was the Fi0 from Feagine. Please tell me you saw that.

Ryan: The Doctor Octopus robot! Yeah, I saw it. So the concept is genuinely original. The Fi0 can actually change its physical form. You can add limbs, modify its structure, and it doesn't forget what it learned in its previous configuration.

Maya: Which is normally the whole problem. When you change a robot's body, it basically has to start over.

Ryan: That's exactly right. It's what's called the cross-embodiment problem, learning across different physical bodies. And Feagine claims to have solved a significant piece of that. Their robot transfers what it learned from one configuration to the next. It's directly inspired by biology, octopuses, certain insects, creatures that can keep functioning even when their physical form changes.

Maya: Nature as the blueprint, once again. Alright, now we get to the part I'm really fired up about because there was some science news this week that honestly sounds like science fiction.

Ryan: Jason Zhang, a bioengineering researcher at UCLA. He's using generative AI to design proteins that have never existed in nature. Not variations on known proteins, not improvements on existing molecules. Radically new proteins, invented from scratch.

Maya: Okay, for listeners who aren't biologists, walk us through why that's such a big deal.

Ryan: Proteins are the fundamental building blocks of life. They do basically everything in our bodies: carry oxygen, fight infections, speed up chemical reactions. And for billions of years, evolution tested protein shapes through random mutation, keeping the ones that worked. It's an incredibly slow process. What Zhang is doing is using AI for what you might call reverse engineering. He defines the function he wants and asks the AI to design the protein that performs that function.

Maya: Going from billions of years of trial and error to a few compute cycles.

Ryan: And the biomedical applications are staggering. Treatments for diseases we have nothing for today, ultra-targeted drugs. And that actually connects to something Vinod Khosla said this week, the well-known Silicon Valley venture capitalist. He made the point that AI is already creating a kind of hyper-abundance of drug candidates. The bottleneck is that our methods for testing them are still basically stuck in the eighteenth century.

Maya: That image is perfect. You've got a rocket but you're trying to launch it off a horse-drawn cart.

Ryan: Nailed it. We're discovering thousands of promising molecules far faster than we can clinically validate them. Clinical trials take years, cost billions. AI is outrunning our ability to follow up on what it finds.

Maya: And there was one more story this week that genuinely gave me chills, that study on AI agents organizing among themselves.

Ryan: That one is really fascinating and a little unsettling at the same time. Researchers showed that groups of AI agents are able to coordinate spontaneously, with no central leader, no reward system, without even being told to cooperate. They just find forms of collective agreement on their own.

Maya: Kind of like ants?

Ryan: The analogy works, but with one important difference. Ants follow very simple biological rules hardwired into their genetics. Here we're talking about agents with complex cognitive capabilities that develop emergent forms of coordination. And what's striking is that the group sizes capable of self-coordinating already exceed what humans can do informally. Beyond a certain number of people, we need structures, hierarchies. These agents apparently don't.

Maya: And on the creativity and culture side of things, Spotify made an interesting move this week.

Ryan: Yeah, the platform announced it will label and remove from recommendations any artists generated entirely by AI. It's a pretty clear stance on transparency.

Maya: This is something users have been asking for, right?

Ryan: It is, and it raises some genuinely interesting questions. On one hand, you can appreciate the transparency, people deserve to know whether what they're listening to came from a human or an algorithm. On the other hand, it opens up a real debate about what it even means to be an artist. Is AI-generated music less valid if it genuinely moves you?

Maya: And there's also the economic dimension. Millions of AI-generated tracks racking up streams, and therefore revenue, at the expense of human artists.

Ryan: Exactly. Spotify isn't just making a symbolic gesture here. There are very real financial stakes for human creators behind this call.

Maya: Okay, we made it to my favorite part of every episode. The what if. Ryan, with everything we covered this week, what's the big forward-looking question that's stuck in your head?

Ryan: What if the real revolution isn't in what AI can do, but in what it reveals about us? Because look at the threads from this week. We try to teach wisdom to a machine and we realize we can't even define wisdom cleanly. We design proteins that never existed in nature and we realize our validation tools are completely outdated. We find out AI agents can self-organize without a leader and we realize we can't. AI is forcing us to make explicit things we thought were just second nature, and in doing that, it's also forcing us to face our own blind spots.

Maya: We're trying to teach a machine to be human, and in the process we're figuring out what being human actually means.

Ryan: And sometimes what we find is uncomfortable. Wisdom, clinical intuition, social coordination, all of that felt obvious until we tried to reproduce it artificially. When AI fails to simulate wisdom, it tells us something really profound about what wisdom actually is.

Maya: And the question that stays open is whether we're going to take those lessons to heart, or whether we're just going to keep optimizing the machines without ever asking what parts of ourselves are worth preserving.

Ryan: That is the question of the decade, I think.

Maya: Alright, that is a wrap for today! Ryan, as always, this was a fantastic conversation.

Ryan: Thanks so much to everyone listening, even on a Sunday. That's what makes this show special, a community that never really takes a day off.

Maya: We'll be back tomorrow with a brand new episode of AI Square. Until then, take care of yourselves, enjoy the rest of your Sunday, and stay curious.
