Maya: Hey everyone, welcome to AI Squared, the podcast that breaks down artificial intelligence every single day, no exceptions. I'm Maya, and today is a packed one: OpenAI is dropping GPT-6, Alibaba is gunning for ten trillion parameters, and researchers at MIT are finally giving us a real answer to the question of when you can actually trust an AI agent to make decisions on its own. Buckle up, let's get into it.

Maya: Ryan, good to have you here. Let's jump straight into the biggest story of the day, and it is a big one: OpenAI is announcing GPT-6, and it comes in two versions called Sol and Luna. What exactly are we looking at?

Ryan: Hey Maya, great to be here. Yeah, this is the announcement of the day. OpenAI is rolling out two new models that represent a pretty clear shift in philosophy. GPT-6 Sol is the high-end model: it delivers twice the accuracy of GPT-5.6, at the same price point. GPT-6 Luna is the efficiency model: it hits the performance levels we used to associate with premium models, but at a dramatically lower cost.

Maya: So the story here isn't "more powerful than anything ever" — it's "cheaper and more reliable"?

Ryan: Exactly. And that is a really significant commercial pivot. Up until now, the AI race looked like a pure speed race, everyone just chasing the most powerful model. Now OpenAI is saying clearly that what companies actually care about when they're deploying agents in production is the cost-to-performance ratio. And this matters even more because Anthropic simultaneously launched Claude Opus 5.5, targeting the exact same segment. These two giants are now going head to head on the same playing field.

Maya: And in practical terms, for someone who's actually using these models day to day, what does this change?

Ryan: It changes a lot. If you're a company running AI agents continuously — systems that make decisions automatically, handling thousands of requests a day — the cost of API calls becomes a major limiting factor. Luna is the promise that you can scale massively without your bill going through the roof. Sol is for the cases where precision is critical: medical, legal, finance. We're shifting from a mindset of tech demos to a mindset of industrial deployment.

Maya: And on the other side of the world, Alibaba is definitely not sitting on its hands.

Ryan: Not at all. Alibaba is announcing plans to build a model targeting somewhere between five and ten trillion parameters. To put that in perspective, that's two to four times the size of their current best model, Qwen 3.8 Max. And to get there, they're unveiling a new proprietary chip called the Zhenwu V900, which they claim is three times more performant than their previous generation and can be deployed in clusters of five hundred thousand cards.

Maya: Five hundred thousand cards. That is an absolutely massive infrastructure.

Ryan: It's truly titanic. And you have to read this announcement in its geopolitical context. Alibaba is investing heavily in its own chips precisely because US export restrictions on semiconductors are cutting them off from access to the most advanced Nvidia hardware. This is a direct response to that constraint, and it's also a signal: China is building its own AI value chain, from the chip all the way up to frontier models.

Maya: A real challenge to technological dependency. Alright, let's move on to new tools, because there's some interesting stuff on the frameworks and security side. Ryan, let's start with security, because Zimperium just published something that should make people stop and think.

Ryan: Yeah, and this is a topic I care a lot about, because we talk constantly about the benefits of AI agents without talking enough about the security risks they create. Zimperium, a company that specializes in mobile security, just published an analysis documenting a new attack vector: AI agents are now being used by bad actors to design, adapt, and deploy sophisticated attacks against mobile applications at scale. What used to require highly skilled experts is now accessible to pretty much anyone with a well-configured agent.

Maya: So the technical barrier to launching a cyberattack is basically collapsing?

Ryan: That's exactly it. An attacker no longer needs to master reverse engineering, vulnerability exploitation, or writing malicious code. An AI agent can do that work for them, adapt it in real time, and deploy it at mass scale. And what's particularly worrying in the Zimperium report is that these attacks are happening outside traditional enterprise environments — on personal devices, consumer apps, areas that security teams have a much harder time monitoring.

Maya: It's a kind of democratization that's genuinely alarming. On a more positive note, Corelight is working to strengthen security on the defense side, with tools aimed at security operations centers.

Ryan: Right, and it's a direct response to exactly this kind of threat. Corelight is integrating what they call an Agent Builder Library — essentially a library of pre-built investigation material for security agents. In practical terms, an analyst in a security operations center can now build monitoring queries in plain natural language, without having to code complex rules. And one important detail: it works in air-gapped environments, meaning no cloud connection required, which is absolutely essential for sensitive infrastructure — defense, energy, hospitals.

Maya: Then on the frameworks side, Splunk just published a comparison that takes stock of where things stand in 2026.

Ryan: It's a really useful document for developers. Splunk lays out the evolution of the major agent frameworks, and highlights one major architectural shift at Google. Their agent development kit, the Google ADK, has moved since May 2026 from a hierarchical architecture — where agents had fixed roles — to a graph-based engine where agents, tools, and functions are nodes you can connect freely. That's way more flexible for building complex systems. Splunk covers eight major frameworks in total: CrewAI, LangGraph, the OpenAI Agents SDK, Google ADK, Microsoft's tools, Pydantic AI, Strands, and the A2A protocol. And they offer an observability tool that works across all those platforms, which addresses a genuine pain point in the market.

Maya: Speaking of observability — that's a word you're hearing everywhere right now. OpenObserve is shipping version 1.0, right?

Ryan: Yes, and the number they drop in their announcement is striking. According to a study by the firm Cube, ninety-three percent of companies are already building custom agents. But only about one in five of them have mature governance in place to monitor those agents. OpenObserve v1.0 wants to close that gap by offering a unified platform that traces a request all the way from the user's browser through to the full execution of an agentic loop, with logs, metrics, and session evaluations all in one interface.

Maya: Now let's get to the research section, and this might be the story with the most real-world practical value today: when can you actually let an AI agent make decisions on its own?

Ryan: It's a fundamental question, and MIT Sloan just published a very concrete decision framework to answer it. They worked with One New Zealand, a telecom operator that was already running more than fifty operational AI agents by early 2026. And the question this operator was asking was: how far can we actually go in delegating decisions to these agents?

Maya: And what does MIT say?

Ryan: They answer with a framework built on four pillars. Pillar one: build an internal AI center of excellence — a dedicated team that understands both the technical capabilities and the business stakes. Pillar two: build a unified data platform, because an agent can only make good decisions if it has access to consistent, high-quality data. Pillar three: put in place a horizontal orchestration layer, so agents can collaborate with each other in a coordinated way. And pillar four, the one I think is the most important: an iterative responsible AI policy. This isn't a document you write once and file away — it's a framework you revise regularly as agents take on more autonomy.

Maya: That fourth point is huge. Because we're talking about systems that evolve fast, and a policy written in 2025 can already be outdated by 2026.

Ryan: Absolutely. And that's exactly what MIT emphasizes: trust in agents has to be earned gradually, with regular review mechanisms built in. You don't go overnight from an agent that suggests to an agent that decides. That takes instrumentation, guardrails, and an organizational culture that's willing to continuously re-examine how much it's delegating.

Maya: Let's move on to our industry impact segment. Ryan, we've got two stories that really illustrate how agents are spreading into very different sectors.

Ryan: Yeah, and I want to start with PeopleReign, an HR solution that integrates what they call agentic loops. The idea is to let HR teams at small and mid-sized businesses configure agents in plain natural language, no coding required, with workflow cloning and automated remediation of knowledge gaps. What's interesting here is the target audience: small and medium-sized businesses. Up to now, sophisticated AI agents were mostly accessible to large enterprises with dedicated technical teams. PeopleReign and similar solutions are signaling a real democratization of access to agentic AI.

Maya: And the MCP protocol they're integrating is something we've been watching closely for several months now.

Ryan: Exactly. The Model Context Protocol is a standard that lets agents interact with external tools in a standardized way. The fact that PeopleReign is integrating it natively shows that this protocol is becoming a baseline expectation for any serious agentic solution — even in sectors like HR.

Maya: And on the legal side, Casepoint is launching Casepoint IQ.

Ryan: Yeah, and the legal sector is particularly interesting because it's a highly regulated field where mistakes have real and serious consequences. Casepoint IQ is a unified platform that combines assistive capabilities — drafting support and research assistance — with agentic capabilities to automate workflows like e-discovery, which involves sifting through millions of documents in legal proceedings. The fact that they're baking governance directly into the platform rather than treating it as an external layer is a strong signal about what the market is now expecting from AI vendors in the legal space.

Maya: To wrap things up, a quick mention on Xiaomi and OpenAI on the research side.

Ryan: Yes, two quick ones. Xiaomi is open-sourcing their MiMo-V2.6 series — multimodal models capable of reading a screen, reasoning about what they see, and deciding on an action in a continuous loop, which is a foundational building block for agents that control computers or physical environments. And OpenAI is announcing that an internal model, not publicly deployed, solved more than a hundred open mathematical problems in twenty-four days. That's a symbolically significant announcement in the ongoing debate about LLM reasoning capabilities — but to be clear, this model is not available to developers today.

Maya: Appreciate the honesty on that. So Ryan, what do we take away from today, September 23rd, 2026?

Ryan: Three big takeaways. First: economic efficiency has become the new battleground for frontier models — GPT-6 and Claude Opus 5.5 are proof of that. Second: agentic AI is simultaneously creating automation opportunities and new security risks, and both are moving at the same pace. Third: governance is no longer a theoretical nice-to-have — it's an operational necessity. MIT says it, OpenObserve says it, Casepoint says it.

Maya: And I'd add a fourth: the AI race is no longer just an American story. Alibaba and Xiaomi are reminding us today that this ecosystem is genuinely global, and the stakes around technological sovereignty have never been more relevant.
