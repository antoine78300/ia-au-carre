Full transcript

Maya: Welcome to AI Squared, the podcast that breaks down artificial intelligence every single day — no jargon, no fluff, just straight talk. Today is October 2nd, 2026, and we are diving into a world where AI agents are starting to act on their own — buying things, writing code, making decisions — while regulators, companies, and researchers are all scrambling to keep up. Buckle up, because we are moving fast.

Maya: Ryan, good to have you here! Let's kick things off with a question that I have to imagine is making a lot of boardrooms pretty uncomfortable right now: are AI agents in the enterprise actually delivering?

Ryan: Hey Maya. And the short answer is: barely. A study published in July 2026 by Teradata in partnership with Wakefield Research, surveying a thousand business decision-makers, found that only 7% of organizations are reaching the stage of tangible, real-world results with agentic AI. Seven percent. That number hits hard.

Maya: Seven percent. So what are the other 93% doing?

Ryan: They are experimenting. 68% of organizations are still in the pilot phase. They are running tests, launching proof-of-concept projects, kicking the tires. And Gartner — the gold standard for industry analysis — is predicting that 40% of agentic AI projects will simply be cancelled by the end of 2027. Just abandoned.

Maya: But why? We have been hearing for months that the models keep getting more powerful. If the model is not the problem, what is?

Ryan: This is exactly where it gets really interesting. Analysis published by Forbes India makes it pretty clear: the model itself is not the issue. What is breaking down is the operational architecture and the governance layer around it. In other words, companies have capable models, but they have not built the scaffolding around them — the processes, the rules, the control systems — that would allow these agents to operate reliably and safely in a real professional environment.

Maya: So you have got the engine, but nobody built the car around it.

Ryan: Exactly. And what we are seeing is that all the hype around AI agents has created what you might call a confidence gap. Companies move fast, they overestimate how mature the system is, and when an agent makes a mistake in production — and it will — there is no safety net, no governance structure there to catch it.

Maya: And that governance question is going to come up again when we get to what is happening on the regulatory front later in the show. We will circle back to that. But first, let us talk tools and frameworks, because there was some real movement this week. Ryan, Amazon dropped something?

Ryan: Yes, Amazon Web Services launched Strands Decider 2B, an open-source decision-making model. And the key thing to understand here is what separates this from a general-purpose large language model. Strands Decider 2B is not built to write emails or answer broad questions. It is designed to do exactly one thing: make calibrated decisions, and do it in fractions of a second, at very low cost. It runs locally, which means you do not have to send your data up to the cloud.

Maya: What is it built on under the hood?

Ryan: It is built on Qwen 3.5-2B. And what makes this launch really noteworthy is the timing: the very same week, OpenAI announced a similar decision-focused offering. So within just a few days, two of the biggest players in the space are each positioning specialized decision-making models. That is a strong signal that the market is fragmenting. We are moving away from the idea of one universal model that does everything, and toward architectures where each component is purpose-built for a specific task.

Maya: It is almost like the way jobs work inside a company. You have got your accountant, your lawyer, your sales rep — each one is an expert in their lane.

Ryan: That is a great way to put it. And in that spirit of multi-agent architectures, there is also a really solid explainer piece making the rounds this week on communication protocols between agents. Because if you have got multiple specialized agents working together, they need to be able to talk to each other.

Maya: So how does that actually work?

Ryan: A few standards are emerging. There is MCP, which gives an agent structured access to enterprise tools and data. Then there is A2A — Agent to Agent — which handles coordination between autonomous agents. The idea is having clear rules: who delegates what to whom, how results get passed back up the chain, how errors are handled. And there is an important distinction that this analysis makes: implementing these protocols is a completely different skill set from actually building agents. These are two separate layers of technical expertise that engineering teams need to develop independently.

Maya: Alright, let us shift to the research side, because this next one is genuinely jaw-dropping. There is a paper in Science that is getting a ton of attention.

Ryan: Yes, and it is worth slowing down for. A study published in Science in September 2026 introduces something called Virtual Biotech, a multi-agent framework built specifically for drug discovery. The core idea is to coordinate specialized AI agents across every stage of the pharmaceutical process, from identifying a molecular target all the way through clinical trials.

Maya: And what did they actually do with it?

Ryan: They deployed more than 37,000 AI agents to analyze 55,984 clinical trials. That is a scale that would simply be impossible for human teams to tackle in any reasonable timeframe. And the results are striking: targeting genes specific to a particular cell type increases the chances of a drug reaching the market by 48%, and reduces adverse events — meaning serious side effects — by 32%.

Maya: A 48% improvement in the odds of getting a drug to market is massive in an industry where failure rates are already sky-high.

Ryan: Absolutely. And what is really critical to highlight here is that this was not a simulation or a lab demonstration. This is the first prospective, operational validation of multi-step AI agents in drug discovery. In plain terms: it worked under real conditions. That is a genuine turning point for pharmaceutical research.

Maya: Now let us get into the industrial impact side of things, and this is where it starts to get a little more charged, especially when you bring regulators into the picture. Ryan, the FTC is making some noise?

Ryan: Quite a bit of noise. The Federal Trade Commission — the agency that oversees competition and consumer protection in the US — has opened an investigation into OpenAI and Anthropic over their autonomous AI agents. The potential charge: unfair practices tied to the deployment of these agents.

Maya: Why do autonomous AI agents raise a fundamentally different liability question than, say, a regular chatbot?

Ryan: That is the central question this investigation is trying to answer. A chatbot responds and stops. An AI agent can execute code, make purchases, send emails — all without a human watching in real time. And that changes everything from a legal standpoint. Who is on the hook when the agent makes a bad call? The company that built the model? The company that deployed it? The end user? The traditional liability framework, which assumes a human operator is in the loop, just does not hold up when the agent is acting on its own.

Maya: And it is not just federal regulators getting involved — there is action happening at the state level too?

Ryan: Yes, and that trend is picking up speed. New Mexico just announced an initiative led by the state attorney general and local lawmakers to regulate uncontrolled autonomous AI models. That stacks on top of similar moves in other states, like the New York RAISE Act. What we are seeing is regulatory fragmentation across the US, with individual states starting to legislate in their own ways, which is creating a growing headache for companies trying to deploy these agents at a national scale.

Ryan: And in the financial sector — which I know is something a lot of our listeners pay close attention to — Nasdaq has also made a move on the agentic AI front.

Maya: What did they do?

Ryan: Nasdaq has integrated an agentic AI framework into its Calypso platform, which is already used by a large number of financial institutions. The goal is to provide a governed environment for deploying and scaling AI agents across the full trading lifecycle, from order intake all the way through settlement and delivery. That word "governed" is key here, because in finance, letting an agent operate without strict guardrails means exposing the institution to enormous regulatory and financial risk.

Maya: And speaking of measurable financial value from agentic AI, we also have an announcement from Caylent that really illustrates how this technology is starting to prove its worth in cloud operations — with actual numbers to back it up.

Ryan: Right. Caylent was just named a major player by IDC in its 2026 MarketScape for professional cloud services in the agentic era. Their flagship product, Caylent Accelerate, is built on Amazon Bedrock AgentCore and was trained on twelve years of cloud operations data. The results their clients are reporting: 70% of remediation work accelerated, a 40% reduction in mean time to resolve incidents, and up to 75% time savings in application modernization projects.

Maya: Those are numbers that speak directly to CFOs.

Ryan: And that is precisely why they are converting. Not because the technology is impressive on paper, but because it directly hits operational costs. This actually ties back to what that enterprise failure study was telling us at the top of the show: what separates the 7% who are succeeding is exactly this ability to translate technical performance into measurable business value.

Maya: As we wrap up today's episode, Ryan, if you had to pull one through-line from everything we covered today, what would it be?

Ryan: I would say that 2026 is the year AI agents move from the lab to the courtroom — in every sense of that phrase. On one side, research like Virtual Biotech is proving that agents can operate at a scale and precision we have never seen before. On the other side, the FTC, state legislatures, and soon Europe are all going to have to answer a fundamental question: who is responsible when an agent goes wrong? And caught in the middle, the companies that actually survive this transition will not be the ones with the best model — remember, 93% are falling short despite having capable models — but the ones that built the governance structure around it.

Maya: Could not have said it better. Ryan, thank you so much for breaking all of that down. And thank you to everyone who tuned in for today's episode of AI Squared. If you are enjoying the show, tell a friend — word of mouth genuinely makes a difference. We will be back tomorrow with another deep dive into everything happening in the world of artificial intelligence. See you tomorrow!
