Maya: Welcome to AI Squared, the podcast that breaks down artificial intelligence and its impact on our world every single day. I'm Maya, and today is one of those episodes where the news just keeps coming at you from every direction. Hospitals, banks, trucking fleets, governments — autonomous AI agents are reshaping all of it. Let's dive straight in.

Maya: Ryan, good to have you here. Let's kick things off with the biggest story of the morning, and it comes from the world of finance — a document a lot of people have been waiting on for a long time.

Ryan: Hey Maya, absolutely. The Financial Stability Board — the FSB — which is the international body responsible for monitoring the stability of the global financial system, has just released its final report titled Sound Practices for Responsible Adoption of Artificial Intelligence. This is the first time we have a unified, global framework specifically designed to govern agentic AI in finance. And what makes this report remarkable goes well beyond the fact that it's official.

Maya: So what actually sets it apart from the other governance documents we've seen up to this point?

Ryan: What really jumps out is a very deliberate terminological choice. The FSB is dropping the phrase "synthetic employees" when referring to AI agents. Their position is that the term is too anthropomorphic — it carries too many human connotations. Instead, the report redefines AI agents as complex autonomous software systems, without human reasoning. Now that might sound like a semantic detail, but it really isn't. By making that call, the FSB is sending a very clear signal to financial institutions: do not treat your AI agents like human colleagues with implied responsibilities. Treat them like technical systems that require structured, non-anthropomorphic governance.

Maya: And what does that actually mean in practice for, say, a bank?

Ryan: It changes everything about how you organize accountability. If you think of an AI agent as an employee, you're naturally going to delegate tasks to it and assume it "understands" its own limits. But if you treat it as an autonomous software system, you have to explicitly define what it's allowed to do, what it's not allowed to do, who is responsible for its decisions, and how you audit its actions. And here's where it gets really interesting — this FSB framework lands in perfect sync with something the American Medical Association published on the exact same day, for healthcare.

Maya: Let's get into that, because the AMA is the most influential medical organization in the country, and they're also putting out a governance framework for AI agents — but focused on the clinical side.

Ryan: Exactly. And the structure of their approach is really telling. The AMA organizes its framework around four critical questions that every healthcare institution needs to answer before deploying an AI agent in a clinical setting. What actions is the agent authorized to take? Who is accountable for those actions? It's a perimeter-and-accountability logic that's very much in the same spirit as what the FSB is asking of finance. What's new here is that up until now, AI governance initiatives in healthcare came almost entirely from government agencies. This time, it's a medical organization — one that represents physicians — stepping up and setting its own rules.

Maya: That almost feels like a sign of maturity for the industry.

Ryan: That's exactly what it is. When the professionals within a field start producing their own governance standards, it tells you the technology is no longer experimental. It's in real deployment, with real stakes, affecting real patients. We've moved from a pilot phase into a normalization phase.

Maya: We're going to take a quick break from that big picture, and when we come back we're getting into tools and frameworks — including some news that technical teams are really going to want to hear. And trust me, there is a security alert today that you absolutely cannot ignore.

Maya: Ryan, teams running AI agents in production got some pretty rough news today.

Ryan: Really rough news, yes. Check Point, the cybersecurity firm, has discovered two critical vulnerabilities in an LLM gateway that's widely deployed across enterprise environments. They're tracked as CVE-2026-35029 and CVE-2026-59823. What these vulnerabilities actually allow is, first, unauthorized administrator access, and second, remote code execution through malicious Python modules.

Maya: Let's back up for a second — what exactly is an LLM gateway for listeners who aren't necessarily deep in the technical weeds?

Ryan: Think of an LLM gateway as the front door that connects your internal applications to the large language models your organization uses. It's typically a centralized software layer that handles requests, authentication, and usage quotas. If that gateway is compromised, an attacker doesn't need to go after each individual application. They take control of the central hub. And with the ability to execute remote code, the entire AI infrastructure of an organization is potentially exposed.

Maya: And the timing here is particularly alarming, isn't it, right at the moment when companies are spinning up more and more autonomous agent deployments?

Ryan: That's exactly the problem. The more autonomy you give to AI agents, the more strategically valuable that gateway becomes as an entry point for an attacker. An autonomous agent can trigger real-world actions — place orders, send communications, modify data. If someone takes control of the gateway that's orchestrating those agents, the consequences could be catastrophic. And this is actually something that was front and center at the SecTor conference in Toronto just two days ago, which brought together top cybersecurity minds from Canada and internationally.

Maya: What was the main takeaway from SecTor?

Ryan: A very sharp wake-up call. Helen Oakley, a strategic AI and cybersecurity advisor, put her finger on something really essential: autonomous AI agents don't pose the same security challenges as traditional threats. It's not just about protecting data or blocking intrusions anymore. It's about decision-making authority. When an agent is making decisions in place of humans, who controls those decisions? Who can override them? SecTor framed this redefinition of the governance perimeter in security as a fundamentally new challenge — unlike anything the industry has dealt with before.

Maya: On a more constructive note when it comes to tools, ServiceNow also made a pretty significant announcement today.

Ryan: They did, and it reads like a direct response to exactly these governance concerns. ServiceNow, which powers the operations of hundreds of thousands of businesses around the world, announced the integration of native governance controls for autonomous AI agents directly into its platform. What's notable is that these controls are embedded at the ERP level itself — not bolted on as an external layer. And alongside that, they're rolling out automation tools integrated into their EmployeeWorks module that let employees build specialized AI assistants without writing a single line of code.

Maya: Let's take another quick break, and when we come back we're getting into research and some really concrete examples of industries crossing a major threshold with AI agents.

Maya: Ryan, we've got some very concrete industry examples today of organizations that are taking things to a whole new level. Let's start with finance — and a pretty well-known British bank.

Ryan: Starling Bank. It's a British neobank with 6.2 million customers, and they just announced something genuinely remarkable. They're developing what they're calling a Transactional Foundation Model — TFM — trained on 15 billion anonymized customer transactions. And the technology sovereignty angle here is really significant.

Maya: What do you mean by technology sovereignty?

Ryan: Starling chose to train this model in partnership with Civo, a British sovereign cloud provider. And that's not a small footnote. Up until now, when a European company wanted to train a large AI model, they were almost entirely dependent on American hyperscalers — Amazon, Google, Microsoft. Starling is explicitly saying it wants to reduce that dependency and keep its British customers' data on British infrastructure. They're the first British bank to train a proprietary AI model of this scale on a local sovereign cloud.

Maya: Is this a trend we're going to see accelerate across Europe?

Ryan: No question about it. And while we're talking about sovereignty, Abu Dhabi is doing something even more ambitious — but from the opposite direction, focused on export.

Maya: Abu Dhabi wanting to sell its AI government to the rest of the world.

Ryan: That's literally what's happening. Abu Dhabi has just launched TAMM Global, a new commercial entity whose entire mission is to license its AI government technology to other countries. TAMM is a platform that manages more than 1,200 public and private services for Abu Dhabi's 4.7 million residents. They've run this system at scale, they've proven it works, and now they want to sell it to other governments. It's a very clear pivot — from pilot demonstration to a globally exportable, commercial model of automated government.

Maya: It's almost like a brand-new market being born — turnkey AI government.

Ryan: Exactly. And if you want further proof that AI agents are genuinely weaving themselves into the fabric of the real economy, look at what's happening in fleet management with Samsara.

Maya: Right — trucking and vehicle fleets don't always grab the biggest headlines, but the numbers here are pretty eye-opening.

Ryan: Samsara, which provides technology solutions to fleet managers, has launched a platform called Agent Studio into general availability. It's a no-code platform that lets operational teams build voice and text AI agents to automate regulatory compliance, reporting, and driver support. And to give you a sense of just how fast adoption is moving, they shared an internal figure: AI agent usage on the Samsara platform has grown seven times over since June 2026. In four months.

Maya: Seven times over in four months — that's a jaw-dropping number. And to wrap up our industry tour, Huawei is making an announcement that goes to the infrastructure level itself.

Ryan: Yes, and it's a really interesting angle. Huawei unveiled what they're calling the Stellar AI Campus Network at HUAWEI CONNECT 2026. It's an enterprise network infrastructure specifically optimized for autonomous AI agents, featuring Wi-Fi 7 Advanced, built-in security, and network autonomy capabilities. Huawei's message is pretty direct: the network itself is a critical success factor for deploying autonomous AI agents in enterprise environments. This isn't just a question of models or software. If your network infrastructure can't keep up, your agents will be bottlenecked.

Maya: We'll have our wrap-up in just a moment, but Ryan, one more story before we get there — and it's about professional training.

Ryan: Right, Certiverse — which operates in the professional certification space — has just launched a self-service tier called Self-Starter. The headline promise is an 80 percent reduction in annual certification program costs compared to traditional providers. The underlying idea is that the speed at which AI-related skills are evolving is outpacing the ability of traditional certification systems to keep up. By making agentic certification accessible to organizations that don't have enterprise-level budgets, Certiverse is directly addressing that gap between the velocity of technological change and the slowness of legacy credentialing frameworks.

Maya: What a day it's been. And what really stands out is this historic convergence. In a single day, the FSB in finance, the AMA in healthcare, ServiceNow in enterprise — all of them dropping governance frameworks for autonomous AI agents. That's not a coincidence anymore, that's a signal. The real world is writing the rules because autonomous AI agents are already out there in production, with real stakes on the line. And meanwhile, critical security vulnerabilities are reminding us that governance and technical security have to move at exactly the same pace.

Ryan: And what strikes me most is the sheer diversity of sectors in the mix today. Healthcare, finance, fleet logistics, government, network infrastructure. We are not talking about a niche technology reserved for big tech companies. Autonomous AI agents are redefining operations across wildly different industries, each with its own constraints and its own pace of adoption.
