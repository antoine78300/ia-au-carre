Maya: Love it. Let's dive in with our big discovery of the week, and I have to say, this one genuinely caught me off guard.

Maya: Alright Ryan, let's talk music. ElevenLabs, a company most of us know for its AI-generated voices, just dropped Music v2.5. And apparently this is a really big deal. So what's actually different here?

Ryan: So ElevenLabs is the company behind those synthetic voices that are so realistic they can straight up fool you. Now they're coming for music, and version 2.5 is not just a minor patch or a cosmetic update. They did something pretty clever to validate it: a blind listening test. Not with ten people, not a hundred, but nearly forty-eight thousand listening pairs. And under those conditions, users consistently preferred Music v2.5 over the competition.

Maya: Forty-eight thousand listening pairs, that's massive. But hold on, what does a blind test on AI-generated music actually look like? Like you hear two tracks and just pick the one you like better?

Ryan: Exactly. You get two clips, you have no idea which is which, and you say which one sounds better to you. That's the gold standard method the music industry has used forever to evaluate audio quality. And the fact that ElevenLabs is playing by those rules is really the headline here. They're not saying hey look at our cool tool anymore, they're saying we're competing on the industry's own terms, and we're winning.

Maya: And they're also adding lossless downloads, right?

Ryan: Yeah, and that technical detail is actually more significant than it might sound at first. Lossless audio is the highest quality you can get, no compression artifacts, no degradation. Up until now, generative audio tools were mostly outputting compressed formats, which was fine for experimenting and goofing around but not good enough to deliver to a client or drop into a professional production pipeline. This changes that. AI-generated music is now knocking on the door of real studio workflows, ad agencies, serious content creators.

Maya: So we're moving from this is fun to mess around with to this is actually usable in a real production environment?

Ryan: That's exactly it. And it's kind of a symbolic turning point. For years, generative audio was the neglected middle child of creative AI. You had images with Midjourney, you had text with the big language models, and then music kind of lagging behind, often sounding weird, full of artifacts, not really ready for prime time. What we're seeing now with something like Music v2.5 suggests that era is ending.

Maya: Does this worry working musicians?

Ryan: That's the real question, and honestly the reactions are all over the map. Some musicians see it as just another tool, kind of like the drum machine or the synthesizer back in the eighties, which terrified people at the time and then ended up spawning entirely new genres. Others are worried about the bread-and-butter gigs, background music for podcasts, show intros, ad jingles, all the stuff that's fairly standardized. That's where the impact is going to hit first.

Maya: Right. And we won't go too deep on music today because we've got a lot to cover, but it is fascinating that ElevenLabs is the one pulling this off, a company that started out doing voices.

Ryan: Yeah, and that tells you something important about where this technology is converging. Voice, music, sound in general, it's all becoming one unified playing field. The logical next step is tools that handle everything in an integrated way, lyrics, melody, arrangement, mixing. We're almost there.

Maya: Okay, let's move to our second big story today, and this one is about robots. A lot of robots. Ryan, the Shanghai conference this week was a pretty huge moment.

Ryan: Really huge. The Shanghai Inclusion Conference is basically the Davos of Chinese tech, except robots are literally roaming the hallways. This year, more than forty companies specializing in what's called embodied robotics showed off their technology. And that word embodied is the key term here.

Maya: What exactly is embodied robotics? Because I keep hearing this phrase more and more.

Ryan: Great question. The idea behind embodied AI is that intelligence needs a physical form, it needs to interact with the real world rather than just processing data on a server somewhere. A chatbot is disembodied AI, it lives entirely in the digital world. A robot that can open a door, pick up an object, paint a car, assemble a part, that's embodied AI. And that's where things get really interesting.

Maya: And at Shanghai we actually saw real demos of this stuff?

Ryan: Very real demos. Flexiv, for example, a company that specializes in robotic arms, showed off arms with built-in torque sensors. A torque sensor is basically what lets the robot feel resistance in whatever it's handling, kind of like our sense of touch. That's what allows it to do tasks that require precision and gentleness, not just brute force.

Maya: And there was also a Chinese company that did something pretty jaw-dropping on the funding side, right?

Ryan: Oh yes, Mifeng Technology. It's a startup incubated within the Zhiyuan Robotics ecosystem, and their approach is genuinely unique: they don't build robots, they build the data that teaches robots how to do things. They've deployed twenty thousand data-collection units out in the real world, they've raised hundreds of millions of yuan, and they hit unicorn status, meaning a one-billion-dollar valuation, in just three months of existence.

Maya: Three months. That's just staggering.

Ryan: That's the Chinese robotics market right now. Fierce competition and enormous amounts of capital moving fast. And what Mifeng really illustrates is something the experts kept hammering at in Shanghai: the real battle in embodied robotics is not actually about the robot itself.

Maya: Then where is it?

Ryan: It's in the data. Teaching a robot to do something in the physical world is incredibly hard. The real world is unpredictable. A human can pick up an object they've never seen before. A robot needs thousands of examples. So whoever controls the training data controls how well robots actually perform. It's a form of strategic leverage, the same way patents or raw materials were in other eras.

Maya: That's fascinating because when you think about it, the real power is in something invisible, in what the robot has learned, not in its arms or legs.

Ryan: Exactly. And that actually connects to what Jeff Dean is doing with his new startup Discovery Loop, which we're about to get into. The idea that data and the ability to use it to automate complex processes, that's where the next wave of economic value is going to come from.

Maya: Alright, we're heading into our third section, AI and society. And we've got two very different stories here. First up is Jeff Dean, the legendary former Google engineer, raising money for his new startup. And then we've got something a little darker. Which one do we start with?

Ryan: Let's start with Jeff Dean, it's more exciting, and we'll end on the heavier topic.

Maya: Smart call. Jeff Dean, for anyone who isn't familiar, who is this guy?

Ryan: He's one of the most respected figures in computer science, full stop. He spent decades at Google, where he contributed to some genuinely foundational work: the distributed systems that keep Google running at planetary scale, the early large-scale infrastructure for deep learning. He was chief scientist at Google DeepMind until last month. And now he's walking away from all of that to build Discovery Loop.

Maya: And what is Discovery Loop?

Ryan: The goal is to automate scientific discovery. Not just help scientists search through literature or draft papers, but actually automate the process itself: forming hypotheses, designing experiments, analyzing results, iterating. And this is not a one-off idea. Right now we're seeing a massive wave of AI-driven scientific output, more than eighty thousand papers in the natural sciences in 2025 alone, which is a twenty-six percent jump in a single year. Stanford, Princeton, Cambridge, ETH Zurich, they all have major programs in this space.

Maya: And the valuation they're targeting is fifty billion dollars. Is that... realistic?

Ryan: Honestly, six months ago I would have said that's crazy talk. But in the current environment for top-tier AI startups, it's less shocking than it used to be. Investors are betting on people as much as on products. And Jeff Dean might be the single most credible person on the planet to pull something like this off. That said, fifty billion dollars with no product in the market yet is still a pretty enormous bet.

Maya: Alright. Now the less cheerful story. The Anthropic report on attempts to use Claude to develop bioweapons. Because that phrase is genuinely alarming.

Ryan: Yeah, and we need to take this seriously because it is serious. Anthropic, the company behind Claude, published a report documenting real, actual attempts to use their model to get information about developing biological weapons. Not theoretical attempts, real ones.

Maya: And did Claude help these people?

Ryan: No, and that's exactly what the report documents. The safety systems detected and blocked those attempts. But the fact that they happened at all, and that experts like Dr. Ashish Jha from Harvard are expressing deep concern about it, tells us something important: large language models are being seen by some bad actors as potential tools to democratize access to dangerous knowledge.

Maya: What's the specific risk? Can a model actually help someone build a biological weapon?

Ryan: The risk is what's called the lowering of the barrier. Creating a biological weapon requires highly specialized scientific knowledge, and historically that's been a limiting factor in who could actually do it. The fear is that models that are very capable in biology and chemistry could provide information that was previously hard to access. Now the actual scale of that risk is genuinely debated among biosecurity experts, but it's a risk that has to be taken seriously.

Maya: We're also looking at the job market today, because there are some numbers out of the UK that are pretty striking.

Ryan: Right, the UK University Guide 2027 released statistics that really make you stop and think. The share of computer science graduates landing jobs as coders or programmers dropped from forty percent to twenty-eight percent in a single year. A twelve-point drop in twelve months. And it's the fastest-declining job category for new graduates.

Maya: That's pretty brutal. Are AI agents actually replacing junior developers?

Ryan: Partly, yes. What's happening is that code-generation tools have gotten good enough to automate a lot of the tasks that used to go to entry-level developers. Companies are hiring fewer juniors because they need fewer lines of code written by hand. That doesn't mean senior developers are going anywhere, not even close, but getting a foot in the door is getting a lot harder.

Maya: And that brings us perfectly to our what-if segment, Ryan.

Ryan: My favorite part of the show.

Maya: So here it is: what if the real disruption from AI isn't that it replaces experts, but that it eliminates the early-career experiences that allow people to become experts in the first place?

Ryan: Oh that is a great one. And I think it's one of the deepest tensions of this moment. Historically, to become a great developer, a great doctor, a great lawyer, you had to start by doing the repetitive, unglamorous work. Debugging simple code. Drafting basic contracts. Reading routine scans. That's where you build your intuition, your instincts, your deep understanding of how things actually work.

Maya: And if AI handles all of that for us, we never build those foundations.

Ryan: Exactly. You can imagine a generation of professionals who are great at directing AI agents without really understanding what those agents are doing under the hood. That works beautifully when everything is running smoothly. But when something unusual comes up, when the situation falls outside the model's experience, who actually has the intellectual resources to step in and handle it?

Maya: It's kind of like airline pilots who freeze up when the autopilot fails because they've lost the habit of flying manually.

Ryan: That is literally the exact same metaphor that human factors experts in aviation use. And it's fascinating because AI is probably going to force us to completely rethink how we design professional training. Do you maintain an old-school track to build the foundational skills, and only then layer in the AI tools? Or do you figure out entirely new ways to develop expert intuition without putting people through decades of repetitive tasks?

Maya: There's no easy answer to that.

Ryan: No, and honestly that's what makes it so compelling. We're in a moment where these questions are genuinely open. Universities are wrestling with them, companies are wrestling with them, governments are starting to. In ten years we'll know whether we made the right calls. Right now, we're all figuring it out as we go.

Maya: I guess that's both the privilege and the discomfort of living through this particular moment in history.

Ryan: Exactly. And it's why we make this show.
