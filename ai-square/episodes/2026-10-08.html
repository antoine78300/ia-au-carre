Maya: Welcome to AI Squared, the podcast that breaks down artificial intelligence for you every single day. It is Thursday, October 8th, 2026, and we have got a packed episode for you today: AI agents protecting kids online, cryptographic standards for auditing AI models, regulators stepping up around financial risk, and a whole lot more. Let's dive in.

Maya: Ryan, let's kick things off with a story that I think is going to hit close to home for a lot of our listeners, and it goes right to the heart of online safety. Meta announced new AI tools designed to detect ads that are secretly being used to funnel people toward child exploitation content. This is serious stuff. Walk us through what's actually going on here.

Ryan: Yeah, this is really the biggest story of the day, and it's worth understanding exactly how it works under the hood. What Meta has formalized is the detection of something they're calling "signposting." Basically, predators are running what look like completely normal, legitimate paid ads on Facebook and Instagram, and using those ads to quietly redirect victims to criminal content that's hosted somewhere else, outside the platform entirely.

Maya: So the danger isn't in the ad itself, it's in where the ad is pointing?

Ryan: Exactly. And that's precisely why traditional content moderation systems were missing it. The ad looks clean on the surface. There's nothing obviously illegal about it. What Meta has deployed is a system built on a large language model that can detect these sophisticated evasion patterns. But here's where it gets really interesting: there's a second layer on top of that, autonomous red-teaming agents.

Maya: Autonomous red-teaming. Break that down for us.

Ryan: So in cybersecurity, red-teaming is the practice of simulating attacks to stress-test your own defenses. What Meta is doing here is deploying AI agents to actually play the role of the bad actors, constantly probing their own detection systems, trying to anticipate new evasion tactics before real predators can use them. It's a multi-agent setup where one group of AIs is attacking and another is defending, and it never stops. What's new here is that this approach has been formally applied to fighting networks of predators who are exploiting paid advertising tools as a gateway to victims.

Maya: That's almost a mind-bending idea. You're turning the same mechanisms the bad guys use against them.

Ryan: That's exactly it. And it really illustrates a broader trend: threats evolve, and defenses have to evolve just as fast, ideally faster. The Meta story is a textbook example of how agentic AI is starting to be deployed in contexts with enormous ethical and legal stakes.

Maya: Alright, let's shift over to our tools and frameworks segment. We've got several significant announcements today. Ryan, where do you want to start?

Ryan: I'd start with something that connects directly to those same security concerns our listeners care about, but now from the enterprise angle. OpenMatter Network and HOL have released proposed standards for what they're calling verifiable AI compliance. And the core idea here is genuinely fascinating.

Maya: Tell us more.

Ryan: Imagine you need to prove that an AI agent is following the rules, the laws, the internal policies of a company, but without exposing the sensitive data that agent has been handling. That is exactly the problem this framework is designed to solve, and it does it through cryptographic verification.

Maya: So you can audit a model without actually seeing what it processed?

Ryan: Right. And that's a really big deal in sectors like healthcare or finance, where the data is confidential but compliance still has to be provable to a regulator. What this framework establishes is a decentralized, cryptographic audit layer that goes well beyond traditional government frameworks, which rely on centralized verification. It's answering the question: how do you trust an autonomous AI agent without asking it to show you everything?

Maya: It's kind of like a transparent safe, if I'm picturing it right. You can verify the lock works without seeing what's inside.

Ryan: That's a great way to put it. Now, staying on the topic of agents accessing enterprise data, Apollo GraphQL announced something really important in that space.

Maya: GraphOS Agent Services, right?

Ryan: Exactly. Apollo GraphQL launched a mediation layer that sits between AI agents and enterprise systems. And the problem they're solving is very concrete: an AI agent querying a database can, without any malicious intent, end up accessing data fields it was never explicitly supposed to look at. Fields that contain sensitive information.

Maya: So AI agents can exhibit unintended behaviors, not necessarily malicious, but still problematic.

Ryan: Yes, and this is a risk angle that anyone in our audience who cares about security needs to keep top of mind. What GraphOS Agent Services offers is field-level access control, and it does not rely on a language model making its own judgment call about what's allowed. The governance is baked into the infrastructure layer, not left up to the model to figure out.

Maya: That's a structural guardrail rather than a behavioral one. Now Dell also announced something along these same lines for enterprise AI agents?

Ryan: Yeah, Dell is expanding its AI data platform with three new capabilities: a unified semantic layer, an enterprise knowledge graph, and knowledge agents. The whole idea is to give AI agents a shared understanding of what a company's data actually means. Because right now, an agent can have access to data without really grasping what that data represents in a real business context.

Maya: It's kind of like handing the agent a map instead of just letting it wander around the server room with no sense of direction.

Ryan: Perfect analogy. The semantic layer is essentially a shared vocabulary between the agent and the company's systems. And Dell is building all of this directly into the infrastructure, which is a pretty different approach from solutions that handle this at the application level.

Maya: Okay, let's move into notable research. Two things caught our attention today. First, a massive investment in biology.

Ryan: Yes, and it's a remarkable international collaboration. Biohub, the US Department of Energy, and the National Institutes of Health are committing nearly 1.8 billion dollars to generate and make available AI-ready data in biology. The Department of Energy alone is putting in more than 500 million dollars over five years.

Maya: And the goal is predicting and treating disease?

Ryan: That's right. The aim is to build the foundational datasets that AI models in biology will actually be trained on. We're talking about high-quality, well-structured data that machine learning systems can work with directly. It's infrastructure that's largely invisible, but it's absolutely fundamental to the medicine of tomorrow.

Maya: Now the second research story is a bit less optimistic. FICO published a pretty alarming report on AI agent governance inside companies.

Ryan: Yeah, and this is a report worth reading carefully, especially for listeners who think a lot about risk. What FICO is documenting is a fundamental trust gap around the explainability of decisions made by AI agents. Virtually all organizations have started exploring agentic AI, but they're at very early stages. And there's a critical blind spot.

Maya: What kind of blind spot?

Ryan: The difference between describing how a model generally handles similar cases, versus explaining why it made a specific decision in a specific situation. Those are two completely different things, and companies are conflating them.

Maya: That's a real problem, especially in finance or insurance where every individual decision has to be justifiable.

Ryan: Absolutely. And this connects directly to the compliance and risk concerns our listeners bring up all the time. If an AI agent denies a loan or sets an insurance premium, you need to be able to explain that particular case, not just say "our model is right 94 percent of the time in general." The FICO report is putting a spotlight on a governance gap that is far from being filled.

Maya: Now let's get into industry impact. Two really important stories here. First, new regulation out of Singapore.

Ryan: Yes, the Monetary Authority of Singapore, the MAS, has released an AI risk management framework specifically for financial institutions. It covers the use of autonomous agents across four key areas: customer interactions, internal operational processes, decision-making, and risk management itself.

Maya: So AI agents that are managing other AI risks, in a sense.

Ryan: Exactly right. What's notable about Singapore's approach is that it's trying to provide regulatory clarity while still supporting innovation. This is not a framework designed to slow things down, it's a framework designed to channel things in the right direction. Singapore is sending a clear signal: we want to be competitive in financial AI, but we also want everyone playing by the same clearly defined rules.

Maya: Are other regulators moving in this direction too?

Ryan: It's a global trend. And for our listeners who care about AI-related financial risk, this is exactly the kind of framework you want to be tracking closely. Because what Singapore does today has a way of showing up in Europe and elsewhere within months.

Maya: The other big industry story today: Shopify and agentic e-commerce.

Ryan: Shopify launched Canvas, a design interface that lets merchants build their storefronts by working directly with an AI agent called Sidekick, either through a visual interface or just by having a conversation with it. And what makes this interesting is that it's not happening in isolation.

Maya: Amazon and TikTok are in the mix too?

Ryan: Exactly. Amazon is simultaneously rolling out an autonomous advertising agent, and TikTok is expanding its shopping capabilities with features like Buy Direct and a Shopping Assistant. The fact that all three of these announcements are dropping at the same time validates something important: agentic e-commerce is moving from experimental to mainstream. Merchants do not just have tools anymore, they have AI collaborators.

Maya: And we'll wrap up with a tool that hits close to home for accountants. Gusto and the MCP protocol.

Ryan: Yes, and this is a really interesting signal about where interfaces are heading. Gusto, a payroll management platform, has integrated the MCP protocol to let accountants interact with payroll data directly from their own personal AI assistant, rather than having to log into Gusto's own interface.

Gusto is still the system of record for data and transactions, but the interaction happens through the accountant's personal language model.
