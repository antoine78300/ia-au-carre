Maya: Welcome to AI Squared, your daily dose of artificial intelligence news. Today we are talking about robots on the Mercedes factory floor, hidden backdoors buried inside digital brains, and AI agents that cost a thousand times less than the competition. It is a packed show, so let us dive right in.

Ryan: Hey Maya, hey everyone. Yeah, today's lineup is seriously dense, and there are some announcements here that really deserve a closer look.

Maya: Let's kick things off with the big story of the day. Ryan, what jumped out at you first this morning?

Ryan: Two things happening at the same time that speak to each other in a way that is almost unsettling. On one side, Google DeepMind is rolling out AlphaProof Code, an agentic framework for formal verification. On the other side, researchers at Microsoft are showing that you only need to tweak fewer than ten neurons in a large language model to hide a dormant backdoor inside it. Taken together, these two stories are really asking the same fundamental question: can you actually trust code that an AI produces?

Maya: Let's start with AlphaProof Code. What exactly is it?

Ryan: AlphaProof Code is a system where AI agents not only generate code, but also produce formal mathematical proofs that certify that code is correct. Those proofs are then checked by an external tool called Lean, a formal verifier that is well respected in academic circles. The critical piece here is that the verification does not rely on trusting the model itself. It relies on an independent outside system that gives you a hard yes or no with no ambiguity.

Maya: And what did they actually test this on in the real world?

Ryan: On Signal, the encrypted messaging app. DeepMind demonstrated the system on the libsignal library, and the agents generated six point five million Lean proofs. That is a demonstration at massive scale. The whole idea is to show that agentic orchestration can produce verifiable software security guarantees, something that until now was almost entirely the domain of highly specialized human experts.

Maya: That is genuinely impressive. Now let's get into the Microsoft discovery, because that one is a lot darker.

Ryan: Yeah, and what makes it so alarming is how simple it is. Researchers at Microsoft, including Dimitris Papailiopoulos, showed that by modifying fewer than ten neurons in a large language model, you can implant a dormant backdoor. Dormant means it does nothing, it just sits there and waits. In their demonstration, it was programmed to trigger at a specific calendar event, midnight in 2027. At that moment, it executes malicious code using the agent's own permissions.

Maya: So the agent is doing its job normally for months, and then one morning it wakes up and starts attacking the very system it was supposed to be protecting?

Ryan: Exactly. And what makes this even worse is that unlike a classic attack where a human clicks on something or makes a mistake, here it is an AI agent doing the acting. And an AI agent can have very broad permissions: reading files, making network calls, interacting with other systems. So the potential impact is far greater than what a human execution would typically cause. And all of this from changing fewer than ten neurons. No need to retrain the entire model.

Maya: That is genuinely mind-bending. Let's move on to the new tools and frameworks making news today, because there are several to get through.

Ryan: Right, three announcements that are pretty different in spirit but all address the question of how you deploy and secure AI agents in production.

Maya: Let's start with Anthropic. They dropped some new models?

Ryan: Two new models, Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2. Both are specifically designed for agentic orchestration, meaning systems where multiple AIs are collaborating in parallel. The improvements are meaningful: thirty percent faster and twenty percent cheaper than version 5.1. The announced price is eight dollars per million tokens. And a key feature of Fable 5.2 is its ability to coordinate more than a thousand agents simultaneously.

Maya: A thousand agents at the same time. That is hard to even picture.

Ryan: That is exactly the kind of capability large enterprises need when they want to automate complex workflows at scale. And Mythos 5.2 has a very specific application in the news today, because Cisco has integrated it into their AI Defense platform for automated red teaming.

Maya: Red teaming, what does that actually mean in this context?

Ryan: Red teaming is essentially the practice of attacking a system to find its weaknesses before a bad actor does. Cisco is using Mythos to adversarially test its enterprise AI applications and agents at machine speed. The advantage is that a model like Mythos can generate sophisticated attacks far faster than a human team ever could. This solution is also paired with Splunk for observability and Cortex XDR for threat detection.

Maya: And the third tool announcement?

Ryan: This one might be the most surprising in terms of economic impact. It comes from Pomerado, a Y Combinator startup. Their idea is to convert any website into an API or a tool compatible with the MCP protocol, without needing a developer to have built an API in the first place.

Maya: Why does that matter so much for AI agents?

Ryan: Because right now, when an AI agent needs to interact with a website, it has two options. Either the site offers an API, which is far from universal, or the agent navigates the site visually through a browser the way a human would. That second approach is what tools like Astra Computer Use or Codex rely on, and it is slow and expensive. Pomerado benchmarked their approach across thirty business tasks and is reporting execution that is two times faster, with costs reduced anywhere from ten to a thousand times. Their average cost is zero point zero two dollars per task, compared to between one point six two and thirteen point six three dollars for competing approaches.

Maya: A thousand times cheaper. That number really makes you stop and think. Let's shift over to notable research today. Ryan, what is coming out on the science side?

Ryan: Two very different pieces of work. The first comes from TIER IV, a company focused on autonomous driving. They are announcing the development of end-to-end AI models for Autoware 2.0, their open-source autonomous vehicle platform. What is new here is the modular approach.

Maya: Modular in what sense?

Ryan: Up until now, a lot of autonomous driving models were monolithic. One model, one hardware setup. If you changed your sensors, you had to retrain or switch models entirely. TIER IV is proposing models that can adapt to different configurations: camera only, LiDAR, radar, with or without HD maps. That flexibility is a big deal for deploying these systems across mixed fleets where the hardware varies from one vehicle to the next. And all of it is open-source.

Maya: And the second research result?

Ryan: AlphaGenome 2, also announced by Google DeepMind. It is a model capable of predicting the effects of DNA variations on specific cell types. The goal is to understand how a genetic mutation will affect particular cells in the human body, which is fundamental for medical research.

Maya: That is a completely different domain from everything else we have talked about today, but AI keeps showing up everywhere in science.

Ryan: Absolutely. And it is further validation that the architectures developed for reasoning and coding can be adapted to wildly different scientific fields. AlphaGenome 2 builds on the legacy of AlphaFold, which transformed structural biology. DeepMind seems determined to work its way through every scientific discipline one by one.

Maya: Now let's talk industry impact, because today there are some concrete real-world deployment announcements.

Ryan: Yeah, and the most striking example is Gemini Robotics 2.5. DeepMind announced this new version of its embodied robotics model, with improvements in dexterity, reliability, and safety. And they demonstrated it in a very concrete setting: the production lines at Mercedes-Benz, with a robot called Apollo 2, developed by a company called Apptronik.

Maya: So an AI robot on an actual automotive factory floor, that is happening today?

Ryan: It is in operational demonstration, which means it is no longer a lab environment. This is a real production environment with real constraints. What is technically interesting is the agent-robot coordination in unstructured settings. On a factory floor, not every situation is anticipated in advance. A model like Gemini Robotics 2.5 has to be able to adapt to unexpected configurations on the fly.

Maya: And on the agent security side, there is also an announcement coming out of the Middle East.

Ryan: Right, Delinea presented at GITEX Global 2026 a governance platform for autonomous AI agents. Their approach relies on what are called runtime controls, meaning permissions that are verified at the exact moment an agent wants to take an action, not just when the agent is created or deployed. What stands out is that this solution is aligned with Saudi and Emirati cybersecurity regulatory standards. This is the first time we are seeing a documented AI agent governance platform built natively into regional regulatory requirements in the Middle East.

Maya: And that brings us back to ARTEX, the AI pentesting framework that Palo Alto published an analysis on today.

Ryan: Exactly. ARTEX is an open-source multi-agent pentesting tool that was released in mid-2026. Palo Alto Networks, through their Unit 42 team, published a technical analysis that confirms something important and honestly a little unsettling: ARTEX is actually being used as an autonomous attack tool in production. Not just in a lab. And Palo Alto explains why: it deploys quickly, requires minimal resources, and operates autonomously. It is a perfect illustration of the dual nature of red teaming tools. They are built for defense, but once they are public, they can just as easily be used to attack.

Maya: Before we wrap up, Google also launched something new today, the Gemini Decisions API. Can you give us a quick rundown?

Ryan: Yeah, this one is worth flagging even if we keep it brief. Up until now, Gemini models have been known for reasoning, coding, and multimodal generation. Gemini Decisions is different. It is a specialized model built exclusively for agentic orchestration, with one very specific goal: making fast, reliable decisions across heterogeneous data. This is the first Google API built explicitly for autonomous decision-making. And in a world where AI agents need to act quickly and accurately, this could become a pretty central building block.

Maya: And that brings us to the end of today's rundown. Ryan, if you had to sum up the day in one sentence?

Ryan: I would say today is a perfect illustration of the tension that defines agentic AI in 2026: we are building systems that are more and more powerful, more and more autonomous, more and more embedded in the real world, and at the same time we are discovering just how easily those systems can be manipulated or hijacked with very little effort. The race between capability and security has never been more wide open.

Maya: Ryan, thanks so much for breaking all of this down. And thank you all for listening all the way through. AI Squared will be back tomorrow with more from the world of artificial intelligence. See you then.
