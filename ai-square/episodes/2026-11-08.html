Emma: Good morning and welcome to AI Squared, your daily dose of artificial intelligence! It is Saturday, we are keeping things relaxed, the coffee is hot, and we still have a ton of fascinating stuff to dig into. Liam and I have been going through the week's news so you don't have to, and trust me, there is plenty to talk about. Let's get into it!

Liam: Hey Emma, hey everyone! The weekend team is here and ready to break it all down.

Emma: So Liam, we are kicking things off with something that pretty much everyone can relate to because it touches something we all have in our ears every single day: music streaming. Spotify just announced that they pulled a staggering 75 million spam tracks from their platform. And on top of that, 50 million AI-generated tracks have been sidelined from their recommendation algorithms.

Liam: Yeah, and when you just lay those numbers out like that, they are kind of mind-blowing. 75 million spam tracks is enormous. And the 50 million AI tracks, they are not deleted outright, they are just being deprioritized by the algorithms, meaning Spotify is no longer going to actively push them to listeners.

Emma: And what drove that decision? Was it simply that people were not listening to those tracks?

Liam: That is exactly it. The data is pretty clear: AI-generated music gets significantly fewer plays than music made by humans. So Spotify is not making some big principled stand for artists here, this is a business decision. Their users are voting with their ears.

Emma: What I find really fascinating about this is that it validates something a lot of us kind of felt but could never quite put into words: we hear the difference. Or at least, we feel something different.

Liam: Right, and composer Max Richter articulates this really well in an interview he gave to Bloomberg. He says that the flood of AI-generated tracks does not just pollute the sonic landscape, it also dilutes the global royalty pool. Here is how it works: every stream generates a tiny fraction of a cent that gets redistributed. When you multiply the number of tracks by hundreds of millions, that same royalty budget gets spread across a much bigger surface, and every human artist mechanically ends up earning less.

Emma: It is like cutting a pie into more and more slices.

Liam: Exactly. And Richter actually takes his analysis even further. He says that unlike the great musical innovations of the past, things like the rise of the symphony orchestra or the invention of new instruments, which came out of genuine creative needs, today's AI music is not driven by artistic vision. It is driven by the convergence of technology and capital. Which is a pretty striking distinction to draw.

Emma: And does he see that as a real threat to working musicians?

Liam: He raises the concern that music could end up becoming a hobby reserved for artists who have other sources of income, people who can afford to make music without needing to live off it. And that would represent a huge loss in the diversity of voices we actually get to hear.

Emma: Meanwhile, SoundCloud is heading in the completely opposite direction. They just partnered with ElevenLabs to let creators publish AI-generated music directly to the platform, without even needing to upload a file manually.

Liam: Yeah, and that is a perfect illustration of the tension running through the whole industry right now. On one side you have Spotify saying we are going to regulate and filter. On the other you have SoundCloud saying come on in, publish freely, it is free for three months if you are an Artist Pro subscriber. These are two completely different strategic bets on what listeners are actually going to want in the years ahead.

Emma: Alright, let's move on to something that really caught my attention this week, because it steps out of the world of screens and into the physical world, the factory floor. Liam, tell us about AIR.

Liam: So AIR is a humanoid robot that was unveiled on November 6th at a conference called Humanoid Europe. It was developed by a company called Embodied AI, which is a spin-out from EPFL, the Swiss Federal Institute of Technology in Lausanne.

Emma: And what sets it apart from the other humanoid robots we have been hearing about for the past few years?

Liam: Two main things. The first is physical safety. AIR is built with what they call compliant hardware, which means that mechanically, the robot is designed to absorb contact with a human without causing injury. It is meant to work side by side with human workers on the floor, not locked behind a safety cage.

Emma: And the second thing?

Liam: The second thing is what they call continuous on-site learning. Most industrial robots today are trained in simulators, then deployed in a factory and you just hope the transition goes smoothly. Except it often does not. Simulators cannot capture all the complexity of the real world: the lighting that shifts, parts that are not positioned exactly as expected, a floor that is slightly uneven.

Emma: The classic gap between theory and practice.

Liam: Right, and in the field that is known as the sim-to-real problem. What AIR does differently is that it learns directly from real manufacturing data, on the spot, without an engineer needing to step in manually and recalibrate it.

Emma: And in the context of factory work, is that actually a meaningful promise?

Liam: The context really matters here. Projections suggest there could be 1.9 million unfilled manufacturing jobs in the US by 2033. That is a labor shortage, not just a cost problem. So a robot that can learn on the job, adapt to the specific environment of a given factory, and not be a safety hazard for the humans working alongside it, that is a pretty concrete answer to a pretty concrete problem. Now, whether it performs as well on a real factory floor as it does in a conference demo, that is a whole other question.

Emma: It always is. Okay, let's get into our AI and Society segment, and we are staying in the creative space but shifting the angle. We have two really interesting perspectives to put up against each other. Starting with Hideo Kojima.

Liam: Yes, Hideo Kojima, the legendary Japanese video game creator, spoke out about generative AI in creative work this week. And what he says is refreshingly nuanced. He is not saying it is the end of everything, and he is not saying it is some glorious revolution either. He says generative AI is pretty good at replacing the execution of ideas, but not at coming up with original ones.

Emma: So the role of the human creator shifts?

Liam: He frames it this way: the human creator becomes more of an editor or a curator than a craftsperson. And he makes a pretty interesting prediction: within five to ten years, the fact that something was made entirely by hand by humans will itself become a marketing point. The same way handmade is a selling point in artisan goods, or organic is in food.

Emma: What is striking is that this connects directly to what Spotify is experiencing right now. People are already making that distinction, even if they cannot always explain why.

Liam: Exactly. And Kojima adds something useful: the debate is going to evolve. We will stop asking whether AI should be part of the creative process and start asking where in that process it belongs. Do you use it to write dialogue? To animate background characters? To generate environments? Those are very different questions with very different answers depending on the context.

Emma: And on a very different end of the creative spectrum, Anthropic has been making some pretty ambitious claims in the medical space, specifically around drug discovery. And the scientific community is pumping the brakes a little.

Liam: Yeah, and that caution is worth listening to. Anthropic is pushing forward on the promise of AI in drug discovery, potentially for cancer. But researchers like Andreas Bender, a professor in machine learning applied to medicine at Khalifa University, are pointing to a structural issue: as of today, not a single drug whose discovery was led primarily by AI has received full FDA approval.

Emma: Zero?

Liam: Zero full approvals. And the reason, as he explains it, is what he calls the bottleneck of human heterogeneity. Human beings are extraordinarily variable. Age, sex, genetics, the microbiome, drug interactions, co-existing conditions... Current AI models struggle to capture that variability across thousands of parameters all at once.

Emma: So the systems work beautifully in controlled lab settings, but when you get to real-world clinical trials...

Liam: The researchers cited in the article describe a systematic failure of that transition. That is not to say AI is useless in medicine, far from it. But there is a gap between marketing announcements and what the clinical data actually validates. And people like Bender think it is important to keep that distinction visible.

Emma: Which does not mean Anthropic is not doing interesting work in this space. They actually have a program called AI for Science that gives academic researchers access to their models.

Liam: Right, and we have a concrete example this week with Ziqi Xu, a computer science PhD student at Washington University in St. Louis, who was selected for that program. He is receiving significant API credits to access the Claude models over six months, with the goal of accelerating his research in life sciences and biology. It is a boots-on-the-ground approach: put the tools in researchers' hands and see what comes out of it.

Emma: And now, our favorite part of the weekend show: the What If. Liam, take us somewhere.

Liam: Happy to. So here is the question that has been rattling around in my head all week as I followed all this news about music and creativity. What if the real question is not whether AI can be creative, but rather what do we actually value in a piece of art?

Emma: Say more.

Liam: We saw that Spotify listeners are streaming fewer AI-generated tracks. Not necessarily because they know it is AI, but because something just does not grab them the same way. And we have Max Richter warning that music could become a pastime for the wealthy. And we have Kojima saying that handmade will become a marketing argument.

Emma: So running underneath all of this is a question about authenticity.

Liam: Exactly. What if what we value in a piece of art is not really the work itself, but the story behind it? The fact that a human being went through something, wanted to share it, spent years honing their craft to be able to do that? What if AI, by being able to produce anything effortlessly, is actually forcing us to get serious about what we mean by artistic value?

Emma: It is almost like we need the constraint for art to carry meaning.

Liam: Or at least for us to be able to assign meaning to it. It is a question that stays wide open, and I think we are going to be debating it for years to come.

Emma: Either way, it is a pretty great question to sit with over a Saturday morning cup of coffee.

Liam: Absolutely.

Emma: And that is a wrap on today's episode of AI Squared! Thank you so much for hanging out with us this Saturday. I hope we gave you some things to chew on for the rest of your day. We will be back tomorrow with a fresh batch of AI news, as sharp and up-to-date as we can make it. Take care of yourselves, and we will see you tomorrow!

Liam: See you tomorrow everyone!
