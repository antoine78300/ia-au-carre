AI Squared #100 — 08/11/2026

On the program: Hideo Kojima's insights on generative AI in creative industries, AI's impact on video game design, weekly AI discoveries.

Full transcript

Emma: Good morning and welcome to AI Squared, the show that shows up for you even on a Sunday! I'm Emma, and today I've got Liam here with me for our weekend edition — we're kicking back, digging into everything that happened this week in the world of AI. Coffee's hot, vibes are good, let's get into it!

Liam: Hey everyone! The weekend crew is in the house — relaxed, but trust me, no less curious. And honestly, this week gave us plenty to be curious about.

Emma: We're jumping straight into our discovery of the week, and it comes from a pretty unexpected place: the video game industry. Liam, you want to set the scene?

Liam: Absolutely. Hideo Kojima — the legendary game designer behind some of the most iconic titles in the medium — shared a genuinely fascinating take on where generative AI actually stands in creative industries right now. And what's striking is just how sharp and precise his read on the situation is.

Emma: What exactly is he saying?

Liam: He's saying we're still inside a five-to-ten-year window where human-made content is going to hold real commercial value. Meaning that in a few years, "this was created by an actual human" is going to become a full-on marketing argument in its own right.

Emma: Like the "handmade" label on a jar of artisan jam.

Liam: Exactly! And he takes it further. He sees AI fundamentally transforming what the human creator's role looks like. We're shifting from being the maker to being the reviewer, the supervisor. The question won't be "are we using AI" anymore — it'll be "at what point in the process."

Emma: That's a distinction I really like. Not if, but where.

Liam: And he gets really concrete about it. He identifies three areas where AI genuinely delivers: technical engineering work, repetitive productivity tasks, and early-stage ideation — your basic brainstorming. But where it still falls short is in the nuanced creative work: art direction, cinematography, music composition.

Emma: That's interesting because it lines up perfectly with what's been happening in the music world this week too, Liam. We've actually got two stories that are speaking directly to each other. On one side, South Korea trying to regulate AI-generated music, and on the other, a major lawsuit here in the US.

Liam: Right, and together they illustrate exactly the tension Kojima is talking about. In South Korea, the Ministry of Culture held a parliamentary forum specifically to try to clarify copyright rules around AI-generated music. And they drew a line that sounds simple on the surface but carries enormous implications.

Emma: What's the line?

Liam: If a human creates the lyrics or the melody, it's protected by copyright. If it's entirely generated by AI, it's not. And the gray area — which nobody has a clean answer for yet — is when a human takes AI-generated content and rearranges or edits it. That middle ground is still wide open.

Emma: And over here in the States, we've moved from policy forums to actual courtrooms. The Elvis Presley estate and Eminem's rights publisher have both filed suit against Suno.

Liam: Suno, for anyone who hasn't come across it, is an AI music generation platform. And the argument the plaintiffs' attorneys are making is particularly striking: they claim that Suno is mechanically encoding the artistic identity of these artists. That it can produce in a matter of seconds what took these artists years to master.

Emma: And they have specific examples to back that up?

Liam: They do. They point to generated tracks that imitate Elvis's vocal style, and others that replicate Eminem's lyrical patterns. This isn't an abstract complaint about a legal principle — there's real, concrete material behind it.

Emma: Which loops right back to Kojima's point. AI is exceptionally good at reproducing, imitating, recombining. But that raises a genuinely serious ethical question about what we're doing with people's artistic identities.

Liam: And a legal one. Because right now we are literally building case law in real time.

Emma: Alright, let's move into our second big theme of the day, and here we're stepping into the world of robots. And not cute little robots, Liam.

Liam: No, not at all. This was a big week for humanoid robots across the board. Multiple stories all pointing in the same direction: physical robots are moving from the demo stage to actual real-world deployment.

Emma: Let's start with the most dramatic one.

Liam: AGIBOT, a robotics company, did something pretty wild. They sent six humanoid robots to run a store for twenty-four hours straight. These robots were coordinated by an AI system they call Genie Master.

Emma: Twenty-four hours, a store staffed entirely by robots. Did it work?

Liam: Partly. The detail I find most interesting in this whole story is how they handled the failures. Two of the six robots didn't pass their evaluation. And rather than burying that, they explain that both the successes and the failures get converted into training data to improve future behavior.

Emma: So the mistakes actually serve the progress. That's almost philosophical.

Liam: It's also just really smart engineering. And along those same lines, UBTECH has launched a platform called Thinker-Sim, which is built for exactly that purpose: simulating real-world environments, generating synthetic training data, and evaluating robot capabilities inside a unified pipeline. The whole idea is that you no longer need to send a robot into an actual store to learn the basics.

Emma: You learn in the simulation before you ever touch the real world. Like a flight simulator.

Liam: Exactly. And UBTECH is also the company that co-developed, alongside Xinhua — China's state news agency — two humanoid robot TV news anchors named Xinyu and Xinyan.

Emma: Robot journalists. I might need to start watching my back.

Liam: What's worth noting here is that the visual design of these robots was overseen by director Zhang Yimou, specifically to make sure they meet the conventions of broadcast journalism. And we're talking about operational deployment inside Chinese state media — not a lab demonstration.

Emma: And on the financial side, robots are also moving the markets this week?

Liam: Big time. Unitree Robotics, one of the major players in the space, saw its stock jump fifty percent in a single month. The reason: founder Wang Xingxing pulled his timeline forward on a physical AI breakthrough. He's now saying one to three years, where before he was much more vague.

Emma: That's exactly the kind of statement that moves markets.

Liam: And analysts are documenting three bottlenecks that are still holding the sector back. First is what they call the "brain" — the robot's reasoning capability. Second is the hands — fine motor dexterity. And third is mass manufacturing. Actually producing humanoid robots at scale is still extremely complicated.

Emma: And where does Apple fit into all of this? Because we've got a pretty surprising piece of news about them.

Liam: We do, and this is our high-importance story of the day on the robotics side. Apple is reportedly conducting early-stage research on a system they're calling HALO. And the concept behind it is fascinating.

Emma: What is HALO?

Liam: It's a self-evolution system for robots. The idea is that the robot's AI could independently search academic papers, generate its own control code, test it in simulation, and if it passes validation, deploy it onto actual physical machines.

Emma: So the robot improves itself by reading scientific research.

Liam: That's the vision. The phrase going around is that robots "move with their brain" — they carry their learning capacity with them wherever they go. This is still at a preliminary research stage, Apple is calling it research and not an imminent product, but it's a very strong signal about where they're planting their flag.

Emma: And that leads us perfectly into our closing segment, the "What if" question. Liam, you want to kick it off?

Liam: Happy to. What if the real challenge of the next few years in AI isn't about how powerful the models get — but about trust?

Emma: Say more.

Liam: This week we had lawsuits over music being used to train AI systems, questions about who owns what in AI-assisted creation, robots failing their evaluations and learning from those failures, and Apple working on systems that improve themselves. The through line in all of it is: who do we trust, and on what basis?

Emma: Trust in the content, trust in the rights, trust in how robots behave...

Liam: And South Korea is trying to answer that through regulation. The US lawsuits are trying to answer it through case law. The engineers at AGIBOT are answering it by making failures transparent and useful. Everyone is building their own version of an answer.

Emma: And Kojima would say that humans will answer it by valuing their own creative signature more than ever before.

Liam: That might actually be the most interesting flip of all. AI becoming ubiquitous paradoxically makes "made by a human" more valuable, not less.

Emma: And on that thought — a pretty perfect one to close out a Sunday with — we're going to wrap up this virtual coffee we've been sharing with you all. Thanks so much for spending part of your morning with us, and Liam, great stuff as always.

Liam: My pleasure — and seriously, thank you all for tuning in, even on a Sunday!

Emma: We'll be back tomorrow with a brand new episode of AI Squared. Until then, stay curious, stay human, and we'll see you tomorrow!
