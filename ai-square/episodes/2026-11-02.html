Maya: Welcome to AI Squared, the podcast that breaks down artificial intelligence in your everyday world. Today is Monday, November 2nd, 2026, and honestly, the news hitting us this morning is a lot to take in: AI agents hacking autonomously, tech giants actually agreeing on something for once, and a question that keeps getting louder by the day — who is really in control of all these agents we're deploying everywhere? Buckle up, let's get into it.

Maya: Ryan, let's kick things off with what stopped me cold this morning while I was prepping the show. There's been a breach at Chainalysis, the blockchain analytics firm, and this was no ordinary attack. From what's being reported, it was entirely agentic. Can you break down what that actually means?

Ryan: Absolutely. What researchers at Sysdig documented, and what Shattered.io picked up on, is something genuinely new in terms of how it was executed. Autonomous AI agents were used to exfiltrate data from Chainalysis — we're talking four specific data fields. And what makes this different from anything we've seen before is that these agents operated without continuous human involvement. They identified the target, found the access vector, and pulled the data. That's what an agentic attack looks like.

Maya: And this isn't a one-off situation, right?

Ryan: Not at all. Back in July 2026 we saw the first fully agentic ransomware attack on record. And more recently, a group calling themselves CyberStrikeAI coordinated 44 AI agents to automate hacking operations against targets in Japan. That number is worth sitting with: 44 agents working in coordination. We're not talking about a tool in a human hacker's hands anymore. We're talking about a fleet of agents collaborating to run an attack from start to finish.

Maya: That completely changes the threat surface, doesn't it? The speed, the scale...

Ryan: Exactly. The execution speed is in a completely different league compared to a traditional human-led attack. And more importantly, it raises a fundamental question that researchers are starting to document seriously: are our current defenses even designed for this kind of threat? And the answer coming back is pretty clearly no. Madhu Shashanka from Concentric AI puts it really well in a recent analysis. He says behavioral guardrails and prompt injection detection just aren't enough. What actually matters is controlling which data, which systems, and which credentials an agent can reach in the first place. The real security perimeter is access, not model output.

Maya: So we're watching what the agent says or does, when we should be restricting what it can touch to begin with.

Ryan: That's it exactly. It's a complete paradigm shift, and it's urgent. Because while we're debating guardrails, attackers are already running fleets of agents in production against critical infrastructure.

Maya: We're going to come back to agent security when we dig into frameworks in a bit, because there's a ton to unpack there. But first, let's talk tools and new systems. Ryan, there are two announcements that feel really significant when it comes to agent identity and how we govern agents inside enterprise environments. First up: World ID for Agents. What is that exactly?

Ryan: So World is the biometric identity network backed by Sam Altman. They just launched World ID for Agents, and it's a first for the industry. The idea is this: a verified user can delegate a cryptographic proof to an autonomous AI agent. That agent can then carry out financial instructions — payments, transactions — without ever exposing the user's identity.

Maya: So the agent acts on your behalf, but nobody else knows you're the one who gave the order.

Ryan: Exactly. And this is the first commercial infrastructure built specifically for delegating authority to agents in a financial context. Up until now, when people talked about autonomous agents executing transactions, the identity question was always kind of fuzzy. Now you have a formal cryptographic system that says: this agent is authorized by this verified person, and here's the proof.

Maya: That's elegant, but it also raises some serious liability questions, doesn't it? What if the agent makes a mistake, or worse, what if someone steals the cryptographic proof?

Ryan: One hundred percent, and that's precisely why this is something to watch closely. It's an important first piece of serious infrastructure. But the second tool announcement you mentioned might actually be more immediately actionable for enterprise teams right now: Oracle and what they're proposing around the MCP protocol.

Maya: Right, walk us through that. MCP, for anyone who hasn't heard the term?

Ryan: MCP stands for Model Context Protocol. It's a standard that lets AI agents use tools exposed as structured interfaces. What Oracle is proposing is pretty concrete: instead of letting an LLM agent generate SQL on the fly to query your databases — which can produce queries that are wrong, expensive, or just outright dangerous — you expose pre-curated reports as MCP tools. The agent picks from those validated reports rather than inventing its own queries.

Maya: And you can actually shut off ad hoc SQL generation entirely?

Ryan: Exactly. The MCP server can block free SQL execution. The agent can only choose from what you've already put in front of it. It's a containment-by-design approach rather than a monitor-and-catch approach. And it cuts down on both errors and token costs, since the queries are optimized ahead of time. This actually connects directly back to what Shashanka was saying: control access rather than surveil behavior.

Maya: Cisco also dropped something in open source around detecting agentic threats?

Ryan: Yeah, Cisco's Project Quarry, announced at the end of October. It's a neurosymbolic language designed to detect threatening behaviors in AI agents. What's interesting is that security researchers are publishing complementary tools in parallel, like BLA — which stands for Blind LLM Agents — designed to protect binaries against reverse engineering by AI agents. You're seeing an entire defensive ecosystem take shape that's specifically focused on the agentic threat. Two years ago, none of this existed.

Maya: Alright, let's shift to research. Ryan, there was an angle this morning that I found really striking: power grids and AI agents. An expert at Iowa State University documented something pretty counterintuitive.

Ryan: This is a perfect example of the problem autonomous agents create in environments where a single mistake can have massive physical consequences. This energy security expert documented the following scenario: an agentic system authorized to adjust settings at an electrical substation to prevent an outage could, through its own actions, actually trigger the exact outage it was supposed to prevent.

Maya: That's kind of mind-bending. The agent creates the problem by trying to solve it.

Ryan: And it happens precisely because a power grid is a dynamic, deeply interconnected environment. A local adjustment can set off a cascade of effects the model didn't correctly anticipate. The researcher's conclusion is clear: in critical operational environments, you need mandatory human approval gates. The agent can analyze, recommend, and prepare the action, but a human has to sign off before anything actually executes.

Maya: That's not an anti-AI position, that's a pro-safety position.

Ryan: Exactly. And it tracks with what's emerging across the whole industry right now. Nobody is questioning whether agents are useful. The conversation is about which contexts allow for genuine autonomy and which ones require keeping a human in the loop.

Maya: There's also some really concrete research out of a specific application domain, weather forecasting in particular. Envision's Tianji model just won something significant?

Ryan: Yes, a major operational validation. Tianji, Envision's meteorological foundation model, just won the AI Weather Quest competition run by the ECMWF — the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts, which is basically the gold standard in the field. It outperformed 20 teams across all metrics. What's notable is the approach: Tianji combines computational physics with AI. It's not a purely statistical model that ignores the laws of atmospheric physics. It's a genuine integration of the two.

Maya: This is a good example of what people call domain-specific AI. More effective than a generalist model on a focused problem?

Ryan: That's exactly the debate. And this win validates the hypothesis that for complex scientific applications, a specialized foundation model trained with the right domain-specific inductive biases beats the generalists. It's a trend worth watching in other fields too, like drug discovery.

Maya: Speaking of which, let's get into industry. There are several strong signals today about how different players — governments, major enterprises, the financial sector — are actually deploying these technologies in the real world. Ryan, let's start with India, because the numbers are striking.

Ryan: India announced the deployment of 20 agentic solutions in live government production in 2026, with funding of 1,058 crore rupees, heavily allocated to the BharatGen Param2 project. What's strategically interesting is the announced shift in how future funding will work: instead of funding the release of new models, they're moving to funding measured outcomes — how many real users are actually using these systems, how many services are genuinely operational.

Maya: That's a very pragmatic turn. Stop funding basic research, start funding real-world impact.

Ryan: And it's a lesson a lot of other governments are going to have to absorb. What's also worth noting is that government workloads are handled mostly by small and mid-sized models — Param2, quantized variants of Sarvam — not by massive frontier models. Which reinforces another underlying trend: the biggest frontier models aren't necessarily the best fit for operational use cases.

Maya: That connects to an analysis published under the pretty provocative headline "the week AI stopped chasing the smartest model." Can you tell us more about that?

Ryan: It's a synthesis of three late-October announcements that together point to a real strategic pivot. First, OpenAI significantly cut the prices on its frontier models — GPT-6 is now at one dollar per million input tokens and five dollars per million output tokens. Second, Google restricted Gemini 4 Carbon to defenders only, with no public availability date. Third, GitHub now lets developers fully delegate the choice of model to the infrastructure, so they don't have to think about it at all.

Maya: So on one side you're democratizing access through pricing, on the other you're restricting the most powerful models, and you're abstracting the choice away from developers entirely.

Ryan: Exactly. The implicit message from the industry is that the race to build the smartest model is starting to give way to questions of governance, controlled access, and distribution. And that's where the other major industry story of the day comes in: the unified robots.txt standard.

Maya: That one genuinely surprised me. OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, and Anthropic actually agreeing on something — that doesn't happen every day.

Ryan: And yet here we are. This was announced on October 30th. It's a shared robots.txt standard that lets publishers block their data from being used to train generative models with a single instruction. Anthropic is already implementing it. Microsoft is targeting February 2027, and Google is following sometime in 2027. This is the first time all four of those players have aligned on a common standard for respecting publisher opt-outs.

Maya: For publishers, this is a concrete step forward. For the industry, it's a signal that regulatory and legal pressure is starting to actually move the needle.

Ryan: Without question. And it opens the door to a much bigger conversation about the conditions under which data can legitimately flow into model training.

Maya: And to wrap up the industry section, there's this fascinating gap in finance: the suppliers are ready, but the customers aren't so sure.

Ryan: A Signicat study lays it out really clearly: 79 percent of European financial services providers say they're ready to deploy agentic AI for digital onboarding. Meanwhile, 60 percent of European consumers abandon online financial applications. So you've got a sector that has invested heavily in the infrastructure, that is technically ready to go, but facing users who just don't trust it yet. And that's a problem you can't engineer your way out of. It's a perception problem, a user experience problem, and probably a transparency problem around what exactly these agents are doing on people's behalf.

Maya: And that brings us full circle back to where we started this morning: agents are doing more and more powerful things, more and more autonomously, and trust — whether we're talking about end users, regulators, or the targets of potential attacks — is the real bottleneck.

Ryan: Exactly. And the responses taking shape today — cryptographic identity with World ID for Agents, shared standards like robots.txt, human approval gates in critical infrastructure, security built around access control rather than behavioral surveillance — all of these are attempts to build that trust on a solid foundation.

Maya: Which brings us to the takeaway for today's episode. What we'll remember about November 2nd, 2026 is a day defined by one central paradox: AI agents are powerful enough to breach infrastructure, capable enough to out-forecast every weather model on the planet, integrated enough to handle financial transactions in your name — and yet the most fundamental questions about how to secure them, how to trust them, and how to govern them are still very much open. The industry is no longer in the "does this actually work" phase. It's in the "how do we do this right" phase. And honestly? That's the hardest phase of all. Ryan, thank you so much for all of this, great stuff as always.

Ryan: Thanks Maya. And thanks to everyone listening. This is a genuinely fascinating moment to be paying attention.

Maya: That's a wrap on today's episode of AI Squared. See you tomorrow.
