Maya: Welcome to AI Squared, your daily breakdown of everything happening in artificial intelligence. It's Friday, November 27th, 2026, and agentic AI is everywhere right now — on factory floors, inside your productivity apps, behind the wheel of autonomous vehicles. But there's still a pretty significant gap between what's being promised and what's actually being delivered. Let's dig into it.

Ryan: Hey Maya.

Maya: Hey Ryan. So let's kick things off with the biggest story of the day, and honestly one that really caught my attention: Microsoft held its Ignite 2026 conference, and the announcements were massive. What's the takeaway?

Ryan: What really stands out about Microsoft Ignite 2026 is how consistent the message is. Microsoft is positioning Copilot as a full-blown business operating system. This is no longer just an assistant — it's a central orchestration layer for the enterprise. And the concrete announcements are in general availability, meaning this is real deployment, not vaporware.

Maya: So what exactly is available right now?

Ryan: Three big things. First, Agent 365 with a tool registry now in general availability — that's what allows agents to know which tools they can actually use inside a Microsoft environment. Second, Microsoft Perception, with a Triage Agent moving into general availability. And the third one, which I find particularly interesting from a cybersecurity standpoint: Defender runtime protection for local AI agents running on Windows.

Maya: And we're talking about securing the agents themselves here?

Ryan: Exactly. And this is new territory. Microsoft is putting local governance in place for agents — devices running unapproved agents get flagged as non-compliant. And there's a system they're calling Dynamic Agent Risk, which pulls together real-time signals — runtime behavior, the security posture of the device, and what they call the blast radius, meaning how much damage could potentially be done if an agent gets compromised — and uses all of that to assign an individual risk level to each agent.

Maya: That's a huge deal for Fortune 500 companies. We're talking about real enterprise-grade governance tools, not some lab demo.

Ryan: Exactly. And it addresses a genuine concern: up until now, companies were deploying agents without really knowing what they were doing in the background. Now you have an end-to-end control chain.

Maya: Alright, let's move on to new frameworks and tools. And today we've got two major stories on the Google side. Ryan, walk us through what Google has put in place.

Ryan: Google has formalized something that's going to be pretty foundational for any company using Workspace — so Gmail, Drive, Docs, Sheets, Calendar, Chat, Tasks. Going forward, any AI agent that wants to access those services has to go through one of two entry points: either Google's proprietary MCP servers, or the Google Cloud Agent Gateway, which is a native protocol built for traffic between LLMs, MCP, and what are called A2A protocols — that's agent-to-agent.

Maya: MCP, A2A — these acronyms keep coming up. Let's make sure everyone's on the same page.

Ryan: Sure. MCP, or Model Context Protocol, is a standard that lets an agent connect to external tools in a structured way. A2A is the protocol that lets agents talk to each other, hand off tasks to one another. What Google is doing is making those connections mandatory and controlled. Every agent has to register with a central registry, IAM access policies apply, there are rules blocking unauthorized outbound traffic, and there's a content filtering layer they call Model Armor.

Maya: And does this work even if a company isn't running on Google Cloud?

Ryan: That's the detail that changes everything. This infrastructure is available cross-cloud — on AWS EKS, Azure AKS, and OpenShift. So a company can have its agents running in whatever cloud environment they want, and still access Workspace securely through Google's Agent Gateway.

Maya: So Microsoft is locking down agents on the Windows and local side, Google is locking down agents on the cloud data access side. We are literally watching the foundations of a real agentic AI governance infrastructure being built in real time.

Ryan: That's exactly right. And there's a third tool story worth mentioning here: LiteLLM just released version 1.108.19 as an LTS release — meaning a long-term support version. What's notable is the native integration of the MCP Gateway and Agent Gateway directly into the proxy, which enables unified routing across multiple models and agents. They also patched a critical billing bug where agent calls were being billed at zero or at a competitor's rate instead of the correct price. Not a small thing when you're running thousands of calls a day.

Maya: Yeah, a billing bug at that scale can get out of hand really fast. Now let's talk research. We've got two very different angles today. First up, a large-scale study published by Wipro.

Ryan: Wipro analyzed 72 companies across 7 industry sectors — 580 documented use cases in total. The headline number is that 61 percent of companies are already deploying agentic AI in production. Not a pilot, not an experiment — actual production deployment.

Maya: That sounds like a staggering number. But I'm sensing a "but" coming.

Ryan: Because the real finding in this study is what comes right after that stat. Enterprise-wide autonomy is still rare. And Wipro identifies a very clear structural bottleneck: technological maturity is moving way faster than governance, data quality, the ability to actually measure the value being created, and the operational discipline needed to scale responsibly.

Maya: So companies are deploying, but they don't really know what they're getting out of it, or how to keep it under control at scale.

Ryan: That's the paradox of the moment. The tools are mature. The organizations running them? Not quite yet.

Maya: And then there's a second research piece that's really worth unpacking: a comparative analysis of the six major agent orchestration frameworks. What does it tell us?

Ryan: It's a breakdown comparing Microsoft Agent Framework, Google ADK, LangGraph, CrewAI, and the SDKs from OpenAI and Anthropic. And the conclusion is a little counterintuitive: despite all the different terminology and vocabulary each one uses, every single one of these frameworks is actually answering the same six core architectural questions. How do you control execution flow? How do you persist an agent's state between steps? How do you pause and resume so a human can step in? How do you hand off between agents? How do you integrate tools via MCP or A2A? And how do you maintain a full audit trail of what happened?

Maya: So underneath all the apparent fragmentation, there's actually a deep convergence happening.

Ryan: Exactly. And that matters a lot for technical decision-makers: choosing a framework isn't really a fundamental architectural choice — it's usually an organizational one. Which team knows what, which ecosystem the company is already invested in.

Maya: Let's shift to industry impact, because there's a lot going on here. Let's start with Emerson and their AspenTech AVA system.

Ryan: Emerson is a giant in industrial automation. And on December 1st, they're officially rolling out native agentic capabilities inside their AspenTech AVA platform. What's interesting is the architecture: they're combining state-of-the-art LLMs with deep industrial domain expertise — years of encoded operational knowledge — plus each customer's proprietary data. That allows the agents to orchestrate complex industrial engineering tasks end to end.

Maya: This is what a mainstream production deployment looks like — not a prototype.

Ryan: Right, and it puts Emerson among the first to go truly live with agentic frameworks in heavy industry. That's significant because industrial sectors have historically been very conservative when it comes to adopting new technology.

Maya: Second industry story: XPENG, the Chinese automaker, announced something called WorldGPT. And this one really pushes the boundaries.

Ryan: WorldGPT is a physical AI model — sometimes called Physical AI. XPENG announced it on November 24th. This system can learn from six different types of machines: autonomous cars, boats, drones, single and dual-arm robots, and humanoid robots. And critically, it can transfer skills from one type of machine to another.

Maya: How does that actually work in practice?

Ryan: The example they give is pretty compelling: navigation techniques learned by a self-driving car can be reused to improve the grasping ability of a robotic arm. That's not a metaphor — it's an actual transfer of internal representations across completely different physical forms. The system combines visual understanding, reasoning, prediction, and action planning in a unified architecture. It's comparable to what Nvidia has built with GR00T 2 for humanoid robots.

Maya: And third on the industry side: the battle over standards for agents in retail and banking. Because this one's essentially a stalemate.

Ryan: A stalemate that's been going on for a year. Finextra documents that the major LLM providers — OpenAI, Anthropic, Google — have tried to coordinate on building secure agentic orchestration for commerce and banking. But no universal standard is anywhere close to being agreed upon. And Walmart is already testing an Agent Gateway running multiple models simultaneously — Muse, ChatGPT, Google — which kind of illustrates just how fragmented things are.

Maya: Why is it so hard to get alignment in those sectors specifically?

Ryan: Because the security and regulatory compliance requirements in finance and retail are so much more demanding. Every player has its own needs, its own legacy systems, its own regulators to answer to. And on top of that, every major model provider has a vested interest in making sure their own standard is the one that wins out.

Maya: And there's one more story I really don't want to let slide: Reliath AI announcing PROPAI, an architecture that's fundamentally different from LLMs. What's the deal?

Ryan: Reliath AI, a US-based company, announced on November 26th the deployment of PROPAI — which stands for Propositional Reasoning AI. The concept is radically different: instead of predicting the next token the way traditional LLMs do, PROPAI reasons propositionally over organizational documents. Concretely, the model builds a logical representation of the relationships between facts in a document, rather than processing them statistically.

Maya: And what are the actual use cases?

Ryan: Two applications are in general availability: ReliathQ for querying a proprietary knowledge base, and Reliath Harness, a governed runtime for AI agents. The positioning is clear: they're going after critical sectors like pharma and energy, with dramatically lower computational overhead compared to frontier large models. Less resources, lower costs, and tighter governance. It's still early days to know whether the propositional approach will hold up at scale, but it is a genuinely different conceptual alternative.

Maya: To wrap up today's episode, what strikes me is that everything we covered points to the same fundamental question: how do you govern agents that are becoming increasingly autonomous? Microsoft with Defender, Google with its Agent Gateway, Wipro documenting how far organizations still have to go — everyone is wrestling with the same problem.

Ryan: The agentic framework market is projected to grow from 2.99 billion dollars in 2025 to 19.32 billion by 2031 — a compound annual growth rate of 36 percent. This is not a niche trend. It's a structural transformation in how companies run their entire technology stack. And the governance question isn't some technical footnote — it's the defining challenge of the next decade.

Maya: Ryan, thanks so much for all the analysis today. And thank you to everyone listening all the way to the end. AI Squared will be back tomorrow with all the latest from the world of artificial intelligence. See you tomorrow.
