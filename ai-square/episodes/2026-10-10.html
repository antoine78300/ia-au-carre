Maya: Good morning and welcome to AI Squared! It is Saturday, the sun is out — fingers crossed, anyway — and Ryan and I have settled in with our coffee to walk you through everything that went down this week in the world of artificial intelligence. And trust me, there was some genuinely fascinating stuff. So get comfortable, let's dive in!

Ryan: Hey Maya, hey everyone! Great to be here. This is one of those weeks where it really feels like the future just kicked things up another gear.

Maya: Exactly! And we are starting strong, because our discovery of the week comes all the way from Shanghai. Ryan, tell us what happened on stage back on September 11th.

Ryan: So we are talking about Yuri. Yuri is the first AI avatar to be officially certified in China, under the category of idol artists. And on September 11th, 2026, Yuri gave its first live worldwide performance in Shanghai. On a real stage, in front of a real audience.

Maya: And we are not talking about a hologram in an empty arena, right?

Ryan: Not even close. What makes this really interesting is that Yuri was sharing the stage with human musicians. A folk duo, a children's choir, traditional instrumentalists. So this was not some closed-off tech demo for insiders — this was a mainstream event, with genuine collaboration happening between an AI entity and living, breathing human artists.

Maya: That image is kind of mind-bending when you think about it, right? A children's choir performing alongside an AI...

Ryan: Totally. And what strikes me is that this is no longer a lab experiment or a carefully controlled studio performance. We are talking about real operational validation, under live conditions, with everything that comes with it: the acoustics of the venue, real-time synchronization, a crowd reacting in the moment. And it worked.

Maya: Does this mean we are going to see more and more AI artists performing live?

Ryan: That question is wide open, and it ties into something else we will get to in a bit. But what we can say is that China has officially formalized Yuri's status — meaning government bodies have actually certified this artist. That is not a small thing.

Maya: It is almost like issuing an artistic visa to an AI!

Ryan: That is exactly what it is. And it opens up a whole lot of questions about what we even mean by an artist, what we mean by a performance, and what people are actually looking for when they go to a concert. Are you going to see someone who feels something? Or are you there to share a collective experience?

Maya: Something worth sitting with. But before we get into that, let's talk about two experiments that came out this week, because they both genuinely blew me away. Where do we start?

Ryan: Let's start with robots that remember. The startup Monolithos just launched a public alpha of what they are calling Robot Brain.

Maya: Robot Brain. Not exactly the most understated name out there!

Ryan: Yeah, they clearly did not overthink the branding. But behind that very straightforward name is a really important idea. The problem with robots right now is that they learn during training, and once they are deployed in the real world, they do not retain what happens. Every task is essentially like doing it for the first time.

Maya: Like if you forgot everything you did at work the moment you walked out the door every evening.

Ryan: Exactly. Imagine showing up to the office every single morning with zero memory of what you did the day before. That is the problem Robot Brain is trying to solve. It is a persistent memory platform for robotic agents, which lets a robot hold on to information from previous tasks without needing to retrain the entire system.

Maya: And this is the first time we are seeing something like this?

Ryan: It is the first formalized platform, deployed publicly, that specifically targets this bottleneck. And this is not a minor technical footnote. This is what would allow a robot to genuinely improve over time in its work environment — to adapt, to build on its own experience.

Maya: So potentially, a robot that has been working on a factory floor or in a hospital for six months would be significantly more capable than on day one?

Ryan: Exactly. And that completely changes the value proposition of robotics. You go from a tool to something that looks a lot more like a colleague who actually grows on the job.

Maya: That actually connects really naturally to the second story I wanted to bring up, because it is in the same spirit. SPIRE, the Department of Energy project.

Ryan: Oh yeah, SPIRE is fascinating. The DOE is announcing this platform developed in partnership with SLAC, Stanford, and the University of Chicago. The idea is to automate very precise scientific tasks at their research centers, starting with imaging battery material samples.

Maya: So we are talking about scientific robots, not the kind that vacuums your living room.

Ryan: Right. Embodied AI agents that will physically handle samples, take measurements, analyze images of materials, all autonomously. And the goal is to accelerate scientific discovery. Work that might take a human team weeks could be run continuously, no breaks needed.

Maya: Even on Saturdays!

Ryan: Even on Saturdays, even overnight. And that is exactly why massive investment is pouring into this space. Which is a great segue, because the next part of our show is all about the money flowing into AI in some very concrete sectors.

Maya: Right, because this week, on the science and society side of things, the numbers are genuinely staggering. Over three billion dollars going into physical AI for pharmaceutical discovery. Ryan, what exactly is physical AI in this context?

Ryan: Physical AI is AI that operates in the real world — that handles molecules, that runs experiments in actual laboratories. We are a long way from a language model generating text. We are talking about systems that design experiments, execute them through robotics, analyze the results, and propose next steps. In pharmaceutical research, that is potentially revolutionary.

Maya: And the big names are showing up?

Ryan: The very biggest names. Thermo Fisher, Amgen, Roche, Multiply Labs. And a concrete example: Simcere Pharmaceutical, a Chinese player, signed a major global partnership back in August 2026 with Schrödinger, the company that specializes in AI for chemistry. The division of labor is clear: Simcere handles the preclinical and clinical phases, Schrödinger provides the agentic AI infrastructure. Everyone sticks to what they do best.

Maya: It is almost like an assembly line for the drugs of tomorrow.

Ryan: That is a great way to put it. And running alongside that, there is an even larger commitment with the partnership between the Biohub, the DOE, and the NIH — one point eight billion dollars over multiple years. The goal is to generate AI-ready data in biology and deploy fully autonomous labs across the national laboratory network in the US.

Maya: One point eight billion dollars for robots to do biology. That is a sentence I would have found absolutely unhinged five years ago.

Ryan: And Dario Gil, the DOE's Under Secretary for Science, said it plainly: this partnership sets a new standard for open science. And importantly, the data that gets produced will be an open resource for the global research community.

Maya: So not just for Americans — it is for everyone.

Ryan: Exactly. And that connects to something I find really compelling right now, which is the question of AI geography. Because this week we are also seeing a massive push into the Middle East. Robbyant, which is a subsidiary of Ant Group, just signed a memorandum of understanding with the Arab Federation for the Digital Economy to deploy embodied AI in Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and the broader Gulf region.

Maya: Ant Group is the Chinese fintech giant — but now they are pushing into robotics?

Ryan: Right, through their subsidiary Robbyant. And the agreement is structured around five strategic pillars to deploy, localize, and commercialize robotic solutions in the region. What is interesting here is that it shows the AI race is no longer just a US versus China story — it is also playing out across emerging markets, and the Middle East has clearly become a major strategic battleground.

Maya: Which brings us right back to music, because we are not done with that topic!

Ryan: Not even a little bit, and it really deserves a deep look. Because while we were talking about Yuri performing live in Shanghai, something significant is happening in the music industry more broadly. We are finding out that ninety thousand tracks are being uploaded every single day to streaming platforms. Per day.

Maya: Ninety thousand. I mean... how do you even begin to listen to that?

Ryan: You do not. And that is exactly the problem. A huge chunk of those tracks are AI-generated, and the platforms are completely overwhelmed. Faced with that, two very different responses are emerging this week. On one side, Google has deployed Producer AI in its labs — a music generation platform integrating DeepMind models, with one really interesting element: every track produced can be tagged with SynthID, a digital watermark that is completely inaudible to human ears but technically detectable.

Maya: So you can tell that an AI created it.

Ryan: Exactly. And that aligns with what the IFPI — the International Federation of the Phonographic Industry — is pushing for in 2026: a clear distinction between content that is AI-generated versus content that is simply AI-assisted. On the other side, a platform called VAYLA is launching VAYLA Arena in the UAE, with a completely different approach: putting humans back at the center of music discovery. The idea is to let fans evaluate, discover, and engage with emerging artists, in a world where AI is drowning everything out.

Maya: It is like a human resistance movement against the algorithmic flood.

Ryan: I love that framing. And it raises a real question: in a world where AI can produce ninety thousand songs a day, what actually has value? Is it scarcity? Is it human authenticity? Is it simply whatever moves us, regardless of who or what made it?

Maya: And that leads perfectly into our weekly what-if moment. Ryan, go ahead.

Ryan: Alright, here is my big-picture thought for today. What if the next major battle in music, in art, in creative work generally, is not about quality but about certifiable authenticity? We are seeing watermarks like SynthID rolling out, transparency standards from the IFPI, platforms like VAYLA betting on human judgment. And at the same time, artists like Yuri are officially certified as AI and selling out concert venues.

Maya: So two worlds coexisting, but with labels?

Ryan: Maybe. The idea is that we might be in the process of building a kind of dual cultural market. On one side, AI creation that is massively accessible, endlessly customizable, infinite. On the other, certified human creation that is rare, and that carries an emotional and economic premium. Kind of like natural wine versus mass-produced wine — both have their market, but they tell very different stories.

Maya: And which one does the audience choose?

Ryan: Probably both, depending on the mood and the moment. AI music for your morning run, a human concert for a night that actually means something. What is fascinating is that technology is forcing society to define precisely what it considers human, and why that matters. And that is a conversation we never really had to have before.
