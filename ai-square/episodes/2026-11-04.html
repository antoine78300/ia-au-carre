Full transcript

Maya: Welcome to AI Squared, the podcast that breaks down artificial intelligence in your everyday life. Today is Wednesday, November 4th, 2026, and trust me, we have a packed show: an AI agent that handles your server outages at three in the morning, Google absolutely crushing the benchmarks, and a study that might completely change how you pick your models. Let's get into it.

Maya: Ryan, let's kick things off with what's been buzzing in DevOps and SRE circles today. PagerDuty announced yesterday an AI agent called Paige. What exactly are we looking at here?

Ryan: So Paige is what you'd call an autonomous SRE agent. SRE stands for Site Reliability Engineering, which is basically the team responsible for keeping your applications up and running. PagerDuty built this agent on top of sixteen years of operational data accumulated on their platform. And what really stands out is how ambitious the vision is: Paige is designed to own the full incident lifecycle, from detecting the signal all the way through remediation, with zero human involvement.

Maya: Zero human involvement? So if my server goes down at three in the morning, Paige handles it and I just stay asleep?

Ryan: That's exactly the pitch. The idea is to do away with the middle-of-the-night alert that drags an engineer out of bed to go investigate. Paige can run multi-hypothesis investigations in parallel, evaluate several potential root causes at the same time, and trigger the fix. And to do all of that, it integrates with over 750 tools: Slack, Microsoft Teams, and development environments through the MCP protocol, including Claude and Cursor.

Maya: MCP, that's the standard that lets agents talk to external tools, right?

Ryan: Exactly. MCP, or Model Context Protocol, has become kind of a universal language that allows AI agents to interact with the existing tool ecosystem. And PagerDuty is betting that this is their lane: being the first mainstream incident management platform to bring truly autonomous agents into this kind of work. We're not talking about assistance anymore. This is full delegation.

Maya: Doesn't that raise some real concerns though? I mean, you're letting a machine make decisions on its own about critical infrastructure.

Ryan: Absolutely, and that's the central debate here. The value proposition is obvious. Nobody wants to be woken up at three in the morning for an incident that could just be fixed automatically. But trust in autonomous remediation is something you build gradually. PagerDuty has the advantage of sixteen years of data to train Paige's judgment, but teams are still going to need to define very clearly what actions the agent is and isn't allowed to take on its own.

Maya: Alright, let's move on to tools and frameworks, and there are two big announcements here. First up, Snowflake, which held a conference yesterday called Expedition 2026. Ryan, what did they show off?

Ryan: Snowflake announced some major expansions to their AI agent platform, and what's really interesting is the direction they're heading. They're not adding flashy new models or headline-grabbing features. They're going after the real-world problems that are actually blocking large-scale enterprise agent deployment.

Maya: Like what, specifically?

Ryan: Several concrete things. First, agent abuse detection: identifying when an agent is behaving abnormally or has potentially been manipulated. Then there's observability through OpenTelemetry, which lets you trace exactly what an agent is doing step by step. There's also a Cortex sandbox for testing agents in isolation, cost governance, and dynamic routing to the most appropriate models with MCP integration.

Maya: And there's one point around authentication that sounds pretty technical but actually seems really important?

Ryan: Yeah, and honestly this might be the most significant innovation even though it's flying under the radar. Snowflake is implementing a user-identity authentication that gets injected by the calling service but stays completely invisible to the language model itself. In plain terms: when an AI agent is acting on behalf of a user, it cannot access data beyond what that user is allowed to see, even if someone tries to manipulate it into doing so. This is called privilege injection, and it's a critical bottleneck when you're deploying agents at scale in multi-tool enterprise environments.

Maya: So this is essentially a direct answer to the security concerns around enterprise AI agents. Now the second tooling story of the day: E2E raising eight million dollars to verify code generated by AI agents?

Ryan: That's right. E2E, formerly known as TesterArmy, is closing an eight-million-dollar seed round with investors including Credo Ventures, KAYA, and Point Nine Capital. Their proposition is straightforward: AI coding agents are now writing a ton of code, but you need an independent verification layer to make sure that code is actually correct. They combine agent-based steps with exact-match checks, so it's not just AI checking AI, there are formal validation mechanisms in the mix too.

Maya: It almost makes perfect sense: if you automate code production, you have to automate the verification too.

Ryan: And this is a market that's about to explode. The more capable coding agents get, the more code gets generated, and the more critical reliable verification becomes. E2E is planting their flag at exactly the right moment.

Maya: Okay, now we get to the research and benchmarks section, and there are two findings here that are really worth slowing down for. The first comes from Gartner. Ryan, the number is pretty striking.

Ryan: Very striking. Gartner published an analysis saying that open-weight models, meaning models whose weights are publicly available, can handle 90 percent of agentic tasks at one-twentieth the cost of proprietary frontier models.

Maya: One-twentieth. So if I'm running my agents on GPT or Claude, I'm paying twenty times more than I need to for 90 percent of my use cases?

Ryan: That's Gartner's argument, and it's a significant validation. That ratio used to be estimated at ten times in earlier analyses. Moving it to twenty times is a strong signal. But the most interesting takeaway isn't even about cost. It's about where the real value sits in an agentic system.

Maya: And where do they say that is?

Ryan: In the harness, meaning the orchestrator. The component that automatically routes tasks to the right model, manages sequences of actions, and decides when to use a powerful expensive model versus a lean open-weight one. The value is no longer in the raw model itself. It's in the orchestration logic. That's a pretty significant shift in how a lot of companies are thinking about their AI investments.

Maya: And the second big benchmark story of the day: Google with Gemini 4 Carbon. Ryan, the numbers really do speak for themselves here.

Ryan: Gemini 4 Carbon takes the top spot on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index V5 benchmark with a score of 59, edging out Claude Opus 5.5. But the most remarkable number is the hallucination rate: it's the lowest ever recorded on this benchmark.

Maya: Just to make sure everyone's on the same page: hallucinations are when the model just makes things up, right?

Ryan: Exactly. It's one of the fundamental problems with language models. And Gemini 4 Carbon appears to have made a meaningful breakthrough on that front. Google also took a very controlled approach to deployment, working first with the US government for pre-release testing, similar to what Anthropic did with their Mythos model back in 2025. It's an interesting pattern: the most powerful models go through institutional validation before they're released to the general public.

Maya: So Google is reclaiming the top of the leaderboard, at least for now. Let's shift to industry impact, and here we have a story that hits really close to home for hospitals and patients. Ryan, tell us about Mayo Clinic.

Ryan: Yes, and this is a deployment with real reach. Mayo Clinic Platform launched in November 2026 a clinical decision support system integrated directly into the electronic health record, across more than 35 medical specialties. Clinicians at Mayo Clinic, as well as their external partners, now have access to AI-assisted diagnostics through language models.

Maya: AI-assisted diagnostics. Does that mean the AI is diagnosing patients instead of doctors?

Ryan: No, and that distinction really matters. The system is framed as support for clinical judgment, not a replacement for it. The AI suggests, the physician decides. What's new here is the integration into mainstream EHR infrastructure, the electronic health record, inside a world-class medical institution. This is multimodal agentic orchestration entering daily clinical practice at scale.

Maya: And then on a much darker note, there's a cybersecurity story that shows the other side of agentic AI.

Ryan: Yeah. Bitdefender documented a ransomware attack by a group called 3AM targeting British schools. What's new about this attack is the use of autonomous agent loops to build out domains and identify new targets, specifically other schools. This isn't a language model writing phishing emails. This is agent orchestration amplifying the operational capacity of human attackers.

Maya: So cybercriminals are using the exact same agentic orchestration techniques we've been talking about throughout this episode.

Ryan: And that's precisely what's so alarming. The human-agent hybrid approach, where AI amplifies rather than replaces the human operator, works in both directions. PagerDuty uses it for resilience. 3AM uses it for attacks. The technology itself is neutral. What people do with it is not.

Maya: To wrap things up, two quick stories that are worth flagging. First, Meituan, the Chinese commerce and logistics giant.

Ryan: Meituan deployed LongCat-2.5, a multimodal agentic foundation model with 1.6 trillion parameters. What matters here isn't necessarily the parameter count. It's the confirmation that major e-commerce platforms are embedding agentic AI into their core operational infrastructure. Agentic adoption is going mainstream in Asia just as much as it is in the West.

Maya: And then an unexpected collaboration: OpenAI and Mazda.

Ryan: OpenAI announced model capabilities for mechanical, electrical, and systems design, with a formalized partnership with Mazda focused on aerodynamics and crash safety. Agentic AI is moving into automotive design, into physical R and D. This isn't just text or code anymore. This is product engineering.

Maya: Ryan, if we zoom out and look at everything we covered today, is there a common thread running through it all?

Ryan: There is, and it's pretty clear. Agentic AI is moving out of labs and startups and into critical infrastructure: hospitals with Mayo Clinic, server operations with PagerDuty, automotive engineering with OpenAI and Mazda. And at the same time, the questions around governance, cost, and security are no longer theoretical. Snowflake is tackling governance head-on, Gartner is recalibrating how we think about cost, and Bitdefender is documenting the risks in the real world. We are genuinely in the phase of real deployment now, with all the stakes that come with it.

Maya: Ryan, thanks so much for breaking all of this down. And thank you to everyone listening for sticking with us.
