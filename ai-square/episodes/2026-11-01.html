Emma: Good morning everyone, and welcome to AI Squared, the podcast that drops you right into the deep end of artificial intelligence every single day. It is Sunday, November first, 2026, and as always on weekends, Liam and I are here to bring you a slightly more relaxed, grab-your-coffee-and-settle-in kind of episode. And trust me, the news cycle has absolutely zero chill, even on a holiday weekend. So get comfortable, because we have got a lot to get into.

Emma: Liam, good morning! You are looking surprisingly bright-eyed for a Sunday.

Liam: Morning Emma! Yeah, honestly I am fired up, because there are some stories this week that genuinely made me stop and do a double take. More than once.

Emma: Perfect, that is exactly the energy we want. So let us kick things off with your big discovery of the week. What hit you the hardest?

Liam: Okay, without hesitation, I have to go with the TUM RoboGym up in Munich. The New York Times got rare access to this pretty extraordinary facility. Picture a gym, but instead of people lifting weights, you have got one hundred humanoid robots training continuously on real-world tasks.

Emma: One hundred robots. In one room. Training. I will be honest, that image is equal parts fascinating and just a little bit unsettling.

Liam: That is exactly the right reaction. And what makes it remarkable is how concrete it all is. This is not abstract simulation stuff. These robots are handling automotive parts, stacking boxes, picking up fragile objects with real-time tactile adjustments. This is the physical world, full stop.

Emma: But hold on, because the big unsolved problem in robotics for years has been exactly that gap between what works in simulation and what actually holds up in the real world, right? The whole sim-to-real problem?

Liam: You nailed it. And that is precisely what this gym seems to be cracking. By having robots train directly on physical, real-world tasks, over and over, at massive scale, you close that gap. And here is the proof that it is working: BMW and Japan Airlines have already validated and committed to deploying these technologies in real operations. We are not talking about scrappy startups running tests in a garage.

Emma: BMW and Japan Airlines. I mean, that is about as serious a stamp of approval as you can get. So who is actually behind this place?

Liam: It is a collaboration between Neura Robotics, a company specializing in humanoid robotics, and the Technical University of Munich. And this is not happening in isolation. In the same vein, we also learned this week that ByteDance, yes, the parent company of TikTok, is on a major talent hiring spree in robotics, trying to accelerate what is called embodied AI from the research lab into commercial hardware.

Emma: Embodied AI, is that just the technical way of saying robots that have physical intelligence out in the real world?

Liam: Exactly right. AI that lives in a body, that physically interacts with its environment. And to give you a sense of the scale of what is coming, Morgan Stanley is projecting fifty thousand commercial humanoid robot units for 2026, and one hundred thousand for 2027.

Emma: One hundred thousand humanoid robots out in the world within a year. Okay, that number genuinely takes a second to land.

Liam: And here is another player jumping in in a really interesting way. Yanfeng International, a major automotive supplier, is partnering with a Humanoid Robotics Innovation Center to build a dedicated demonstration space for embodied AI in the auto industry. They are opening up their full production line data to train these systems.

Emma: So even the traditional auto suppliers are all in.

Liam: At full speed. And speaking of the auto world, there is a pretty surprising announcement worth flagging: Chery Group, the Chinese automaker, has formalized a connected ecosystem linking their Luxeed smart vehicles with their family companion robots called MOBO, developed by their subsidiary AiMOGA. The idea is that your home robot and your car's intelligence share the same underlying AI architecture, a system they call MoAgent, so that data and capabilities sync seamlessly between your house and your vehicle.

Emma: Wait, so your home robot and your car are literally speaking the same language, sharing the same information?

Liam: That is the vision. One unified ecosystem across your home, your mobility, your vehicle. It is a pretty ambitious bet on the convergence of domestic robotics and automotive tech.

Emma: That is genuinely fascinating. Okay, let us shift gears to experiments and original projects, because I saw Spotify was in the news this week and I absolutely want to talk about that.

Liam: Oh great pick for a Sunday music edition. Spotify is working on an AI remix tool, with a planned launch in the first quarter of 2027. The idea is to let Premium subscribers transform existing songs into different styles, basically creating AI-generated cover versions.

Emma: So I could take a pop song and flip it into a jazz version or a metal version?

Liam: That is exactly the concept. And what is interesting from a business standpoint is the model Spotify is going with. This will not just be a feature baked into your existing subscription. They are looking at a separate add-on subscription dedicated to this tool.

Emma: A subscription inside your subscription. Bold move, Spotify.

Liam: Bold or brilliant, depending on how you look at it. And it stands apart from what competitors are doing. YouTube Music is developing an AI content labeling system, and Deezer is offering a reversible filter. Spotify is going with a distinctly more aggressive commercial strategy.

Emma: Now on the science side, you also had something around drug discovery that I really want to get into.

Liam: Yes, a couple of things actually. First up, Isomorphic Labs, which is a spinoff from Google DeepMind led by Demis Hassabis, is working on something pretty mind-bending. Their goal is to predict how a therapeutic molecule interacts with every other molecule in the human body.

Emma: Every other molecule. That is an almost incomprehensible number of interactions.

Liam: Which is exactly why you cannot test it manually in a lab. The cost would be completely prohibitive. But with their AI models, they are hoping to predict those interactions with enough precision that scientists could design a drug without ever setting foot in a lab. The ultimate target is revolutionizing drug discovery, especially for cancer.

Emma: That is a monumentally ambitious goal. Do they have concrete results yet?

Liam: They are still in the development phase. No candidate produced through this AI method has received FDA approval yet. But the direction is clearly set. And in the same space, there is a really concrete story with a company called Manifold AI, founded in 2016 and pivoted to software in 2022. They have deployed a platform on AWS where researchers can ask questions in plain natural language, and AI agents handle the complex multi-step workflows for them.

Emma: And this is actually working at real scale?

Liam: Three hundred researchers are already using the system independently, with access to a database of over one hundred thirty thousand patients. Questions that used to take months to process are now being run autonomously by the researchers themselves, no technical intermediary needed.

Emma: Three hundred researchers getting months of their lives back. I am here for that. Alright, let us move into our society, science, and creativity segment, because there was a study that really stopped me in my tracks.

Liam: The Israeli study?

Emma: That is the one. Researchers in Israel published something pretty counterintuitive about AI and creativity. Because we usually talk about what AI can or cannot do artistically, but they are asking a completely different question.

Liam: Yes, their angle is really striking. Their starting point is that generative AI lets you skip years of artistic training. It produces sophisticated content instantly. Nothing new there. But what they argue is that in doing so, we are breaking something essential: the historic link between mastering an art form and the personal growth that process produces.

Emma: So it is not art itself that is under threat, it is the path to art.

Liam: Exactly. AI does not kill art, it eliminates the transformative experiences that give creativity its deeper meaning. Spending ten years learning to paint is not just about learning to paint. It is about everything that changes inside you over those ten years.

Emma: And that is a really important distinction. Because if you can generate a beautiful image in thirty seconds, you are not having remotely the same experience as someone who spent years learning to draw, failing, starting over.

Liam: And that process, according to these researchers, is what gives creation its deepest meaning. AI can deliver the output, but it cannot generate the journey. Now in a different register, there is also a study out of the medical world, also from Israeli researchers, this time from Ben-Gurion University and the Clalit health system, published in the Annals of Internal Medicine. They demonstrated that leading large language models can successfully identify which medical interventions are unnecessary.

Emma: Unnecessary how? You mean medical procedures that get done without really being warranted?

Liam: Exactly. We are talking overmedication, redundant tests, treatments with questionable benefit. And what is particularly sharp about their findings is that the AI models were also able to recognize the gray areas, the cases where the medical consensus itself is divided. They are not claiming to have all the answers.

Emma: Honestly that is more reassuring than if they had solved everything. A tool that knows its own limits is already a major step forward.

Liam: Absolutely. And it validates the idea that these models can be genuinely useful in clinical reasoning, not to replace the doctor, but to help them sort through the noise and prioritize.

Emma: Okay, we have reached my favorite part of the Sunday show. The what if. Liam, what are you bringing us today for our big forward-looking thought?

Liam: So I want to connect a few threads we have been pulling on today. We have got a hundred robots training in Munich, a hundred thousand humanoid robots expected by 2027, cars and home robots sharing the same intelligence, AI agents conducting medical research on behalf of scientists. Here is the what if: at what point does embodied AI, meaning physically intelligent robots out in the world, become as ordinary in our daily lives as smartphones became in the 2010s?

Emma: That is a real question. Because we have gotten used to AI that stays behind a screen. Now we are talking about intelligence that takes a physical form, in your home, in your car, on the factory floor.

Liam: And the tipping point, based on everything we are seeing, will not come with a big announcement. It will happen gradually, almost invisibly. An auto supplier opening up its production data. A robot gym in Munich. ByteDance going on a hiring spree. Each piece quietly clicks into place.

Emma: Which brings me to a slightly uncomfortable question: are we actually going to be ready, culturally and socially, to share our living spaces with physically intelligent entities, when we have barely even started working through the implications of digital AI?

Liam: That is exactly the question nobody has a clean answer to yet. And that might be the real story of the next several years. Not so much whether the technology works, because clearly it is moving very fast, but whether we actually know what we want to do with it, and what the ground rules should be.

Emma: A question worth carrying with you as the week gets started. And on that note, equal parts exciting and a little bit dizzying, it is time to wrap up for today.

Liam: Thanks so much to everyone who joined us for this Sunday episode. It was a really great one, covering creativity, medicine, robotics, business strategy, all of it. AI truly does not take a day off.

Emma: Unlike us, who are absolutely going to try, at least mentally. Take care of yourselves, and we will see you tomorrow for another episode of AI Squared. See you then!
