Maya: Good morning and welcome to AI Squared, the podcast that breaks down artificial intelligence in your everyday life! It is Sunday, the sun is out, the coffee is hot, and we have got a seriously fascinating lineup for you today. So get comfortable, because we are talking about drugs discovered in ten minutes flat, robots earning their diplomas, and music made by machines. Let's dive in!

Maya: Ryan, good morning! You holding up okay for a Sunday?

Ryan: Sunday or not, when the news is this wild, I am always ready to go. And trust me, we have got a lot to get through today.

Maya: Alright, let's jump straight into the big story. Anthropic, the company behind Claude, the AI model we talk about all the time, just announced that they have been running a secret biology lab. A secret lab. That is quite an opening.

Ryan: Yeah, and the word secret is not just for dramatic effect. Anthropic quietly stood up an internal life sciences lab that nobody outside the company even knew existed until now. The whole idea is to deploy their agentic AI, Claude Science, directly on some of the most complex biological problems out there. We are talking about molecules called bispecific antibodies, which are molecules that can target two completely different points in the body at the same time, and they are considered practically untreatable using conventional methods.

Maya: So Claude is basically playing mad scientist on its own?

Ryan: Exactly, but not in a passive way like read a bunch of papers and spit out a summary. Claude is operating autonomously, proposing experiments, running them, iterating, just like a researcher actually conducting studies. That is what we mean when we say an AI agent. And what is really striking is how fast this technology has gone from a footnote to a core part of Anthropic's own operations. Back in February 2026, Claude was handling less than one percent of the company's internal R and D. Today that number is twenty-six percent.

Maya: Hold on, twenty-six percent of the R and D at one of the most advanced AI companies in the world, being run by their own AI? In less than eight months?

Ryan: That is exactly right. And this is not just internal messaging. There is a concrete real-world example that really puts it in perspective. Novo Nordisk, the Danish pharmaceutical giant, the company behind Ozempic, used Claude Science to speed up the documentation process for clinical studies. Work that previously took ten weeks, ten weeks of intensive human effort, now takes ten minutes.

Maya: Ten weeks down to ten minutes. Let me make sure everyone really absorbs that, because we are not talking about a modest improvement here. We are talking about a factor of one thousand.

Ryan: A factor of one thousand, exactly. And clinical documentation is absolutely critical in drug development. It is what allows health regulators to validate trials and approve treatments. Cutting that time by a factor of one thousand completely reshapes the timeline for getting a drug to market. A medication that currently takes ten years to reach your pharmacy could take a whole lot less.

Maya: That is mind-bending. And when you think about diseases where we still have nothing, the implications are enormous.

Ryan: That is precisely where it gets philosophically interesting. Anthropic is specifically talking about molecules that are currently untreatable. Not hard to treat, not expensive to treat, untreatable. So the ambition is to go after problems that human science simply does not know how to solve yet, and use AI to open those doors.

Maya: Alright, I am personally putting in an order for a procrastination cure.

Ryan: I will forward the request to Claude. I am sure it can handle that one.

Maya: Okay, moving on to our experiments and original projects of the week. And we are staying in territory that genuinely fascinates me, which is robots. Because there are quite a few making headlines this week.

Ryan: Yeah, and that is meaningful in itself. We are clearly crossing a threshold. At the Hangzhou Robot Summit in China, a major event that took place this week, organizers unveiled a unified framework for evaluating the capabilities of what they call embodied AI. And that term really does mean exactly what it sounds like, an AI that has a physical body and interacts with the real world.

Maya: And four robots actually graduated, if I am reading this right?

Ryan: That is the word they used, and I think it is pretty telling. Four robots were deemed capable enough to be deployed in real-world environments. You have got a museum guide robot, one working at a robotics academy, a third doing quality inspection at a Nestlé factory in Tianjin, and then a fourth entertainment robot over in South Korea.

Maya: The Nestlé inspector is my personal favorite. Is it tasting KitKats?

Ryan: I would argue that is the best job in the building. But seriously, what is interesting about these four cases is the diversity. We are not talking about robots in tightly controlled lab environments. We are talking about robots operating alongside real humans, in real spaces, with all the unpredictability that comes with that.

Maya: And we also have the startup Embodied AI, based in Lausanne, which just launched its own offering for European manufacturing?

Ryan: Right, that announcement dropped last Friday. A Swiss startup looking to deploy their intelligent robots directly on their clients' production lines across Europe. What is interesting about their approach is that they are not just selling hardware. They are deploying their entire technology stack, software included, right there on the factory floor. The AI travels with the robot.

Maya: And on the data side, the stuff you need to actually train these robots, there is also a solid open-source development this week?

Ryan: Absolutely. Boden AI, a Chinese startup, just released a massive open-source dataset, over eighty-two hours of real robot learning data. We are talking three thousand three hundred and forty-seven episodes, more than four million images. And what makes this dataset special is that it captures moments where a human steps in during the robot's learning process to correct it or guide it.

Maya: That is what we call reinforcement learning with human intervention.

Ryan: Exactly. And making that data freely available to researchers around the world could be a serious accelerant. It is kind of like publishing the lecture notes from a brilliant professor. Everyone benefits.

Maya: Now we are heading into our AI and Society segment. And today we are talking music, creativity, and what people actually want. Because there are some pretty interesting studies out this week.

Ryan: Yeah, and this is one of those topics where you do not need an engineering degree to have strong opinions.

Maya: So here are the numbers. Eighty-two percent of listeners say they value human creativity in music. Eighty-one percent want mandatory labeling when a song is entirely AI-generated. On the surface, the message seems clear: people do not want AI music.

Ryan: That is what people say. But the question the studies this week are really digging into is whether that is what people actually do. Because there is a well-known gap between stated preferences and real behavior. If you do not tell someone a song was AI-generated, do they catch it? Do they enjoy it any less?

Maya: That is the big question. It is almost like those blind wine tastings where the experts frequently pick the wrong bottle when they cannot see the label.

Ryan: Great analogy. And what adds nuance here is that seventy-eight percent of musicians themselves say they are open to using AI tools as assistants. Not to replace their creativity, but to enhance it. Things like separating stems in a track, improving audio quality, exploring harmonies.

Maya: And some platforms have made a deliberate choice about where they draw the line?

Ryan: Yeah, Moises AI is a good example. They have decided to put intentional limits on what their AI does. They help with stem separation and audio processing, but they explicitly refuse to generate complete songs. It is almost a brand-level ethical stance.

Maya: And Roland, the instrument manufacturer, actually developed AI governance principles for music creation back in 2022. They saw this coming pretty early.

Ryan: What is interesting is that the music industry, which is often portrayed as being behind the curve, is actually building some pretty sophisticated frameworks here. It is not all-in on AI or AI-free, it is a careful, ongoing negotiation about what is acceptable and what is not.

Ryan: And Berkeley is playing that same nuanced game with a pretty significant restructuring?

Ryan: Yeah, and this is a strong signal. UC Berkeley, one of the most influential universities in the world when it comes to computer science, just created a new interdisciplinary structure they are calling Gateway. The idea is to pull AI out of its computer science bubble and connect it to life sciences, climate, public policy, social sciences.

Maya: So instead of the computer scientists doing their thing in one corner and the biologists doing theirs in another, you actually mix it all together?

Ryan: Exactly. And Dean Jennifer Chase says something really important here. AI should not be siloed, it should flow through every discipline. It is a vision that connects directly to what we were talking about with Anthropic and their biology lab. The real breakthroughs are going to come from intersections, not from isolated silos.

Maya: Alright, we have made it to my favorite part of the show, the What If moment. Ryan, looking at everything we covered today, what is the big open question on your mind?

Ryan: What keeps jumping out at me this week is the speed. At Anthropic, less than one percent to twenty-six percent in eight months. Clinical studies from ten weeks to ten minutes. In China, two hundred and thirty-three percent growth in robot production in seven months. These numbers are not linear. These are curves that are accelerating.

Maya: So what is the What If?

Ryan: What if we are systematically underestimating how fast all of this is moving? Because humans naturally tend to project the future in a straight line. We tell ourselves things will change, but gradually, we will have time to adjust. But the concrete examples we saw this week suggest these transitions can be brutally sudden. A drug that takes ten years to develop could take two years tomorrow. A robot that was in a lab last year is in a Nestlé factory today.

Maya: And that raises very real questions about adaptation, for researchers, for workers, for institutions across the board.

Ryan: It does. And the Berkeley reorganization might actually be a response to exactly that. Train people who know how to navigate this pace of change, who can work across disciplines, who are not locked into a single narrow specialty that could be automated away tomorrow.

Maya: And that is not a doom-and-gloom take. It is actually saying that agility is something you can prepare for.

Ryan: Exactly. The question is not whether things are going to change. The question is whether we are getting ready for how fast that change is coming.

Maya: And that is the perfect note to end on today. Ryan, thanks as always for giving us both the big picture and the questions worth chewing on over our Sunday coffee.

Ryan: My pleasure. And honestly, I hope everyone listening enjoyed the ride today. Sunday is a great day to slow down and ask yourself where all of this is actually taking us.

Maya: Thanks so much for spending this time with us on AI Squared. If you enjoyed the episode, share it, tell a friend, it truly makes a difference. And we will see you tomorrow for a brand new episode. Take care of yourselves, and talk to you tomorrow!
