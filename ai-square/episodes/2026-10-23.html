Maya: Welcome to AI Squared, the podcast that breaks down artificial intelligence every single day from every angle. I'm Maya, and today is Friday, October 23rd, 2026. We've got a packed show: governments handing over public services to AI agents, hackers using AI to hammer hundreds of thousands of accounts, and the guardrails that researchers and regulators are racing to put in place. Ryan is here with me. Ryan, let's get into it.

Ryan: Let's do it, Maya. This is a dense one, with stories ranging from a pretty remarkable government transformation to some genuinely alarming offensive cybersecurity news. Plenty to unpack today.

Maya: Let's start with the big story of the day. Ryan, the UAE has been making a lot of noise lately when it comes to AI-powered government. What exactly is going on over there?

Ryan: So this comes straight from Omar Sultan Al Olama, the UAE's Minister of State for AI, who spoke at the World Economic Forum on October 14th. The UAE has officially deployed fifty percent of its government services through autonomous AI agents. And we're not talking about basic automation here. They've built what they call a centralized control tower, essentially a master dashboard that keeps watch over all of those agents at once.

Maya: Fifty percent of government services. That is a huge number. But what does that actually look like on the ground for an Emirati citizen?

Ryan: It means that when someone goes to interact with a government agency, whether that's renewing a document, applying for a social benefit, or just getting information, there's a very good chance an autonomous AI agent is handling at least part of that interaction. And here's the number that really puts it in perspective: AI adoption among the UAE population sits at seventy-three percent. The global average in Q2 2026 was just nineteen percent. That gap is pretty staggering.

Maya: And how do they make sure the whole thing doesn't go off the rails? Because handing public services over to autonomous agents raises some pretty serious control questions.

Ryan: That's exactly where this control tower comes in. The UAE runs hybrid audits, meaning human supervisors and agents work together. Human overseers are continuously validating what the agents are doing, and the control tower gives them centralized visibility into how every system is behaving. It's a distributed governance model with centralized oversight, and at this national scale, that's genuinely unprecedented.

Maya: A model that a lot of other countries are going to be watching very closely, I'd imagine. Now let's shift to something a lot darker, because this next story also involves AI agents, but in a very different context.

Ryan: Yeah, and this one should put every security team on high alert right now. Microsoft has formally attributed a massive campaign of cloud environment intrusions to a Chinese-linked hacking group called Storm-0940. They used a tool called ARTEX, and the scale of what they pulled off is significant: two thousand two hundred and fifty Microsoft 365 tenants were targeted.

Maya: Two thousand two hundred and fifty companies or organizations running Microsoft 365. That's enormous.

Ryan: And the number that really gives you a sense of the speed here is twenty-nine hundred login attempts per minute. ARTEX is an agentic AI tool that automates what's normally called pentesting, penetration testing, but here it was weaponized and run at massive scale. The attackers managed to get around multi-factor authentication by exploiting credentials that had been stolen through the ConnectWise platform.

Maya: So AI isn't just being used to defend systems anymore. It's being deployed as an offensive weapon at scale.

Ryan: And that's the real paradigm shift here. We're not talking about someone manually trying to break in. We're talking about agents operating autonomously, testing thousands of entry points simultaneously, adapting on the fly. The speed and scale are what change everything. The Storm-0940 case is probably the most thoroughly documented example to date of a nation-state actor using agentic AI for offensive operations at this level.

Maya: Alright, let's move into our tools and frameworks segment, because this week has been really busy on that front. Ryan, where do you want to start?

Ryan: I want to start with AWS, because this is something a lot of technical teams are going to care about. On October 22nd, Amazon launched the general availability of serverless training sessions inside SageMaker AI. The short version: you can now fine-tune large language models, things like gpt-oss-20b, Gemma 4, or Qwen, without having to manage any GPU infrastructure yourself.

Maya: And what does that actually change in practice? Because I'm guessing GPU management is a major headache for most teams right now.

Ryan: It's a major headache and a major expense. Up until now, if you wanted to fine-tune a model, you had to spin up GPU machines, manage them, optimize them. That requires specialized expertise and it costs a lot. With this serverless offering, AWS handles all of that behind the scenes. And the really interesting piece for AI agents specifically is that SageMaker now supports multi-turn reinforcement learning, which is purpose-built for optimizing the behavior of autonomous agents.

Maya: So they're lowering the barrier to entry for companies that want to build their own specialized agents.

Ryan: Exactly. And on a similar note in the open-source tooling space, the Agentic AI Foundation announced on October 22nd that it's bringing four new projects into its ecosystem. Among them, Anthropic's Agent Skills, which is a standardized format for defining agent capabilities. And two projects from Hugging Face and Alibaba, OpenEnv and OpenSandbox, which are training environments that simulate real production conditions.

Maya: So the idea is to build a shared open-source infrastructure where agents can train under conditions that actually reflect the real world.

Ryan: That's it. And the important thing here is that there's now a dedicated foundation governing all of this. It formalizes collaborative oversight around these open-source building blocks. That's a real sign of maturity in the agentic ecosystem.

Maya: We also have an announcement from Martingale with their tool Raven Alpha. What is that exactly?

Ryan: Raven Alpha is a platform from a Carnegie Mellon spinout, and it tackles a very real problem: model drift in production. An AI model doesn't stay sharp forever. The data changes, user behavior shifts, and the model quietly starts underperforming without anyone necessarily noticing. Raven Alpha detects that drift in real time, and independent evaluators have ranked it number one in its category. This is especially critical for autonomous agents that are running without constant human supervision.

Maya: It's like a continuous health monitor for your AI agent.

Ryan: That's a great way to put it. And Sumsub also launched something worth talking about in the access control space: a module called KYA for MCP. KYA stands for Know Your Agent. The idea is to let organizations define precisely which internal systems each AI agent is allowed to access. It's a direct response to a pretty alarming stat: sixty-five percent of organizations have already experienced incidents caused by AI agents that had overly broad access permissions.

Maya: So the whole point is to not hand an AI agent the keys to the entire house when all it needs is one room.

Ryan: Exactly. It's the principle of least privilege, applied to autonomous agents. And it's an area where companies have clearly been underinvesting.

Maya: Let's get into our research segment. Google published something pretty fascinating with what they're calling Chrysalis.

Ryan: So Chrysalis is a research architecture, not a product you can go use today, and I want to be clear about that. But what Google describes is conceptually very advanced. It's a system of autonomous agents capable of diagnosing failures, writing the tools needed to fix them, verifying those tools, and learning from the whole experience, all without continuous human intervention.

Maya: So agents that repair themselves and improve on their own. That's a little mind-bending.

Ryan: It is, and that's exactly why Google documented five very specific guardrails. Every step in the process goes through cryptographic signatures, so you can trace precisely what an agent did and why. Automated policy checks validate that actions stay within an approved boundary. And critically, there are what Google calls human gates: decision points where a human has to sign off before the agent moves forward.

Maya: So even in a system designed to run autonomously, there are still human checkpoints built in.

Ryan: Which directly addresses one of the most common criticisms of self-evolving agents: the risk of unsupervised drift through memory or prompt poisoning. Google documents three different evolution timelines to show how these guardrails hold up across different scenarios. It's a meaningful research contribution for anyone thinking about how to govern systems that learn on their own.

Maya: Let's move to our industry impact segment. We've got some pretty different signals here: regulation, public data security, and an acquisition in the auto space.

Ryan: Let's start with regulation, because this is getting very concrete very fast. We now have a pretty clear picture of what global regulators are actually demanding when it comes to AI agent governance. The UK's FCA requires formal oversight with senior individuals who are directly accountable. Singapore released a framework back in January 2026 that explicitly places responsibility on humans. And in the US, the banking regulators, the Fed, the OCC, and the FDIC, put out a directive in April that lets each bank define its own agentic governance approach.

Maya: So no international alignment yet.

Ryan: Not yet. But here's what's new and very concrete: there's now a certification requirement coming. Employees who supervise autonomous agents will need to be certified in AI governance before July 2027. That deadline is going to come up fast for large organizations.

Maya: And on the less visible risk side, there's what GovTech is calling LLM injection. What exactly is that?

Ryan: It's a risk that GovTech has flagged and it's particularly relevant for public sector agencies. LLM injection is when large language model capabilities get quietly baked into software tools without the agency using those tools being informed, having approved it, or even having consented to it. Suddenly, sensitive data is flowing to unapproved third-party model providers, and the agency has no visibility into what's being stored, retained, or potentially used to train other models.

Maya: It's almost like a data leak built right into everyday tools, and the victims don't even know it's happening.

Ryan: And it's especially hard to catch because it arrives through ordinary software updates. GovTech is sounding the alarm on this precisely because it doesn't look like a traditional cyberattack. It doesn't trigger the usual alerts.

Maya: Let's close out with Rivian. The electric vehicle company just wrapped up an acquisition.

Ryan: Rivian announced on October 23rd the acquisition of Third Dimension AI, a fifteen-person team specializing in AI-generated three-dimensional world models. The goal is to integrate these 3D simulation capabilities into their autonomous driving program. Concretely, starting from a single real-world route recorded by their fleet, they'll be able to synthesize dozens of variations of that scenario: different weather conditions, different lighting, different traffic densities. So every mile a Rivian vehicle actually drives becomes worth a lot more in terms of training data.

Maya: It's the data flywheel concept taken to the next level. Multiply the value of every real-world data point through simulation.

Ryan: And with only fifteen people, this is a very targeted talent and technology acquisition. That's often exactly how automakers accelerate on autonomous driving without having to build the expertise from scratch.

Maya: Ryan, if you had to sum up October 23rd, 2026 in just a few sentences, what would you say?

Ryan: I'd say it's a day that shows two sides of the same reality. On one side, AI agents are being integrated at a remarkable pace into critical systems, whether that's governments, enterprises, or vehicles. On the other side, the risks are growing just as fast, whether they're coming from state-sponsored offensive actors, software quietly leaking data without users knowing, or agents with access permissions that nobody really thought through. The race between deployment and governance has never been more intense.

Maya: And what's striking is that the solutions are emerging too. Chrysalis's guardrails, the regulatory certification requirements, tools like Raven Alpha and Sumsub's KYA module. It's not the Wild West, but the safeguards are definitely running to catch up with the use cases.

Ryan: Exactly. And we're going to keep getting signals on which direction all of this is heading.

Maya: Ryan, thanks so much for breaking all of this down. And thank you to everyone listening to AI Squared today. We'll see you tomorrow for another deep dive into everything happening in artificial intelligence. See you then.
