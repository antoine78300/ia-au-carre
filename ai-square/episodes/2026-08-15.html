Maya: Good morning and welcome to AI Square! It's Saturday, the sun is out, hopefully your coffee is too, and we have got a packed show for you today. Get comfortable, because this is your daily dose of everything happening at the cutting edge of AI, and yes, we show up every single day, weekends included!

Ryan: Hey Maya, hey everyone! I am in full weekend chill mode, but the brain is absolutely firing on all cylinders, because this past week, a lot went down.

Maya: So Ryan, let's just dive straight in with the story that grabbed me the most this week. AI-generated music. Because this is no longer some niche conversation happening in the corners of the internet. This has gone fully mainstream, right?

Ryan: It really has, and it is as fascinating as it is thought-provoking. So here is the picture: we now have virtual artists, entire bands generated by AI, with names like Breaking Rust or Velvet Sundown, putting out full musical compositions. And the unsettling part is that people are listening to this stuff without having any idea it is AI. They genuinely think they are streaming a real band with real musicians and a real backstory.

Maya: Breaking Rust honestly sounds like an alt-rock band from the nineties!

Ryan: That is exactly the point, and that is kind of the sneaky genius of the whole thing. These names are deliberately chosen to sound authentic, to evoke something human, something lived-in. And musically, it is getting harder and harder to tell apart from something a human actually made.

Maya: So what are we actually talking about here, music-wise? Is this background noise or are we talking real songs with melodies, arrangements, lyrics?

Ryan: Real songs. Full tracks with song structures, choruses, bridges, lyrics that make sense. The music generation models have taken enormous leaps in just two or three years. There was a time when AI music had this kind of blurry, slightly off quality to it. Today, some of what is coming out is genuinely jaw-dropping.

Maya: And how are actual musicians taking all of this?

Ryan: Honestly, not well, for a big chunk of them. And this is where it becomes a real societal question. Think about it this way: if you are a composer writing background music for ads, podcasts, video games, apps, that was a massive market. Thousands of musicians made a living doing exactly that. And now, overnight, any company can generate a perfectly decent ambient track in thirty seconds for almost nothing.

Maya: It is kind of what happened with stock photographers, right? People who built entire careers selling their photos through image libraries, and then suddenly AI just generates whatever image you need on demand.

Ryan: That parallel is spot on. And you can see the exact same pattern playing out again. First the most standardized, most repetitive work within a creative field gets wiped out or shrinks dramatically. And then the pressure starts moving up toward more original, more complex creative work.

Maya: But is it necessarily all doom and gloom? Because I have been reading some more nuanced takes on this, particularly coming out of India, where the conversation is actually framed around democratizing creativity.

Ryan: Yeah, and that angle is genuinely interesting. In India, there is a narrative emerging, especially among younger generations, where generative AI is seen as a door-opener rather than a threat. A young musician who cannot afford studio time, who cannot afford to hire an arranger, can now produce something that sounds completely professional using AI tools.

Maya: So AI as the great equalizer, as an empowerment tool for people who never had access to those resources before.

Ryan: Exactly. And that is not mutually exclusive with the fears established artists have. Both things are true at the same time. AI is opening up space for people who had none, and it is closing off space for people who made their living there. And figuring out how to navigate between those two realities is one of the biggest cultural challenges we are facing right now.

Maya: And what about copyright in all of this? Because if an AI generates a track that sounds a whole lot like an existing artist...

Ryan: It is the Wild West legally right now. There is no international consensus, legislation is moving at a snail's pace, and in the meantime platforms are just getting flooded with AI-generated content. Some estimates suggest that within a few years, the majority of music published online will be artificial in origin. And that raises a fundamental question: does the value of a piece of music come from its intrinsic quality, or from the fact that a human being suffered, sweated, and lived to create it?

Maya: Now there is a philosophical question to kick off your Saturday morning! We are actually going to come back to that in our What If segment a little later. But first, let's talk about experiments and projects that are making moves right now. Ryan, what surprised you on the robotics front this week?

Ryan: So I have got two things that really caught my attention. First one: a company called CiDi, which comes out of the autonomous driving world, just dropped its first financial results after going public. And they are showing 97% revenue growth.

Maya: 97%! That is not a rounding error.

Ryan: That is massive. And what is really interesting is that CiDi is in the middle of a pivot. They are moving from autonomous vehicles into what they call heavy embodied intelligence. Basically robots designed to operate in tough industrial environments: construction sites, warehouses, factories. Places where you need serious durability, precision, and the ability to navigate a genuinely complex physical world.

Maya: Heavy embodied intelligence, I actually love that phrase. It paints a picture of a robot that is not just a fixed arm on a factory rail, but something far more autonomous, far more mobile.

Ryan: Exactly. And this fits into a much broader trend we are watching across Asia-Pacific. The humanoid robot and physical AI market is booming. And what is really striking in the market analysis is that manufacturers are making a strategic shift. They used to make most of their money on hardware, on the physical robot itself. Now a growing slice of revenue is coming from software, updates, subscriptions, the embedded intelligence.

Maya: That is basically the Apple model. You sell the phone, but you also make your money on the App Store and services.

Ryan: Exact same playbook. And it completely changes the economic logic. A robot whose intelligence keeps improving through software updates is a robot that gets more and more useful without you ever having to buy a new one.

Maya: And it also means these robots can potentially learn new behaviors remotely, getting updated just like your phone.

Ryan: Right. And that is where the line between software and physical hardware gets genuinely blurry. We are building things that are simultaneously physical objects and evolving services. That is a pretty profound shift in how we think about what a product even is.

Maya: Alright, let's move on to something I am really passionate about, which is science. Because this week there were some developments that honestly gave me chills. AI and scientific discovery are really starting to accelerate.

Ryan: Yeah, and there are two levels to this. The first is the big-picture level. The architects of AI, meaning the people actually building the most advanced systems, are describing an ongoing transformation of cognitive work. And it is touching professions that we long assumed were safe from automation: lawyers, software developers, diagnostic physicians.

Maya: Hold on. Diagnostic physicians? We are talking about an enormous level of complexity here.

Ryan: And yet. Medical diagnosis at its core is pattern recognition. You take symptoms, biological data, medical imaging, and you match them against known patterns. And AI systems are becoming extraordinarily good at exactly that, often outperforming humans in very specific areas like reading radiology scans or detecting certain cancers in images.

Maya: So we are not talking about replacing the doctor who holds your hand and walks you through your treatment plan, but more the pure analysis layer.

Ryan: That is the key distinction. What seems to be taking shape is a model where human oversight replaces routine cognitive execution. The doctor validates, contextualizes, communicates, makes the complex calls. But that first layer of analysis, AI does it better and faster.

Maya: And on the pure scientific discovery side, there was some really exciting news too.

Ryan: Very exciting. NVIDIA and Microsoft are significantly deepening their partnerships with pharmaceutical labs, particularly around genomics, medical imaging, and drug discovery. And the game-changer here is speed. A discovery that used to take ten years of research could potentially happen in two or three years when AI is handling a significant portion of the hypothesis exploration.

Maya: That is almost dizzying to think about. Because if you compress discovery timelines like that, you are talking about treating diseases that currently have no treatment.

Ryan: And the market numbers are staggering. Precision medicine, meaning treatments tailored to each patient's individual genetic profile, is projected to exceed 25 billion dollars within the next few years. And AI is the central engine driving all of it.

Maya: There was also an event that caught my eye, a summit coming up in Singapore in October. AI for Science.

Ryan: Yes! Nobel Heroes Day 2026 is launching a summit entirely dedicated to scientific AI in Singapore this October. And what I love about this initiative is that it acknowledges something really important: the major scientific breakthroughs of tomorrow are not going to come from laboratories alone. They require collaboration between researchers, entrepreneurs, and policymakers. Because discovering something is only half the battle. It still has to be developed, funded, and deployed at scale.

Maya: Singapore positioning itself as a hub for this kind of thinking makes total sense given what we have been watching across Asia-Pacific these past few years.

Ryan: Completely. And it is a strong signal that this conversation is gaining serious institutional weight. We are not in the realm of tech curiosity anymore. We are squarely in the territory of global science policy.

Maya: I love it. And now we have made it to my favorite part of the show: the What If segment. Ryan, what are you bringing us today?

Ryan: So I want to pick up a thread from what we were talking about with music earlier. That question you raised: does the value of a work come from its quality, or from the fact that a human created it? And I want to zoom out and apply that to something much bigger. What if the real transformation AI is bringing is not economic or technological, but philosophical?

Maya: Ooh. Keep going.

Ryan: We have spent centuries defining human value through the ability to produce, to create, to think. First physical labor, mechanized by the Industrial Revolution. And now cognitive labor, getting automated in turn. So here is the question I keep coming back to: if AI can compose music, diagnose disease, draft legal contracts, and maybe soon conduct scientific research, what is left that is distinctly human? What do we actually cherish and find meaningful about human creation?

Maya: And do you have an answer?

Ryan: Not a definitive one, but I have a strong intuition. I think what we are going to rediscover how to value is presence. The fact that a human being actually lived through something, chose to share it, took a risk in putting it out there. AI can replicate the output, but it cannot replicate the intentionality, the vulnerability, the human context behind a piece of work.

Maya: It is like preferring a live concert to a studio album. Not necessarily because it is technically superior, but because there are real people on that stage who can mess up, who are electric, who are actually there.

Ryan: Exactly. And maybe in a world saturated with AI-generated content, the scarcity of authentic human presence will become something genuinely precious. Maybe even a luxury. Which creates this delicious irony: the more AI spreads, the more a human being just being human might actually become a rising value.

Maya: Honestly, that is a pretty reassuring way to think about it on a Saturday morning.

Ryan: I do what I can!
