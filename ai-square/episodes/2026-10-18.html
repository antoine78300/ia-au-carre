Emma: Good morning and welcome to AI Squared, your daily dose of everything artificial intelligence! It is Sunday, it is early, and honestly it is way too soon to be thinking hard about anything — and yet, as always, AI did not take the weekend off. I'm Emma, joined as always on weekends by Liam, our AI analyst. We've got coffee, we've got energy, and we've got stories that are genuinely worth getting out of bed for. Let's get into it!

Emma: So Liam, good news for everyone listening in their pajamas right now: you do not have to pretend to be fully awake, because the topics we're covering today are the kind that pull you in all on their own.

Liam: That is exactly the vibe I needed to hear. And to ease us in, I want to start with something that touches a lot of people's everyday lives: music. Specifically, how streaming platforms are starting to grapple with the absolute flood of AI-generated music hitting their libraries.

Emma: Right, because we are talking about a genuinely staggering volume of content here.

Liam: Staggering is the right word. Deezer has announced it's building a global, reversible filter that lets users choose to include or exclude entirely AI-generated music from their search results, and it should roll out before the end of 2026. And to understand why that actually matters, you need one number in your head: ninety thousand new tracks are being uploaded to streaming platforms every single day.

Emma: Ninety thousand. Every day. That is almost impossible to wrap your mind around.

Liam: Think of it this way — it's roughly like a music library doubling in size every single week. And a growing slice of that is coming from tools like Creatune.ai and AIMusicCreator.io, which now accept text descriptions, lyrics, reference tracks, even video clips as a starting point. You describe a mood, and within seconds you have a finished track.

Emma: And that is exactly the problem Deezer is trying to solve, by putting that choice in the listener's hands?

Liam: Exactly. And what makes their approach interesting is how it compares to what competitors are doing. YouTube Music, for example, uses static labels. You see a little tag that says AI-generated, and that's it. Passive. Deezer is going a step further by giving users active control. You decide what shows up in your searches, and you can change your mind whenever you want.

Emma: That is a pretty different philosophy. They're not banning it, they're not hiding it — they're just handing the wheel to the listener.

Liam: And that is where it gets almost philosophical. Is AI-generated music real music? Does it belong on the same platforms? By offering this filter, Deezer is essentially saying: that's not our call to make, it's yours. And that is a pretty bold editorial stance.

Emma: Honestly, I think it's a pretty elegant solution. Especially because it sidesteps that endless debate about artistic legitimacy.

Liam: Completely. And for content creators, small businesses, composers who just need to crank out demos quickly, these tools are very real and very practical. And there's no copyright headache either, since AI-generated outputs don't carry the same protections as human-created works. So you've got an entire ecosystem forming, and it's forming fast.

Emma: Alright, let's move to our experiments of the week, because I have a story that genuinely stopped me in my tracks. We're talking about robots that can feel what they touch.

Liam: EmArm! I was waiting for us to get here. So EmArm is a robotic arm developed by a research team, and what sets it apart from anything we've seen before is that it has tactile feedback built in — feedback that actually mimics human sensitivity. Not just a 3D camera to see objects, but a real sense of physical contact.

Emma: What does that mean in practice? Like the robot actually feels that it's holding something?

Liam: Exactly. And that is a huge deal when you're trying to manipulate complex objects in environments that aren't perfectly controlled. Up until now, 3D vision let a robot locate an object, calculate a path, and grab it. But if the object is soft, or fragile, or the surface is not what it expected, the robot would struggle. With multimodal tactile perception, the arm adjusts its grip in real time based on what it is, quote, feeling.

Emma: Like how our hands instinctively squeeze harder or ease up without us even thinking about it.

Liam: Exactly like that. And almost at the same time, we're hearing about a former head of foundation models from Huawei's automotive division who just launched a startup in Hangzhou, with close to a hundred million yuan in funding, entirely focused on this kind of tactile manipulation intelligence.

Emma: So serious money is starting to flow in this direction?

Liam: Very serious money. And the ambition behind this startup is striking. They're building what they call an embodied brain. The idea is to use a closed loop of multimodal data — dominated by haptic sensation and vision — combined with what they describe as a physical inverse world model. In plain terms: the robot isn't just following instructions. It's building its own representation of physical reality and learning from its own experience in real industrial environments.

Emma: It learns by doing. Like we do.

Liam: Like we do, yeah. And the promise is that the robot continuously builds up practical experience on its own, without a human needing to reprogram it every time it runs into a situation it hasn't seen before. Which is a lot more efficient than calling tech support every time something unexpected happens on the factory floor.

Emma: And obviously this is something industry is paying close attention to. Manufacturing, logistics...

Liam: Absolutely. The European market for embodied AI — meaning robots that act physically in the real world — is projected to hit eight point seven billion dollars by 2034. Players like ABB, Siemens, and Universal Robots are already in the race. This is a sector that is scaling up at an incredible pace.

Emma: Let's shift now to our society and science segment, because I've got two stories I really want to dig into. First up: Google and its healthcare ambitions. And second: can AI actually replace scientists?

Liam: Let's start with Google, because the numbers they've put out are genuinely impressive. Their MedGemma model, built for clinical health applications, has been downloaded more than ten million times by sixty-five thousand innovators around the world. That is a very strong signal of real-world adoption.

Emma: And they're not stopping there?

Liam: Not even close. They're expanding their AMIE system — which was originally designed for initial diagnosis — into long-term disease management. And this is where things get genuinely new: they're running national randomized studies of real-time virtual consultations through a platform called Included Health. The AI isn't just saying here's what you might have — it's walking with the patient over time.

Emma: And it can read medical images, lab results, all of that at once?

Liam: That's exactly it. We're talking about multimodal clinical recommendations, meaning the AI agent is combining medical imaging with lab results to produce an analysis. And this is happening in real care settings, not just research labs.

Emma: Which leads right into the second question: if AI is handling consultations, diagnoses, and now potentially running scientific experiments on its own — are scientists becoming obsolete?

Liam: That is the question being raised by what are called AutoLabs. These are platforms capable of designing, running, and analyzing physical experiments autonomously. And AI being connected to real lab equipment is not science fiction in 2026 — it's happening. But we have to be honest about the limits.

Emma: What kind of limits?

Liam: These systems can still fall apart when faced with real uncertainty. If an experiment produces an unexpected result that's outside their training environment, they can get lost. And even if an AI identifies a promising pharmaceutical compound, clinical trials and regulatory approval still have to happen. AI can accelerate discovery, but it does not shortcut human validation.

Emma: So the scientist changes roles rather than disappears?

Liam: That's the way I'd frame it. More conductor than instrumentalist. And you see this same dynamic playing out geopolitically in healthcare. Abu Dhabi just wrapped up an inaugural visit to China with a health delegation led by the undersecretary of the health department, exploring collaboration on fully AI-native hospitals, surgical robotics, lab automation, advanced imaging, and even brain-computer interfaces.

Emma: Medical AI is becoming a diplomatic issue.

Liam: And an industrial one. Abu Dhabi is positioning itself as a proving ground for agentic AI in integrated healthcare. This is not about one isolated AI app here or there — it's a whole-system vision of what the hospital of the future looks like.

Emma: And to wrap us up, our What If segment. This is honestly my favorite part of the show because it's where we actually let ourselves think out loud. Liam, what's been rattling around in your head this week?

Liam: So here's what I keep coming back to. We talked about robots that feel what they touch, AI systems that run scientific experiments, medical models that follow patients over months and years. And at the same time, we have Deezer building a button that lets users say I want AI-generated music in my feed, or I don't.

Emma: And you see a thread connecting all of that?

Liam: I see a fascinating tension. On one side, AI is becoming more and more embedded in the physical world — in bodies, in labs, in hospitals. It touches things, it treats people, it experiments. On the other side, we're starting to build spaces where humans can choose to keep it at arm's length, to filter its presence, to decide how far it comes into their lives. And my What If question is this: what if the next major breakthrough isn't a more powerful AI, but a better shared language for deciding where AI belongs and where it doesn't?

Emma: Almost like a right to be left alone. A right not to be optimized.

Liam: Exactly. Because look at the creative bootcamp organized by SpikeStory Limited at the University of Dhaka in Bangladesh. Students learning to weave generative AI into their own creative work. This is not a Western tech hub — this is Dhaka. And that tells you something important: AI is spreading everywhere, into wildly different contexts, with different needs and different cultures. The real question might not be how far does AI go, but who gets to decide how it fits into each specific context. And that is a deeply human question.

Emma: That is a great note to land on for a Sunday. Liam, thank you as always — you have this way of making the complicated feel accessible and the accessible feel genuinely fascinating.

Liam: That's what Sunday morning coffee is for.

Emma: Couldn't agree more. Thanks so much for spending part of your Sunday with us. If you've got questions, reactions, or topics you want us to dig into, you know where to find us. And don't forget — a brand new episode is waiting for you tomorrow. See you then!
