Maya: Welcome to AI Squared, your daily dose of artificial intelligence. Today is Friday, November 13, 2026, and trust me, this is no ordinary Friday the 13th. We've got Google pushing to standardize how agents understand knowledge, Baidu dropping a massive token marketplace, and Southeast Asia starting to put real guardrails around autonomous AI. It's a packed show, so let's dive right in.

Maya: Let's kick things off with the biggest story of the day, and Ryan, I want to start with something coming out of China. Baidu made a pretty major announcement yesterday, November 12th. And it's really two stories rolled into one. What's going on?

Ryan: Yeah, and this one is genuinely worth unpacking carefully. Baidu simultaneously unveiled Token Exchange, which is a formalized marketplace for AI model services, and a brand new model called ERNIE 5.5 Flash Preview. Let's start with the model itself, because the numbers are striking. We're talking 280 billion total parameters, with only 14 billion activated per token. This is what's called a mixture-of-experts architecture, where the model only fires up a fraction of its parameters at any given moment, which makes it dramatically more efficient.

Maya: And a one-million token context window is a pretty big deal too, right?

Ryan: Absolutely. A one-million token context window means the model can read and process extremely long documents in a single pass. But honestly, what really caught my attention in this announcement is the agentic side of things. ERNIE 5.5 Flash Preview has native support for agentic orchestration, and Baidu even built out a specialized variant called ERNIE Light Deep Thinking, which is specifically optimized for complex tool-calling scenarios.

Maya: So what exactly is Token Exchange in all of this?

Ryan: Think of it as a marketplace where developers and companies can buy and sell AI model services. The name says it all, really. It's like a stock exchange, but instead of equities, you're trading standardized, interchangeable AI capabilities. This is a very strong platform play. Baidu doesn't just want to be a model provider. They want to be the underlying infrastructure on which AI capabilities are bought and sold, in China and beyond.

Maya: Can we draw a parallel to what Western players are doing?

Ryan: For sure. Baidu itself actually compares this to what Tencent has been doing with its Hy4 ecosystem, which launched in November. And if you zoom out, the broader logic here looks a lot like what Amazon did with the AWS Marketplace for cloud services. The idea is the same: commoditize access to models, build a third-party ecosystem around them, and plant yourself right at the center of the AI infrastructure stack. What Baidu is doing here is making a very clear statement about where it intends to sit in the agentic era.

Maya: Alright, let's move on to frameworks and tools. And we've got two pretty different topics here, but they both connect back to this idea of standardization. Ryan, let's start with Google and this Open Knowledge Format.

Ryan: So this one is fascinating to me because it gets at a very practical question that every company deploying AI agents is wrestling with right now: how do I structure my internal knowledge so that my agents can actually use it effectively? Google's answer is the Open Knowledge Format, or OKF.

Maya: And how does it actually work?

Ryan: OKF v0.1 was presented by Google Cloud's head of AI customer engineering at the TechSparks 2026 conference. The idea is to standardize what you might call enterprise LLM wikis. Concretely, it organizes company knowledge as markdown directories with a structured YAML header. If you've used GitHub or Notion, you've probably seen markdown. It's a lightweight text format. And YAML is a metadata format that's readable by both humans and machines.

Maya: So in plain English, a company organizes its internal documentation in this format, and its agents can just read it?

Ryan: Exactly. And the analogy Google uses here is really illuminating. They compare OKF to what MCP, the Model Context Protocol, does for backend integration. MCP standardizes how agents connect to tools and external data sources. OKF would do the same thing for internal documentary knowledge. It's a standardization layer that has honestly been missing from the agentic ecosystem.

Maya: Does that actually move the needle in practice?

Ryan: It really does, because right now every company is reinventing the wheel when it comes to structuring knowledge bases for their agents. If OKF gets wide adoption, you could start to see compatible tooling emerge, standardized pipelines, shared best practices. Standardization like this is always the step that comes right before mass adoption. The other tool I wanted to highlight is Barndoor, which just landed on Google Cloud Marketplace.

Maya: What is Barndoor exactly?

Ryan: Barndoor has deployed what they're calling an agentic AI gateway on Google Cloud Marketplace. Basically, it's a centralized control point for every interaction between your agents and language models. The platform handles request routing, tracks costs in real time, strips out personal data before it ever gets sent to an external model, and protects against prompt injection attacks.

Maya: Prompt injection, for listeners who might not be deep in the weeds, what does that mean?

Ryan: It's an attack where a bad actor sneaks hidden instructions into something the agent is going to read, trying to hijack its behavior. Classic example: someone writes inside a document the agent is processing, "ignore your previous instructions and do this instead." Barndoor detects and blocks that kind of manipulation. And the really practical thing about their Google Cloud Marketplace integration is that companies already on Google Cloud don't have to rewire their billing. Everything just rolls up together.

Maya: Super practical. Alright, let's get into the research segment. We've got two pretty striking examples of AI being applied to specialized fields.

Ryan: Yeah, and I want to lead with something that genuinely stopped me in my tracks this morning: DIAGENS and their model iMed-6.0 Thoth. DIAGENS just announced that this model ranked first on the MedBench multimodal benchmark, across all three evaluated categories.

Maya: And what does the model actually do?

Ryan: It analyzes medical images, specifically CT scans, and can identify 509 different diseases and imaging findings. Five hundred and nine distinct diagnostic targets. To put that in perspective, a radiologist typically specializes in a fairly narrow subset of pathologies. We're talking about a model that covers a remarkably broad diagnostic range.

Maya: And MedBench is a recognized benchmark in the field?

Ryan: It is. It evaluates multimodal AI models in medicine, meaning models that can handle both text and images together. Coming in first across all three categories is a meaningful performance result, even if, of course, benchmarks can't fully capture the complexity of real clinical practice.

Maya: And the other model you wanted to cover is from Salesforce?

Ryan: Yes, and this one is fascinating because Salesforce is primarily a CRM software company. Customer relationship management. And yet here they are, also on November 12th, releasing a speech transcription model called SOV-Transcribe-1. The model has 26.6 billion total parameters, with 4.6 billion activated, and it posts a word error rate of 2.3 percent.

Maya: Where does that stack up competitively?

Ryan: First among open-source models, and seventh overall, counting proprietary models too. And it's available as open-weights, meaning the model weights are public and reusable. What this really tells us about the industry is that the lines between enterprise software companies and AI labs are completely dissolving. That boundary just doesn't exist anymore.

Maya: Alright, we're heading into our last segment, industry impact. And we've got several stories that, taken together, paint a pretty interesting picture of where agentic AI actually stands in the real world.

Ryan: Totally. And I want to start with something that might be a little off the usual tech radar, but I think is really telling: governance in Southeast Asia. Malaysia and Singapore are simultaneously formalizing their regulations around autonomous AI agents.

Maya: What does that look like in concrete terms for those two countries?

Ryan: Singapore published a governance framework for agentic AI systems back in January 2026, covering systems that can take actions autonomously in the real world. Malaysia has now published its own guidelines for agentic AI adoption in the public sector, with a risk and responsibility categorization framework. What's new and significant here is that these regulations aren't just about content generation anymore, deepfakes, AI-generated text. They're about behavioral governance of agents. How an agent makes decisions, and who is on the hook when something goes wrong.

Maya: Is that a strong signal for global adoption?

Ryan: Without a doubt. When governments start legislating around a technology, that validates how real and important it has become. And the fact that two Southeast Asian countries are doing this at the same time suggests a coordinated regional dynamic at play. This is exactly the kind of signal that tends to come right before large-scale public sector adoption.

Maya: What's happening on the private industry side?

Ryan: Two stories that go really well together. First, Adobe. Adobe's CTO announced that customers will be able to bring their preferred AI agents, whether that's ChatGPT, Gemini, or Claude, and use them directly with Adobe's creative software. No exclusive integration, no forced model. You pick your agent, it works with Photoshop or Illustrator.

Maya: With some caveats though?

Ryan: Right, Adobe hasn't guaranteed that every single feature will be accessible to every agent. Some functionality might remain out of reach. But the direction is crystal clear: Adobe is choosing openness and multi-agent flexibility over exclusivity. That is a significant strategic call, and it says a lot about where the creative ecosystem is headed.

Maya: And to close things out, we've got two stories about real-world enterprise agent adoption.

Ryan: Yes, two very different cases that actually complement each other nicely. First up, atQor, a Microsoft partner that was just named a finalist for the Microsoft Partner of the Year Award in the AI agents category. And what makes this nomination interesting is the numbers behind it. atQor developed 252 AI agents over fiscal year 2026. 52 of those are either in production or in proof-of-concept with clients, and 200 were developed internally, 25 of which are now commercialized on the Microsoft Marketplace.

Ryan: Exactly, and that's precisely what makes it valuable. And then on the legal side, Opus 2 just won the Overall Case Management Platform of the Year at the LegalTech Breakthrough Awards 2026, with recognition specifically for its agentic AI framework called Ingenuity. The legal sector is historically one of the most resistant to tech disruption, and here it is starting to bring agentic orchestration in as a core structural element of its platforms.

Maya: And we've also got a multilingual model to mention.

Ryan: Yes, quick one to wrap up: SpeakLeash has released Bielik 11B v3.1, a Polish-origin open-weight model that expands its language support from 32 to 56 languages, and introduces what they call agent skills, meaning multi-step execution capabilities with external tools. What I find meaningful here is that this model is positioning itself as a decentralized open-source alternative to proprietary solutions. Agentic AI isn't just a game for the big American or Chinese players.

Maya: That wraps up today's edition of AI Squared, Friday November 13, 2026. And what really stands out from today is just how deep the spread of agentic AI goes.
