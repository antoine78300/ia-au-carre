Full transcript

Maya: What if your AI tools were making decisions on your behalf, and nobody in your company even knew it was happening? Welcome to AI Square, the daily podcast that breaks down artificial intelligence and what it means for our world. Today, we've got revelations that should have every IT director sitting up straight, a two-hundred-dollar-a-month AI agent coming from Meta, and models that can literally improve themselves. Let's get into it.

Maya: Ryan, good morning. I want to start with a number that stopped me cold this morning: four out of five AI tools inside companies are running with zero IT oversight. Is that really what we're looking at?

Ryan: Good morning Maya. That's exactly right, and this comes out today from Infosecurity Magazine. The study is pretty alarming. In small and mid-sized businesses, researchers found an average of four hundred and fourteen unauthorized AI tools for every thousand employees. That means workers are downloading, installing, and using AI tools entirely on their own, without IT or management having any idea.

Maya: Four hundred and fourteen unauthorized tools per thousand employees. That's a staggering number. But let's make it real — why is this actually dangerous?

Ryan: Think about what these tools are doing on a daily basis. They're processing customer data, contracts, financial information, sometimes personal data that's protected under privacy regulations. If those tools haven't been vetted by IT, nobody knows where that data is going. Is it sitting on a third-party server? Is it being used to train other models? The honest answer is: we don't know.

Maya: The study also flags risks tied to something called MCP, the Model Context Protocol. We've talked about this before on the show, but for anyone hearing that term for the first time, Ryan, what exactly is MCP?

Ryan: MCP is a standard that lets AI agents connect to external data sources, databases, business tools, APIs. Think of it like a universal adapter that plugs an AI agent into basically any information source you want. Incredibly powerful. But the study points out that when this protocol is used without oversight, it opens up real vulnerabilities. A misconfigured tool could potentially reach data it was never supposed to touch.

Maya: So the takeaway for companies is that they need to urgently map out what's actually being used?

Ryan: Absolutely. And this isn't about blocking innovation, it's actually the opposite. The companies that get this right are the ones building a framework of trust: a vetted list of approved tools, clear rules about what data can flow through them, and real employee training. Just banning things without explaining why doesn't work. People find workarounds. You have to bring them along.

Maya: Thanks Ryan. Let's move into our tools and frameworks segment, and we're actually staying on the MCP thread because there's a very concrete real-world example from the HR world. Ryan, G-P just launched its own MCP server. Who are they and why does this matter?

Ryan: G-P, formerly known as Globalization Partners, is a major player in what's called Employer of Record services. Basically, they let companies hire employees in other countries without having to set up a local legal entity. They handle legal compliance, contracts, payroll across dozens of countries. And they just announced an MCP server that connects all of that global HR data directly to the major AI models: Anthropic's Claude, OpenAI's ChatGPT, Google's Gemini.

Maya: So an HR director could ask their AI assistant and get real-time answers about hiring rules in Germany, Brazil, or South Korea?

Ryan: Exactly. And that's where this gets genuinely useful. Picture this: you're a fast-growing startup and you want to hire a developer in Singapore. What are the legal notice periods? What are the payroll taxes? What are the mandatory leave requirements? Right now, an HR leader has to wade through complex legal documents or bring in an outside consultant. With this kind of MCP integration, the AI agent can answer in seconds with up-to-date information.

Maya: And G-P is putting a lot of emphasis on compliance, right? Because in HR, mistakes can be incredibly costly.

Ryan: Compliance is the centerpiece of their whole announcement. That's precisely the value-add here. It's not just about accessing data, it's about accessing reliable, verified data that's contextualized against the actual local laws in effect. The promise is that AI agents won't give you bad HR legal advice, which, honestly, is a very real risk when you let a general-purpose model answer highly specialized questions like these.

Maya: We've also got another tool making waves today, and for a pretty different reason. Perplexity is launching something called Portable Computer, an AI agent that runs directly on your device. Ryan, is on-device AI a real turning point?

Ryan: It's a deep underlying trend that's really picking up speed. The idea with Portable Computer is to run an AI agent entirely on your phone or your laptop, no remote servers involved. Perplexity is using lightweight models like Qwen 3.8 and NVIDIA Nemotron 3.5 Lightning, which are designed to run efficiently with limited hardware resources.

Maya: And what's the practical upside for the user?

Ryan: Two big ones. First, privacy: your data never leaves your device. No more uncertainty about what's being sent off to some company's servers. Second, cost: no per-token fees, no per-query charges. You can use the agent as much as you want with no additional cost. For heavy users, that's a genuinely game-changing shift.

Maya: Let's pivot to the research side. Ryan, Deep Cogito just raised forty-three million dollars, and their goal is ambitious: AI models that improve themselves.

Ryan: Deep Cogito is a startup founded by researchers from some leading institutions, and their approach is fascinating. They're building what they call IDA architecture, which stands for Iterated Distillation and Amplification. The core idea is that a model can learn from its own outputs and improve autonomously, without a human needing to step in at every stage to correct it or retrain it.

Maya: Okay, I have to ask, because this is the part that makes a lot of people nervous. A model that improves itself on its own — doesn't that sound a little too close to some sci-fi nightmare scenario we've all heard about?

Ryan: The concern is completely fair. But we need to add some important context. What we're talking about here is self-improvement within a very controlled framework, on specific defined tasks. This is not a model deciding on its own what to learn or what goals to pursue. The researchers define the improvement metrics, the guardrails, the boundaries. What's new is the efficiency of the process: less human intervention needed to get better results on well-defined tasks.

Maya: And the forty-three million dollars, where is that actually going?

Ryan: Mainly three places. One, computing infrastructure, because these architectures are extremely resource-intensive. Two, hiring researchers who specialize in reinforcement learning. And three, developing real-world industrial applications. Deep Cogito is targeting fields where continuous improvement is critical: cybersecurity, fraud detection, logistics optimization. Domains where conditions are constantly shifting and a model that adapts automatically has genuine, tangible value.

Maya: This is one of the most interesting funding rounds of the month of August. And it really underscores that fundamental AI research is still very much on fire.

Ryan: Absolutely. And Google is not sitting on the sidelines when it comes to innovation either. They just announced Gemini 3.5 Transcribe, a model purpose-built for voice transcription. What sets it apart from existing solutions is that it doesn't just transcribe word for word. It automatically cleans up the filler words, the ums, the self-corrections, the false starts, and delivers clean, readable text right out of the gate.

Maya: Okay, that is something that everyone who has ever transcribed a meeting has been dreaming about for years.

Ryan: And it's rolling out first through Gboard Rambler, Google's virtual keyboard, before making its way into Chrome. That means this feature is going to reach hundreds of millions of users very quickly, with no third-party app required. Smart transcription is becoming a built-in, baseline feature of the operating environment.

Maya: Let's close out with the story that's been generating the most buzz in the industry today: Meta is getting ready to launch an AI agent called Hatch. Ryan, what exactly is this and why is the price point turning so many heads?

Ryan: Meta is building something very different from their current chatbot. Hatch would be a next-generation AI agent capable of completing complex tasks autonomously, not just answering questions. And according to internal documents that The Next Web was able to review, Meta is considering a premium tier priced at a hundred and ninety-nine dollars and ninety-nine cents per month.

Maya: A hundred and ninety-nine dollars a month. That is twenty-five times the price of their current AI subscription. That is a serious signal.

Ryan: A very serious one. It tells you first that Meta is betting on a high-value AI productivity market, not just the general consumer looking for a basic conversational assistant. At that price point, the targets are professionals, heavy-duty content creators, consultants, entrepreneurs who are willing to pay a premium if the tool saves them several hours every single day.

Maya: And there's a name showing up in the coverage, OpenClaw. Who exactly is that?

Ryan: OpenClaw is an AI agent from OpenAI that launched earlier this year, positioned in that same space of high-end autonomous agents. Meta is clearly coming after that market head-on. What's really interesting is what it reveals about a broader convergence: every major platform, Google with its Gemini agents, OpenAI with OpenClaw, Meta with Hatch, is now fighting over the same territory. The territory of agents that don't just respond, they act.

Maya: Does Meta have a specific edge in this fight?

Ryan: They actually have two. First, their massive user base across WhatsApp, Instagram, and Facebook. If Hatch is baked into those platforms, the distribution is instant, we're talking billions of users. And second, their open-source model Llama, which lets them control the entire technology stack, cut computing costs, and move faster on certain types of innovation. The AI agent wars have genuinely shifted into a higher gear.

Maya: That's a wrap on today's episode of AI Square. Let's do a quick rundown of what we covered: four out of five AI tools inside companies are running without oversight, and that's a security wake-up call nobody can afford to ignore. MCP is moving into human resources with G-P bringing real-time global employment data to AI agents. On-device AI is making a comeback with Perplexity Portable Computer. Deep Cogito raised forty-three million dollars to build models that improve themselves. And Meta is getting ready to launch a two-hundred-dollar-a-month AI agent that could shake up the entire competitive landscape. Ryan, as always, thank you for the analysis. Really sharp stuff today.

Ryan: Thanks Maya. And if there's one thing to take away from today: agentic AI is no longer some distant horizon, it's happening right now, and putting clear rules around it has never been more urgent.

Maya: We'll see you tomorrow for another episode of AI Square. Until then, stay curious, stay informed, and if you got something out of today's show, share it with someone who needs to hear it. See you tomorrow!
