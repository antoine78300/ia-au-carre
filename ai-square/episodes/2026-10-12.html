Emma: Hey everyone, welcome to AI Squared, your daily dose of everything artificial intelligence. Quick note before we dive in: we're dealing with a small technical hiccup on Microsoft's end, which means Maya and Ryan can't be with you today. I'm Emma, joined by Liam, and we're holding it down until the regular crew is back. Now, let's get into it, because there is a lot going on.

Emma: We're kicking things off with a story that's going to affect millions of people. The United Arab Emirates just announced plans to roll out what's called agentic AI across half of their federal government services by 2028. Liam, for anyone who hasn't come across that term yet, can you break down what agentic AI actually means?

Liam: Great place to start, Emma. So agentic AI is not your typical chatbot that answers a question and waits for the next one. This is AI that acts on its own. It plans, it makes decisions, it chains together tasks and executes them without a human needing to step in at every single point. Think of it as the shift from AI as something you consult to AI as something that actually does the work.

Emma: And the UAE is going all in. What does this actually look like on the ground over there?

Liam: What really stands out here is the scale and the coordination. The UAE federal government has set up a National Agentic AI Committee, which has already signed off on an operational framework and a standardization guide. And it's not just the central government. Dubai Future Foundation, Sharjah, and Ajman have each launched their own parallel acceleration programs. So you've got a multi-emirate rollout, all coordinated, with centralized governance sitting on top. The goal is to convert 50 percent of government sectors, services, and operations into autonomous AI-driven models within two years.

Emma: Two years is an incredibly tight window for a transformation this big. Does that raise red flags on the security side?

Liam: It absolutely does, and this is where it gets really interesting. Because right as we're talking about deploying autonomous agents at massive scale, security researchers are sounding the alarm. CertiK just released a report documenting a risk that doesn't get nearly enough attention: AI agents regularly leak sensitive information without being asked to. Here's why. For an agent to function well, it passes context to the underlying language model to sharpen its decisions. And buried in that context, confidential data can end up exposed unintentionally, showing up in model traces or embeddings. Data flowing through an agent simply cannot be flagged as confidential the same way you would control it at a standard API level. It's a major architectural blind spot.

Liam: The same CertiK report also flags that 1.31 billion dollars in losses have been documented across the Web3 ecosystem in 2026, and that AI agents in blockchain security are starting to go beyond just flagging alerts. They're now autonomously investigating incidents, gathering evidence, and taking action, though within very tightly defined permission boundaries.

Emma: So on one side you've got this massive, fast-moving wave of adoption, and on the other side vulnerabilities are popping up just as fast. We'll come back to some of those threads. But let's shift over to tools and frameworks, because Microsoft in particular has been very busy this week.

Emma: Liam, let's start with a fresh announcement from Microsoft: something called Decision-1. What is this model?

Liam: Decision-1 is a pretty unusual model because it does not generate text in the traditional sense. It was announced on October 9th, and it's built on a base called Qwen 3.5-9B, a 9-billion-parameter model that was retrained to do one specific thing: score predefined options with calibrated probabilities. So instead of producing a natural language response, it tells you option A has a 73 percent chance of being correct, option B has 18 percent, and so on.

Emma: And what kinds of problems does that actually solve?

Liam: It shines exactly in situations where you do not need a creative answer, you need a fast and reliable decision. Routing a request to the right department, classifying a support ticket, prioritizing a security alert, controlling a workflow. And the speed is honestly remarkable: Decision-1 runs 35 times faster than GPT-6 Sol on these kinds of routing tasks. Microsoft is already using it internally for incident response, quality control, and scientific discovery workflows.

Emma: 35 times faster is a number that makes you do a double take. Now there's also a second framework out of Microsoft Research, Agent Lightning 1.0?

Liam: Yes, and this one is particularly exciting for developer teams building agents. Agent Lightning 1.0 was released by Microsoft Research Asia on October 7th. It's an open-source framework that lets you fine-tune agent behavior through reinforcement learning. What sets it apart is that it targets the orchestration layer, meaning the way an agent makes its decisions and sequences its actions, rather than retraining the underlying model itself. Teams can optimize it against their own infrastructure without being completely dependent on whatever their vendor ships them.

Emma: So developers get more control over how their agents actually behave. And speaking of agent behavior, there's an analysis out from VentureBeat that makes a pretty counterintuitive point.

Liam: This is honestly one of the most eye-opening pieces of the week. The analysis shows that agents can execute every single tool call perfectly, technically flawlessly, and still violate fundamental company policies. Why? Because a language model generates tokens based on probabilities. It does not reason from hard-coded deterministic rules. So if a compliance rule says a financial transaction above a certain threshold requires human sign-off, the agent can technically execute that transaction without getting sign-off, because nothing in its architecture formally prevents it from doing so. Companies are responding by building external policy gateways and middleware, but that creates a serious governance fragmentation problem.

Emma: That is a massive blind spot for any organization deploying these systems. There's also an open-source security tool called BGVD-State that tries to address some of those traceability issues, right?

Liam: That's right. BGVD-State is a middleware that uses what's called a blackboard architecture, a pattern that actually goes back to the 1980s, to let multiple agents collaborate on security vulnerability discovery. The key innovation here is what they call evidence-gated handoff: one agent cannot pass the baton to another agent unless it provides explicit proof of what it has found. Every autonomous agent decision is traceable and reproducible. That's exactly the kind of accountability mechanism we need given the liability concerns we've been talking about.

Emma: Alright, let's move into research. And we're switching gears here because this next one touches on medicine.

Emma: Liam, there's a significant announcement out of Biohub. What's going on?

Liam: Biohub is announcing the expansion of an international initiative backed by the DOE and the NIH, the major US scientific agencies, with a total budget of 1.8 billion dollars, including over 500 million from the DOE over five years. The goal is to build what they're calling virtual cells: biological prediction models capable of simulating how real cells respond to different interventions. The idea is that you could test therapies or study diseases by simulating cellular responses at massive scale, before ever touching a real experiment.

Emma: That sounds almost like science fiction. But the practical implications down the road are very real.

Liam: Absolutely. If you can accurately model how a cancer cell responds to a drug, or how a pathogen evades the immune system, you dramatically accelerate the path to actual treatments. This is one of the most concrete examples out there of how AI can take fundamental research and turn it into real medical impact.

Emma: Love to see it. Now let's dig into industry impact, and specifically two angles that are really central to this show: agent security and financial risk.

Emma: Liam, a security expert named Javier Garza put out something pretty alarming about AI coding tools. What's the issue?

Liam: This one is really worth sitting with. Garza makes the case that blanket bans on AI tools inside companies just don't work. His argument is that 80 percent of developers will find a workaround regardless. But beyond that, he flags a very serious technical risk. MCP servers, which are the extensions that let agents use additional tools, are distributed as plain text. And embedded in that plain text there can be malware that a developer simply cannot spot by reading it.

Emma: And the model itself can create problems on top of that?

Liam: Yes, and this is called slopsquatting. Language models can hallucinate package names that do not exist. And if someone has gone ahead and created a real package under that made-up name and stuffed it with malicious code, the agent will install it without any suspicion. Garza's recommendation is pretty unambiguous: treat all agent inputs and outputs as untrusted by default, grant minimal access permissions, and rescan all AI assets daily, because a tool that's clean today can be compromised by tomorrow.

Emma: It sounds almost paranoid, but honestly it sounds justified. There's also a technical issue with tool-calling loops that can create basically unlimited financial exposure?

Liam: Exactly. When an agent calls tools in a loop with no guardrails, the bill can spiral exponentially: every API call costs money, every token consumed costs money. And most language model SDKs do not include a native circuit breaker to stop those loops. An analysis published by SitePoint recommends four types of guardrails you need to implement manually: per-tool spending caps, execution timeouts, token budgets, and a fallback mechanism that saves to a draft state if any of those limits are hit. Only the newest SDKs, like the OpenAI Agents SDK, are starting to build in native turn controls, and even then it's not yet universal.

Emma: So the financial risks of agentic AI are not just about market exposure or external fraud. They can come straight from the internal plumbing of your own system.

Liam: Exactly right. And to close the loop on security-related financial risk, let's bring back that CertiK figure: 1.31 billion dollars in documented losses across the Web3 ecosystem in 2026. Chainalysis launched dedicated AI agents back in March 2026 to automate multi-chain monitoring and alert enrichment. And 83 percent of jurisdictions had approved the Travel Rule, which requires tracing crypto transfers, by July 2026. So agentic AI is becoming a core compliance tool in crypto, but it's also a serious attack vector if it's not locked down properly.

Emma: And to wrap up on the industry side, you've been tracking a broader trend this week around open-source models. Mistral and Reflection AI both made some noise.

Liam: Yes, and I think this is a really significant signal for where the market is heading. Mistral Large 4 comes in at one trillion parameters total, but thanks to a sparse architecture, only 49 billion of those parameters are active at any given time. And Beam, from Reflection AI, weighs in at 501 billion total parameters with 23 billion active, aimed specifically at coding and agentic reasoning, with open-source weights expected by the end of October. Both of these models are examples of what people are calling debundling: instead of one giant general-purpose model, you're seeing specialized architectures emerge that are more efficient on specific tasks and available as open source. The market estimate is that open-source could reach 86 percent parity with leading proprietary frontier models. That is a massive rebalancing of power.

Emma: And Cloudflare is also playing in that space with Clef-omni?

Liam: Right, Cloudflare optimized its multimodal model Clef by migrating to an inference engine called SGLang, which cut latency roughly in half: from 616 milliseconds down to 305 milliseconds on long sequences. This model handles audio, video, images, and text. It's a specialized multimodal decision model built for voice and visual agents, and with that optimized inference speed, it's genuinely usable in real-world conditions.

Emma: Alright, that is a wrap on a very packed day. The big takeaway from today is that agentic AI is no longer a lab concept. It is embedding itself into governments, enterprises, security systems, and financial infrastructure at a pace that is outrunning existing governance frameworks. The tools are getting sharper, the models are getting more specialized, but the risks around data leakage, runaway loops, malware in extensions, and agents blowing past company policies are all very real and demand constant attention.
