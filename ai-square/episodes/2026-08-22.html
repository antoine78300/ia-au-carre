Maya: Good morning and welcome to AI Square! It's Saturday, it's still early, the coffee is hot, and we have got a ton of fascinating stuff to get into today. So go ahead and get comfortable, because on today's show we are talking music, robots, and a few questions that are genuinely going to make you think. Ryan, you with me?

Ryan: I am here, and honestly, I am not going to lie, I barely slept last night because the news this week just completely had me hooked.

Maya: Alright, let's kick things off with the big story that has everyone talking: Apple Music is going to start labeling AI-generated music. Ryan, break that down for us in plain English.

Ryan: Yeah, so here is what is happening. Apple announced that starting at the end of 2026, any track on Apple Music that was created primarily using generative AI tools is going to have to carry a transparency label. A visible tag that basically says: this song was made by an AI.

Maya: And when we say AI tools, we are talking about things like Suno, Udio, platforms like that?

Ryan: Exactly. Suno, Udio, and anything in that space. You describe what you want, and they spit out a complete track in a matter of seconds. Lyrics, melody, arrangements, the whole thing. And Apple is saying: if your song came primarily from one of those tools, you have got to disclose it.

Maya: Do you think that is the right call?

Ryan: Honestly, yeah, I do, and here is why. This is not a ban. It is a label. Think of it like food packaging where you can see whether something is organic or locally sourced. It puts the choice in the listener's hands. If you only want to hear music made by humans, you can filter for that. If you do not care either way, you just keep listening. At the end of the day, it is about respecting the audience.

Maya: And it opens up this enormous question that I really wanted to get into, which is: do people actually grasp the scale of what is happening here? Because I came across a number this week that genuinely stopped me in my tracks.

Ryan: Deezer?

Maya: Deezer! More than fifty percent of new music uploaded to Deezer during peak periods is entirely AI-generated. One out of every two tracks, Ryan. One out of two.

Ryan: And that is where it gets really mind-bending. Because the bottleneck is no longer production. A few years ago, making a song meant you needed a studio, musicians, time, money. Now anyone can generate a hundred tracks in a single afternoon. The truly scarce resource now is attention. Who is going to listen to what, and why?

Maya: It is kind of like if someone invented a printer that just endlessly spits out books. The challenge is no longer writing. It is getting read.

Ryan: Exactly. And that is what makes Apple's move so interesting. In a world where everyone can produce, transparency about where something came from starts to carry real value. Are you listening to this track because a human being lived through something and turned it into music, or because an algorithm optimized patterns to make you like it?

Maya: The million-dollar question. Okay, let's shift gears to our second big topic of the day, and we are moving into a completely different world: robots.

Ryan: And not just any robot news. There is something really significant happening in China right now, and it is worth taking a close look at.

Maya: Walk us through it.

Ryan: So there were several stories this week that all point in the same direction. First, the head of autonomous driving at Nio, which is a major Chinese electric vehicle company, just left to found an embodied robotics startup. And he said something that I found really striking: every automaker has to become an AI company.

Maya: That is a pretty bold statement.

Ryan: Very bold. And at the same time, when you think about it, it makes total sense. The skills you need to make a car drive itself, to perceive its environment, make real-time decisions, avoid obstacles, those are the exact same skills you need to get a humanoid robot moving around a warehouse or someone's living room.

Maya: So the engineers building self-driving cars are becoming the engineers building the robots of the future?

Ryan: In China, that is clearly what is happening right now. It is a massive talent migration. And right alongside that, a robotics AI startup called Dexmal is looking to raise funding at a three-billion-dollar valuation. Three billion dollars. To put that in perspective, that kind of number would have seemed completely out of reach for a robotics company just five years ago.

Maya: And then there is Unitree as well, which also came up this week.

Ryan: Right, Unitree is the company behind those little yellow robot dogs you see all over social media. They also make humanoid robots. And their message this week was interesting because they said something you almost never hear in this industry: we are not going to have our ChatGPT moment for embodied robotics for at least two to three years.

Maya: It is pretty rare to hear a tech company pump the brakes like that, is it not?

Ryan: Really rare. Usually everybody is overpromising. But they are being upfront about it: current AI models have real limitations when it comes to robotics. A robot that has to physically interact with the world, grab objects, navigate a cluttered apartment, that is infinitely more complex than a chatbot answering questions.

Maya: But the Chinese humanoid robot market is still on track to hit fifteen billion yuan in 2026?

Ryan: Yes, and that is precisely because even without that ChatGPT moment, industrial applications are already real. Factories, logistics. It is not as flashy as a robot that can cook you dinner, but that is where the money is actually flowing right now.

Maya: Great. And we are staying in the world of science for our third big topic, because this one I am really excited about: AI and drug discovery.

Ryan: The collaboration between FRONTEO and Tokyo University of Science!

Maya: That is the one. For anyone who is not familiar with FRONTEO, give us the quick rundown.

Ryan: FRONTEO is a Japanese company that specializes in AI-driven data analysis, particularly in the medical and legal fields. And now they are partnering with one of Japan's top science universities to build what they are calling a research cluster, basically a collaborative hub, to accelerate the discovery of new drugs using AI.

Maya: And what is so fascinating about this is not just that they are using AI to crunch data. It is that they are combining two very different things.

Ryan: Exactly, and that is what makes this project especially ambitious. On one side, the AI generates hypotheses. It might say, for example, this chemical compound could interact with this protein in a way that blocks this disease. And on the other side, human researchers go into the lab and experimentally verify whether that hypothesis actually holds up.

Maya: It is a feedback loop.

Ryan: A feedback loop that keeps accelerating. Because traditionally, discovering a drug takes ten to fifteen years and costs billions of dollars. If AI can generate and screen thousands of hypotheses and surface only the most promising ones before a human ever sets foot in the lab, you are saving years and enormous resources. And this project already has patents granted in Japan, Europe, and the United States, which tells you this is the real deal, not just a flashy press release.

Maya: And it raises a really interesting question about scientific creativity. Because we always hear that the great breakthroughs come from a researcher's intuition, that eureka moment. Can AI actually have those kinds of moments?

Ryan: It is a beautiful question and I do not have a definitive answer. What we can observe is that AI can find connections that a human mind simply would not have made, because making them requires going through millions of scientific papers simultaneously. So it may not be intuition in the human sense, but it is a form of recombination at a scale we just cannot reach on our own.

Maya: Alright, and now we have arrived at my favorite part of the show. The what if. Ryan, what have you been chewing on this week?

Ryan: Yeah, so given everything we talked about today, I kept coming back to this kind of dizzying thought. What if ten years from now, half of all popular culture, and I mean music, movies, books, video games, was primarily generated by AI, but nobody really noticed or particularly cared?

Maya: Hmm. So you are saying that Apple Music label could end up being about as invisible as the "made in China" sticker on the back of your phone?

Ryan: Exactly. We see it, we know it is there, but it does not really change how we feel about the thing. And then the real question becomes: does art have value because it is human, or does it have value because it moves us? Does it actually change the emotion you feel listening to a song if you know it was generated in ten seconds?

Maya: Personally, I am still not there yet. The human context still matters a lot to me. Knowing that an artist went through something real to write a song, that adds a whole other layer to it.

Ryan: And a lot of people feel exactly the same way, and that is completely valid. But the generations growing up right now, with AI everywhere they look, they may have an entirely different relationship with all of this. For them, the question of who made something might be secondary to the question of what it makes them feel.

Maya: Which would mean that our whole framework for cultural value is not just changing on the surface. It is shifting at a much deeper level.

Ryan: And honestly, I find that equal parts fascinating and just a little bit unsettling. Both at the same time.

Maya: Both at the same time is the right answer. Alright, that is a wrap on AI Square for this Saturday. Thank you all so much for spending this time with us. We hope these questions stick with you, that you keep turning them over in your head with your next cup of coffee. And of course we will be right back tomorrow with a brand new dose of artificial intelligence, open-ended questions, and that sense of curiosity we all share. See you tomorrow everyone!

Ryan: See you tomorrow!
