Maya: Good morning and welcome to AI Squared, the podcast that breaks down artificial intelligence every single day of the year, weekends included! It is Saturday, I am Maya, and joining me as always on weekends is Ryan, our in-house AI analyst. We are relaxed, coffee in hand, and the AI news cycle definitely did not take the day off. So let's just dive right in.

Ryan: Good morning Maya, good morning everyone. Seriously, this industry does not slow down for anybody. There is some genuinely fascinating stuff to dig into today.

Maya: We are kicking things off with our discovery of the week, and it actually comes in two parts because they speak to each other perfectly. First up, Suno just launched something called Suno Bridge, an app that lets you generate stems directly inside Ableton Live. For anyone who does not know, Ableton is basically the go-to digital audio workstation for a huge chunk of music producers out there. And now AI is baked right into it natively. It reads your project's tempo, key, chord structure, and generates vocals and instrumentals that sync up automatically.

Ryan: Yeah, this dropped on November 13th, so it is fresh off the press. And what is really interesting here is not just the feature itself, it is what it represents architecturally. We are not talking about a plugin that does one isolated thing. We are talking about an AI agent that actually understands the context of your entire musical project and responds accordingly. That is what we call agentic orchestration. The tool knows where it is, it knows what already exists, and it adapts to that.

Maya: And staying in that AI music space, Mozart AI also just announced a full platform, an integrated studio with track generation, stem separation, audio to MIDI conversion, the whole thing orchestrated by AI. They are claiming it can speed up music production by a factor of ten.

Ryan: Ten times faster. That number is going to get producers excited and probably make a lot of other people pretty uncomfortable. Because here is where the second part of our story comes in, and his name is Nick Cave.

Maya: Nick Cave. So for anyone not familiar, he is an Australian artist, singer, songwriter, an absolute icon who has been a major force in music for decades. And he just published a piece in his newsletter, Red Hand Files, issue 381, and it is a straight-up frontal attack on Suno. He literally calls it the Great Cancellator. Not exactly a glowing review.

Ryan: That phrase is striking and it is not an accident. This is not just some technical critique like oh the AI does not sound authentic. It is a civilizational argument. For Cave, Suno is not just draining human creativity of its meaning, it is erasing something fundamental about what art is even supposed to be.

Maya: And this is not the first time he has gone there. He had already voiced serious concerns back in April 2026, but now he is formalizing it, he is naming the platform directly, and he is turning up the volume. This is not a warning anymore, it is an indictment.

Ryan: What strikes me is that Cave and the AI music advocates are not necessarily disagreeing on the facts. Suno can generate music. Mozart AI can speed up production by ten times. Nobody is disputing those numbers. The real question is what we decide that is worth, and what it means for us as human beings.

Maya: And right alongside that debate, there is also Jack Wall, the composer behind the Mass Effect soundtracks, which is an absolutely iconic video game franchise. And he makes a really specific point. He says AI can generate a hundred variations of a theme in minutes, but it cannot tell you why it chose a particular chord, or who it is trying to move emotionally.

Ryan: And for him, that is exactly what art is. It is communication between human beings. A machine does not have that. But he is nuanced about it, because he does acknowledge that AI can be a legitimate productivity tool for composers. He uses this image of musical coffee, something that helps you get through the work but that is not doing the creating for you.

Maya: So on one side you have tools that are getting more powerful and more deeply integrated by the day. On the other side you have artists drawing very clear lines around what a machine simply cannot replace. And both of those conversations are moving forward at the same time.

Ryan: And honestly, both sides are right on their own terms. That is what makes this debate so hard to settle and so compelling to follow.

Maya: Alright, let's move into our second segment, experiments and original projects. And here we are staying very much in the real world, actual robots doing actual things.

Ryan: Six humanoid robots. A convenience store in Shanghai. Twenty-four hours. And they completed one thousand seven hundred and thirty tasks.

Maya: One thousand seven hundred and thirty. That is an oddly specific number.

Ryan: Deliberately specific. The precision is the whole point. These are AGIBOT G2 robots, and they are coordinated by a system called Genie Master, an AI agent framework that orchestrates all of them together. And here is what really makes it interesting. Every success and every failure during that shift automatically becomes training data to improve how the robots behave going forward.

Maya: So they are literally learning on the job.

Ryan: In real time. And in an unstructured environment, which is the key thing here. A warehouse is one thing, everything is predictable, standardized, controlled. A twenty-four-hour convenience store is a completely different animal. Customers walking in at random times, products getting moved around, unexpected situations popping up constantly.

Maya: And they held up. Now in the broader context of this robotics acceleration, there is a really important analysis out of Pantera Capital that documents something significant about the geopolitics of all this.

Ryan: And it is pretty striking. China controls over ninety percent of global humanoid robot production. And that advantage does not come primarily from algorithms. It comes from hardware supply chains, components, factories, raw materials.

Maya: So what is the picture on the American side?

Ryan: Several companies are pushing forward in parallel, Physical Intelligence, Generalist, Dyna, Figure, among others. And the analysis makes a point that unlike large language models, where you pretty quickly saw two or three players dominate the whole landscape, embodied AI, meaning actual robots, probably will not get monopolized in the same way. The hardware side of things naturally makes it more fragmented.

Maya: Is that a good thing for the industry, or is it just an economic reality?

Ryan: Honestly, both at the same time. And Cathie Wood over at ARK Invest is positioning humanoid robots as a twenty-six-billion-dollar opportunity over the next ten to fifteen years. Her argument is that embodied AI will gradually take over from software as the primary engine of tech growth. With one notable detail, robotaxis are expected to get there even faster, estimated at ten billion dollars but on a shorter timeline.

Maya: And here is another concrete data point on the funding side. In China in October 2026, forty-nine companies announced funding rounds in embodied AI. And forty percent of them are focused on specialized applications, what people call vertical scenarios. Not the do-everything general-purpose robot, but the expert robot that is really good at one specific domain.

Ryan: Which mirrors exactly how autonomous vehicles evolved historically. First everyone dreamed of the car that could drive anywhere in any condition, then it became clear that the first real commercial wins would come from well-defined, limited use cases. This is the same pattern. Manufacturing, services, everyday life, each sector has its own specific requirements.

Maya: Fourth segment, AI and society, science, creativity. And there is one story here that genuinely caught my attention, and it comes out of Singapore.

Ryan: Singapore and Google. The country is deploying frontier AI models across its national healthcare system. And the word to focus on there is national, we are talking the scale of an entire country.

Maya: What does that actually look like in practice?

Ryan: It means they are merging population-level data with geolocated information, climate, environment, behavioral signals, to predict the risk of chronic diseases across entire population groups. Not individual patients, population groups. And they are also rolling out models called MedGemma for direct medical applications.

Maya: That is a seriously ambitious undertaking. And a little dizzying when you think about the sheer volume of data involved.

Ryan: Dizzying and genuinely worth watching, because Singapore has a track record of being a kind of sandbox for innovative public policy. What they are doing here could become a model for other countries, or depending on how it plays out, it could become a cautionary tale.

Maya: And to wrap up this segment, something a lot lighter. There is a startup called Haut.AI that analyzes more than a hundred and fifty skin biomarkers from a single selfie.

Ryan: Founded in 2018 by Anastasia Georgievskaya, who was a drug discovery researcher at Insilico Medicine. And the platform is now integrated at Ulta Beauty, the major American cosmetics retail chain.

Maya: So you take a selfie in the store and the AI reads your skin and recommends products.

Ryan: A hundred and fifty biomarkers from a photo on your phone. It is actually a great example of what people mean when they talk about AI understanding the physical world and operating within it, even if in this case the physical world in question is your face.

Maya: Fifth segment, our forward-looking take. Ryan, do you have a what-if for us today?

Ryan: I do, and it comes directly from a story we have not touched on yet. OpenAI just dissolved its dedicated robotics team. They are folding those researchers back into their general AI groups.

Maya: Wait, OpenAI does not have a robotics team anymore?

Ryan: No dedicated team, no. And the bet behind that decision is that generalist models trained on language, code, and images could directly control physical systems, robots, autonomous vehicles, drones, without needing a specialized AI brain built separately for each application.

Maya: And if that bet pays off?

Ryan: If it pays off, it changes everything. It would mean that the distinction between AI that thinks and AI that acts in the physical world is not actually a fundamental distinction at all. That general intelligence and embodied intelligence are essentially the same thing.

Maya: That is a massive bet. Because at the same time we just saw Chinese companies and others going all-in on highly specialized, vertical systems. So both visions are out there running in parallel.

Ryan: Exactly. And if OpenAI is right, the specialized approaches are just an intermediate step, not the final destination. And if the specialists are right, OpenAI just made a major strategic mistake. In five years we will know the answer.

Maya: And that is exactly what makes this space absolutely impossible to look away from. We are watching fundamental strategic bets play out in real time, and nobody actually knows who has it right.

Ryan: And we are here every single day to follow it all with you.

Maya: That is exactly right. And that is a wrap on today's episode of AI Squared. We covered music and robots and skin and geopolitics and OpenAI reshuffling its teams, so just a pretty average day in AI.
