Full transcript

Maya: Hey everyone, welcome to AI Square, the podcast that breaks down artificial intelligence for you every single day. Today, we are talking about AI agents absolutely everywhere — in your servers, in your pocket, in Google's boardrooms, and yes, in the hands of hackers. Buckle up, because we have got a packed show.

Maya: Ryan, before we dive into the stories, can you set the stage for us? Because looking at everything that dropped this Thursday, August 6th, 2026, I feel like agentic AI is genuinely crossing some kind of threshold right now.

Ryan: Absolutely, Maya. And the story that really crystallizes that more than anything else today is what is happening at Google. Demis Hassabis, the founder of DeepMind, is stepping back from day-to-day operational leadership. And in the same move, Jeff Dean, who is basically a living legend in the AI world, is leaving Google after twenty-seven years with the company.

Maya: Twenty-seven years. That is almost as old as the internet itself.

Ryan: Right. And for listeners who may not know the name, Jeff Dean is essentially the godfather of Google's AI infrastructure. He contributed to TensorFlow, he was at the center of the Gemini strategy. This is not a quiet exit. And the markets got that immediately — Alphabet shares dropped four percent on the news.

Maya: Four percent is real money. But what does this actually tell us about what is going on inside Google right now?

Ryan: It tells us two things. First, Google is in the middle of a major strategic reorganization at an absolutely critical moment, right as they are gearing up for Gemini 4. Second, and this is maybe the more interesting angle, it raises a question that a lot of big tech companies are going to have to reckon with: are the people who architect a technology intellectually the same people best suited to industrialize it at massive scale?

Maya: So you are saying building the foundational research and actually shipping a huge product at scale are two completely different jobs?

Ryan: Exactly. Hassabis is a brilliant researcher, a true visionary. But at this stage, Google needs someone who is optimizing production pipelines, managing thousands of engineers on very tight delivery cycles. That is not necessarily the same person. And honestly, we have seen this exact same pattern play out at other companies over the past couple of years.

Maya: So Google restructuring around Gemini 4 with fresh leadership — does that leave them more vulnerable or could it actually make them stronger?

Ryan: In the short term, it creates uncertainty, and markets hate uncertainty. But over the medium term, if Google pulls off this leadership transition cleanly, it could actually let them move faster. The real risk is the in-between period, where key talent might start looking around, especially with competitors who are recruiting incredibly aggressively right now.

Maya: A quiet earthquake with some potentially very big aftershocks. Alright, let us get into the tools and frameworks, because there was a lot published this morning. Ryan, let us start with Microsoft.

Ryan: Yeah, so this is one people have been waiting on for a while — the Microsoft Agent Framework has officially hit version 1.0 stable. As a quick refresher, this framework is essentially the merger of two projects developers already knew well: AutoGen and Semantic Kernel. Microsoft took the best of both and built a unified foundation out of them.

Maya: So what does this actually change day-to-day for a developer who wants to build AI agents today?

Ryan: It changes quite a bit. For starters, you get native support for MCP, the Model Context Protocol, which is rapidly becoming the de facto standard for connecting agents to external tools. Then you have graph-based workflows, which let you model complex multi-agent pipelines in a way that is actually readable and maintainable. And what is really significant for enterprise deployments specifically: checkpointing and human-in-the-loop patterns.

Maya: Break that down for us — what exactly does human-in-the-loop mean in this context?

Ryan: It is the ability to put a human being into the decision-making process at specific points in the workflow. Picture an AI agent that is handling supplier purchase orders — above a certain dollar amount, instead of just going ahead and approving it, the agent pauses its execution and waits for a manager to sign off. That is exactly the kind of mechanism we are talking about. And this actually connects really nicely to something we are about to get into with PubMatic.

Maya: Perfect segue — let us talk about PubMatic. They are an ad tech platform, and they made an interesting announcement today.

Ryan: Very interesting, and it is a textbook illustration of a broader trend. PubMatic is rolling out a governance framework specifically designed to control what autonomous AI agents can and cannot do inside advertising environments. In plain terms: an AI agent can only access ad inventory that has already been pre-approved by a human. Everything else is blocked.

Maya: So basically a whitelist system.

Ryan: Exactly, combined with independent transaction-level validation on top of that. And what is really elegant about their approach is that the control layer is completely agnostic to whatever AI agent is sitting on the other side. Whether it is a GPT-5-based agent, a Gemini-based agent, or anything else entirely — the verification applies the same way. It is a centralized system-level check, not something the agent is responsible for enforcing on itself.

Maya: And that matters because it means you cannot just swap out the agent to get around the controls?

Ryan: Exactly. And that is where it gets particularly relevant from a security standpoint. Because you cannot trust that an agent is well-configured if that agent is also the one supposed to be regulating itself. The control has to be external. That is a pretty basic security principle, but a lot of companies completely forget it when they start deploying AI agents.

Maya: Which brings me right to the security beat, because there is some major news today with Tenable and Black Hat.

Ryan: Yeah, and this one is really worth sitting with for a minute. Tenable, one of the top names in cybersecurity, is launching CyberAgents Exchange — an open-source platform with more than fifty AI components purpose-built for security. And alongside that, they are running a hackathon at Black Hat USA 2026 called SWARM.

Maya: SWARM — that name carries some ominous energy in a cybersecurity context, does it not?

Ryan: The name is very much intentional. The idea behind SWARM, sponsored by AWS and backed by Anthropic, is to let security practitioners build agents that can operate as a coordinated swarm to detect and counter threats. And this brings us straight to the core issue of the moment: autonomous AI agents are now tools that cut both ways. They can defend a system, and they can attack one.

Maya: And that actually leads right into something a lot of our listeners have been worried about lately — autonomous ransomware. Where do things stand on that front?

Ryan: It is a genuinely alarming development. Autonomous AI agents have fundamentally changed the ransomware playbook. Traditionally, an attack required human coordination at every stage: reconnaissance, intrusion, lateral movement, encryption. Now we are starting to see agents that can chain all of those steps together autonomously, adapting to defenses in real time.

Maya: What does that actually mean practically for a business today?

Ryan: It means the speed of an attack has gone up by an order of magnitude. A human attacker might spend days or even weeks quietly getting established in a network before triggering encryption. An autonomous agent can compress that down to hours, or even minutes in some documented test scenarios. And that is precisely why what Tenable is doing with CyberAgents Exchange matters so much — you cannot fight autonomous malicious agents with manual defenses. You need autonomous defensive agents to fight back.

Maya: It is a full-on arms race.

Ryan: A real-time arms race, yes. And the good news is the security community gets it. Black Hat 2026 this year is heavily defined by the theme of autonomous security agents. It has become the central conversation.

Maya: Let us move into research, because there are two really significant papers out today. Let us start with SkillWeaver from Alibaba.

Ryan: SkillWeaver is fascinating, and the numbers they are putting out are honestly kind of jaw-dropping. Researchers at Alibaba are presenting an architecture that reduces an AI agent's token consumption by 99.9 percent.

Maya: Hold on — 99.9 percent? That is nearly a thousand times less?

Ryan: That is exactly right. And to understand why that is such a big deal, you need to understand the underlying problem. A modern AI agent might have access to hundreds or even thousands of different tools. At every decision point, it potentially has to look through the documentation for all of those tools just to figure out which one to use. That burns through the context window fast, it gets expensive computationally, and it slows everything down.

Maya: So how does SkillWeaver actually solve that?

Ryan: Through a technique called Skill-Aware Decomposition, or SAD. The core idea is that you do not hand the agent the entire tool catalog every single time. Instead, you use a smaller model — in this case a 7-billion-parameter Qwen 2.5 — paired with FAISS semantic search, to identify upfront the small handful of tools that are actually relevant to the current task. The main agent only ever sees those tools.

Maya: It is like having an assistant who filters your inbox before anything even hits your desk.

Ryan: That is a perfect way to put it. And maybe the most striking result is that SkillWeaver with this small 7-billion-parameter model actually outperforms much larger and much more expensive models. It really challenges the assumption that bigger is always better in AI.

Maya: The other research story today is about the energy consumption of agentic AI. And I get the sense this is a topic that makes some people uncomfortable.

Ryan: Uncomfortable and too often swept under the rug in AI conversations. Zeke Hausfather from The Climate Brink is publishing a really thorough analysis on the actual energy consumption of AI agents, and he is pairing it with a standardized framework so companies can actually measure and report their AI-related emissions.

Maya: Because right now most companies are not measuring this at all?

Ryan: Most are not measuring it, and the ones that do are using such wildly different methodologies that comparison is basically impossible. What Hausfather is proposing is a common standard: grams of CO2 equivalent per query, by task type, by infrastructure type. The silver lining in his analysis is that the energy efficiency of data center GPUs is improving fast. According to Epoch AI data, it roughly doubles every two years.

Maya: Like Moore's Law, but applied to energy efficiency.

Ryan: That is a solid analogy. But the problem is demand for AI agents is growing even faster than efficiency is improving. So in absolute terms, total energy consumption keeps climbing. And with agentic AI, it is worse than simple chatbot queries, because one agent can chain dozens of API calls just to complete a single task.

Maya: Alright, let us wrap up with the industry impact angle, and there are two threads to cover here — AnythingLLM on Snapdragon on one side, and the broader evolution of agents in the enterprise on the other.

Ryan: AnythingLLM on Qualcomm's Snapdragon X chips is the payoff of a trend we have been watching for two years now: AI moving off the cloud and running locally on devices. What Qualcomm is doing here is optimizing AnythingLLM specifically for the NPU — the dedicated AI processor — in their chips. The result is that you have an agent that can process your documents, do local web research, build custom tools, all without a single byte of your data leaving the device.

Maya: For professionals dealing with sensitive data, that has got to be incredibly appealing.

Ryan: Enormously appealing. Law firms, physicians, companies operating under strict data-residency regulations. That is a massive market. And it also addresses the energy concern we just talked about, because a local NPU uses a fraction of the power that a data center burns through for the same task.

Maya: And if we zoom out and look at everything we covered today as a whole, what is the big through-line you are taking away?

Ryan: Maturity. Everything we have seen today points to the same conclusion: we are moving out of the experimentation phase and into the industrialization phase of agentic AI. Microsoft shipping a stable release, PubMatic putting guardrails in place, Tenable structuring agentic security, Alibaba optimizing for efficiency, Qualcomm pushing AI to the edge. These are not lab demos anymore. These are architectural decisions for real production systems.

Maya: And with humans stepping out of the loop at Google, while others are deliberately building them back in with human-in-the-loop at Microsoft and PubMatic.

Ryan: That is the defining tension of the moment. AI is becoming more autonomous, but the best-designed systems are the ones that thoughtfully integrate human oversight exactly where it is genuinely needed. Not automate everything, not keep everything manual. Find the right balance.

Maya: Ryan, thank you so much — as always, a really thorough and genuinely illuminating breakdown.

Ryan: My pleasure, Maya.
