Full transcript

Maya: Welcome to AI Square, the podcast that breaks down artificial intelligence for you every single day. Today we are talking about AI agent security, some game-changing acquisitions, and the question that every developer seems to be wrestling with right now: can you actually trust an autonomous AI? Stay with us, because we have a packed episode.

Ryan: Hey Maya.

Maya: Hey Ryan. So let's just dive right in with the story that has everyone talking today, and honestly it is a little unsettling. Researchers are digging into techniques for manipulating AI agents. So can you actually convince an AI to break its own rules?

Ryan: Yeah, and this is becoming one of the most pressing questions in the field, especially now that autonomous agents are everywhere. What we are really talking about here are very specific attack techniques that do not target the underlying models themselves, but rather the conversational agents, meaning the systems that can actually go out and do things in the real world, send emails, place orders, access databases.

Maya: So how does that actually work in practice? How do you quote-unquote persuade an AI?

Ryan: There are several ways in. The most well-known is what is called prompt injection. Basically you slip hidden instructions into a conversation that end up short-circuiting the model's safety rules. But researchers are now documenting much more sophisticated attacks. One example is what they call contextual jailbreaking, where the attacker builds up a long conversation that gradually nudges the agent into operating from a completely different frame of reference than the one it was trained on. And the agent does not even realize its context has shifted.

Maya: That is almost like slowly talking someone into changing their entire worldview, one small step at a time, without them ever noticing it is happening.

Ryan: That is exactly it. And what makes this even more serious is that these agents now have access to real tools. We are talking about agents that can modify files, interact with APIs, handle transactions. So the consequences of a successful manipulation are no longer just theoretical.

Maya: So what can actually be done about it?

Ryan: Well, that leads us right into our second big story today. Engineers at USC, the University of Southern California, have developed auditing and monitoring tools specifically designed to watch what AI agents are doing in real time. The whole idea is to stop simply trusting the agent on its own and instead be able to observe every single action it takes, log it, and analyze it.

Maya: It is kind of like handing an employee the keys but keeping a detailed record of everything they do with them.

Ryan: That is actually the exact analogy the researchers themselves use. They describe it as a transparency layer that sits between the agent and the systems it is accessing. Every action gets tracked, given context, and can trigger an alert or a human review if it falls outside expected parameters. And what is really compelling here is that this directly addresses the vulnerabilities we just talked about. If an agent starts behaving strangely because it has been manipulated, these tools can catch it before any real damage is done.

Maya: Does that mean we will never truly be able to trust an AI agent without a human watching over it?

Ryan: Right now, that is pretty clearly where the scientific community stands. Blindly trusting an agent to operate on its own is considered a design flaw. The best systems being built today pair autonomy with oversight. A human stays in the loop, at least for anything high-stakes.

Maya: Alright, let's move into our frameworks and tools segment. Ryan, there is a pretty comprehensive landscape report that just dropped on local LLMs, the models you actually run on your own machine. What does the market look like in 2026?

Ryan: So what this deep-dive analysis from Storage Review reveals is that local LLMs have made serious strides, but the market is still extremely fragmented. On one side you have runtimes like Ollama, LM Studio, and llama.cpp, and on the other side you have interfaces, agents, and applications that plug into them. The problem is those layers do not always play nicely together.

Maya: For someone who is just getting started with this stuff, is it actually accessible?

Ryan: It really depends on what you need. If data privacy is your top priority, or if you are working in an environment with no internet access, what is called an air-gapped environment, then yes, local LLMs are a genuinely viable option today. From a cost perspective too, they can look really attractive over the long run. But if you are hoping to match the performance of the best cloud-based models, you are going to be disappointed. There is still a meaningful gap there.

Maya: And for developers specifically, are there any tools that stand out?

Ryan: The analysis does highlight a few mature solutions. But it also raises a really important flag: a lot of the articles and recommendations you will find online are already out of date. This ecosystem moves so fast that a tool someone was raving about six months ago might already be abandoned or replaced. So the advice is to always check the date on anything you read and to favor GitHub repositories that are actively maintained with recent commits.

Maya: Solid advice. Now we have also got SAP getting into the game with agents built for ABAP development. For folks who might not know, what is ABAP?

Ryan: ABAP is SAP's proprietary programming language, used in the ERP systems that millions of companies around the world run their operations on. It has been around for several decades, it is highly specialized, and developers who actually know it well are increasingly hard to find. So SAP's play here is to deploy AI agents that can write, fix, and migrate ABAP code. They are doing it through an MCP server, the Model Context Protocol that Anthropic popularized, and it integrates directly with Visual Studio Code.

Maya: And it is usage-based pricing?

Ryan: Exactly, and that is where it becomes a real strategic question. Usage-based pricing can seem attractive at first glance, but SAP itself acknowledges that the success of this whole thing hinges on three things: the accuracy of the generated code, the ability to preserve sometimes very complex business logic, and cost predictability. That last one is huge in enterprise settings. If your AI agent bill becomes unpredictable, CIOs are going to pump the brakes very fast.

Maya: That is a real concern. Okay, now let's get into notable research. And we are kind of staying in that security and control theme, right?

Ryan: Yeah, and it is almost a nice coincidence in today's news cycle. The USC work on agent monitoring is part of a broader academic push to create what you might call technical guardrails for agentic AI. What is new about USC's approach is that they are not just trying to block bad actions. They are trying to create a record that a human being can actually understand.

Maya: So it is not just some unreadable technical log?

Ryan: Exactly. Explainability is the core challenge here. A log entry that says API request sent does not mean much to a hospital's chief medical officer or a CFO. What these researchers are proposing is a semantic representation of actions, where you understand not just what the agent did, but why it did it, what context it was operating in, and what alternatives it considered. That is a meaningful step forward for what we call AI governance.

Maya: And is this going to make it into real-world production soon, or are we still in theory land?

Ryan: We are somewhere in between. The tools exist, and prototypes have been tested in real environments. But integrating them into enterprise production pipelines takes time. You need standards and interoperability. The big question is whether the major cloud platforms adopt these approaches or just roll their own. Right now it is still pretty siloed.

Maya: Got it. Now we are heading into our industry impact segment, and we have two really major stories here. Where do you want to start?

Ryan: Let's start with the acquisition that has been making the most noise: Stripe buying OpenRouter for 7.5 billion dollars.

Maya: Okay, let's back up for a second and explain what OpenRouter actually is for anyone who has not come across it.

Ryan: OpenRouter is a platform that lets developers access a wide range of different language models through a single unified API. Instead of having to separately integrate OpenAI's API, Anthropic's API, Google's API, you go through OpenRouter, which automatically routes to the best model based on your needs, context, and cost. It is what you would call a neutral orchestration layer.

Maya: And Stripe getting involved feels like a surprise. Everyone knows Stripe as a payments company.

Ryan: And that is exactly what makes this so strategically fascinating. Stripe is not buying OpenRouter to become an AI company. Stripe is buying OpenRouter because payments and AI agents are going to be deeply intertwined. We are entering what people are calling the agentic commerce era. AI agents that place orders, negotiate prices, execute financial transactions. If you are the payments layer, you also want to be the model orchestration layer. It is both an offensive and a defensive move.

Maya: And 7.5 billion dollars sends a very loud signal about how much that infrastructure is worth.

Ryan: Absolutely. This is one of the biggest AI acquisitions of the year. And the message is crystal clear: model routing infrastructure has enormous strategic value. In a world where there are dozens of competitive models, whoever controls the routing controls a very powerful position.

Maya: We also have Oracle Health rolling out a clinical AI agent. Healthcare is really moving fast on this stuff.

Ryan: Very fast. And Oracle is making a concrete move here. Their clinical AI agent integrates into electronic health records, EHRs, and now handles not just documentation, but also medical coding for billing and patient data contextualization. That last piece is key: instead of just throwing raw data at you, the agent places it in the context of the patient's full history.

Maya: And a physician is still reviewing everything, I would assume?

Ryan: Yes, and that is a non-negotiable principle in healthcare right now. Oracle built what they call human review checkpoints into the system. The agent proposes, the clinician approves. It is not full autonomy, but it dramatically cuts down on the administrative burden that doctors face, which is one of the biggest pain points in healthcare. Studies have shown that physicians can spend more time on documentation than on actually caring for patients.

Maya: And we have one more story coming out of Asia, with DBS Hong Kong and Visa teaming up around agentic commerce.

Ryan: This one really reinforces the trend we have been seeing all day. DBS Hong Kong, Visa, and a company called Preface are launching a program to help merchants get ready for the age of AI agents in payments. The idea is that in the near future, the buyer may not be a human clicking a purchase button. It will be an agent acting on that human's behalf. Merchants and payment systems need to be prepared for that reality.

Maya: That is a genuine paradigm shift.

Ryan: Completely. And it ties directly back to the Stripe and OpenRouter acquisition. All of these players are laying the groundwork for an economy where AI agents are full participants in commercial transactions.

Maya: Ryan, before we wrap things up, there is one more story we really cannot skip over: South Korea narrowing its national AI model competition down to three finalist teams.

Ryan: Right, and this is a really telling signal about the geopolitical dynamics playing out around AI. The South Korean government wants to develop a homegrown foundation model, and three contenders have made the cut: Upstage, SK Telecom, and LG AI Research. The final selection is expected in early 2027. This fits into a global trend where countries are actively working to avoid being entirely dependent on American or Chinese models.

Maya: A form of technological sovereignty.

Ryan: Exactly. And South Korea is far from alone in this. You are seeing similar initiatives across Europe, Japan, and India. The question of who controls the foundation models of tomorrow is just as much a geopolitical question as it is a technological one.

Maya: Alright. So what is your big takeaway from everything we covered today?

Ryan: I would say the through-line of today's episode is trust and control. Trust in AI agents, which still has to be earned, and control over those same agents, which is becoming its own discipline. From agent manipulation to monitoring tools, to billion-dollar acquisitions over infrastructure, everything keeps circling back to the same question: how do we bring order to an ecosystem that is evolving faster than the rules we are trying to apply to it?

Maya: Well said. Ryan, thank you so much for all of these insights today.

Ryan: Always a pleasure, Maya.

Maya: And thank you all for listening all the way through. If you got something out of today's episode, share it with someone who would appreciate it, subscribe to AI Square on your favorite podcast app, and drop us a review, it genuinely makes a huge difference for us. We will be back tomorrow with all the biggest AI stories of the day. See you then.
