Full transcript

Maya: Welcome to AI Squared, the podcast that breaks down the world of artificial intelligence every single day. Today is Friday, October 9th, 2026, and honestly, the news is on fire: AI agents hacking banks, Google reinventing how multiple models work together, and regulators trying to figure out who's actually on the hook when an AI makes a mistake. Let's get into it.

Maya: Ryan, let's kick things off with something that is genuinely going to give our listeners pause. There's a story that's been making the rounds for the past few hours and it deserves a real conversation. AI agents being used to attack banks?

Ryan: Yeah, Maya, and this is very real, not science fiction. CrowdStrike just publicly documented a tool called ARTEX, which is an autonomous pentesting framework, meaning a system that can probe a network for security vulnerabilities in a fully automated way. And what makes this unprecedented is that ARTEX was used in actual attacks against South Korean financial institutions in late September and early October 2026.

Maya: When you say autonomous, you mean this thing is essentially operating on its own?

Ryan: Exactly. ARTEX runs on multiple LLM agents that collaborate with each other, with 46 built-in automation tools. The agent can simulate human behavior, for example by spoofing geolocation or browser fingerprints, what's known as canvas spoofing. And according to reports, the entire infrastructure was operating out of Hong Kong. What makes this a historic case is that it's the first time a named agentic pentesting tool has been officially attributed to a data exfiltration campaign against regulated banks.

Maya: And we're talking about an open-source tool here, right? So anyone can get their hands on it?

Ryan: That's where this gets really alarming. ARTEX is open-source, and according to reports from The Hacker News and CrowdStrike, it was using Claude Code, Anthropic's coding model, as its reasoning engine. That means the barrier to entry for pulling off this kind of attack has dropped dramatically. You no longer need a team of expert hackers. A well-configured AI agent can map out the architecture of a regulated banking system and exploit it.

Maya: That is genuinely unsettling. So what about the defensive side? Are we seeing any responses emerge to this kind of threat?

Ryan: We are, and the timing is actually pretty relevant here because a company was just recognized specifically for thinking through this problem. Mate Security took home the Security Response Solution of the Year award at the 2026 CyberSecurity Breakthrough Awards. Their approach is a framework they call CPR, which stands for Confidence, Precision, and Reversibility. The core idea is straightforward but powerful: before a security AI agent takes any action, the system weighs the risk of acting against the risk of not acting. The agent only moves forward if taking action is assessed as the safer choice.

Maya: So on one side you've got agents attacking, on the other you've got agents defending. It's basically an agent arms race at this point.

Ryan: That's a fair way to put it. And it's exactly why the regulatory framework around these technologies is becoming so urgent. But we'll circle back to that when we get to the industry impact segment.

Maya: Perfect. Let's move on to the tools and frameworks that made headlines today, because there was significant movement from Google and SAP in particular.

Ryan: Yeah, let's start with Google because this is an announcement that's going to shape how a lot of companies operate over the coming months. Google Cloud confirmed that its Gemini agent can now orchestrate not just its own models, but also Anthropic's Claude models, and potentially other proprietary or open-source models down the road. The idea is smart routing that selects the most appropriate model based on the task at hand.

Maya: Hold on, I want to make sure I'm hearing this right. Google is offering an agent that can use models from its competitor Anthropic?

Ryan: Exactly. And that is a very strong signal about the direction the industry is heading. The architecture separates the agent that interacts with the user from the underlying reasoning model. So a company can build its workflows around the Google ecosystem and still choose Claude for specific tasks where it performs better. Google is putting some very concrete numbers on the table to show the scale here: 500 Google Cloud customers processed more than one trillion tokens in a single year, 80 percent of Cloud customers are now using AI, and 90 percent of Fortune 100 companies are using Gemini Enterprise.

Maya: What about SAP? What's going on there?

Ryan: SAP has made its AI Agent Hub generally available. It's a governance platform that lets organizations oversee every agent, every LLM, and every MCP server, which is the standard protocol that allows agents to connect to tools and data. What's notable is that the hub works across both SAP and non-SAP environments. And SAP is rolling out a module called Signavio Agent Mining, which lets you concretely measure the real-world results of each agent running in production.

Maya: You mention MCP a lot, and there's also eXp Realty adopting it today, right?

Ryan: Yeah, and that's a really interesting example because it's a sector you wouldn't automatically associate with cutting-edge AI. eXp Realty, a real estate network, is launching what it calls an MCP First strategy. The idea is that its real estate agents can connect whichever LLM they prefer, whether that's ChatGPT or Claude, to the company's back-office infrastructure through the MCP protocol. Their AGNT OS platform comes loaded with a library of pre-built prompts for typical real estate workflows. The launch is planned for early 2027.

Maya: It's fascinating to watch MCP become a standard that's being adopted across such completely different industries. Ready to move into research?

Ryan: Let's do it.

Maya: So this morning we got results from a sandbox experiment that was run in Singapore. Ryan, what exactly was this about?

Ryan: This is a really valuable study because it delivers concrete results tested under real-world conditions. Between August and December 2025, Singapore, Google, the Cyber Security Agency, and the IMDA jointly ran a sandbox specifically focused on AI agents in high-risk environments. And the findings directly fed into the publication in July 2026 of the SAFR framework, which stands for Safeguards for Agentic Finance at Runtime, put out by the Monetary Authority of Singapore.

Maya: And what did they actually find inside that sandbox?

Ryan: Two critical risks were identified. The first is indirect prompt injection: an AI agent processing external data can be manipulated by malicious instructions hidden inside that data, without the user ever knowing. The second risk is data leakage. When an agent has access to many systems to get its job done, it can inadvertently expose sensitive information. The operational takeaway is clear: high-risk actions must require explicit human approval before being executed.

Maya: That's essentially what Mate Security is implementing in their CPR framework that you mentioned earlier.

Ryan: That's exactly right. And that convergence is really significant: sandbox researchers and enterprise practitioners are independently arriving at the same conclusions. We're also seeing a new architectural pattern emerge on the technical side, documented today by Forkast. They call it the dual-gateway stack, and it separates LLM mediation, which handles request routing, prompt security, and token budgeting, from agent orchestration, which handles MCP governance, credentials, and action-by-action approval.

Maya: And Microsoft also dropped something today?

Ryan: Yes, Microsoft is launching three new models specialized for voice: MAI-Transcribe-2-Streaming, which is currently ranked number one on Artificial Analysis, MAI-Voice-2.1, and a Flash version optimized for ultra-low latency. The concept is to hand voice off to dedicated models so agents can free up reasoning capacity, while still keeping conversations feeling natural and fluid.

Maya: Agents that talk, reason, and act. Let's dig into the industry impact now and especially the regulatory picture, because there was a very pointed statement from the UK's FCA today.

Ryan: Very pointed, and remarkably candid. David Geale, Executive Director at the Financial Conduct Authority in the UK, described agentic AI as a massive opportunity but a potential threat. He put his finger on a major regulatory question that still doesn't have an answer: who is responsible when an autonomous AI agent makes a mistake that isn't fraud? A judgment error, for example, that costs a consumer money.

Maya: And the FCA doesn't have an answer?

Ryan: They're publicly acknowledging they don't. And it matters that the regulator is being transparent about that. At the same time, the FCA is rolling out a new regime for crypto assets built around consumer protection and market integrity. But the question of who is liable for non-fraudulent agentic errors remains wide open. Is it the fault of the model developer? The company that deployed the agent? The customer who authorized the agent to act on their behalf?

Maya: And that question becomes even more pressing given what we saw with ARTEX. If an agent attacks a bank, who is responsible?

Ryan: Exactly. And you can see the industry already trying to get ahead of that regulatory vacuum with technical solutions. The AI gateway is becoming a full-fledged product category. Players like Palo Alto with its Prisma AIRS solution, which is now processing trillions of tokens per month since its general availability in July 2026, and Kong with version 3.14, have built security layers that operate at the infrastructure level, before the agent ever takes action.

Maya: Progress Software also had some news today?

Ryan: Yes, Progress Software raised its financial guidance for 2026 and shipped a new version of its Agentic RAG platform, which integrates its collaboration tools even more tightly with AI agents. Their bet is on what they call glue AI, an AI that connects existing systems together rather than replacing them. And financially, that bet is paying off.

Maya: Alright, let's zoom out for a second. What do we take away from this October 9th, 2026 in the bigger story of AI?

Ryan: Three things stand out for me. First, agentic AI has moved from experimental to operational, and that cuts both ways. ARTEX attacking banks is proof that these tools carry real power. Second, the industry is actively working to build guardrails, whether that's Mate Security's CPR framework, Singapore's SAFR guidelines, or AI gateways becoming serious commercial products. And third, regulatory governance is lagging behind, and at least some regulators like the FCA have the self-awareness to admit it.

Maya: And on the positive side, Google opening its orchestration up to Claude, SAP delivering unified governance, Microsoft specializing its models, this is an industry that is maturing fast.

Ryan: Very fast. The real question is whether regulation and security can keep up with the pace.

Maya: And that's a wrap on today's edition of AI Squared. Ryan, thanks as always for the sharp analysis. Today we covered AI agent-powered bank attacks, new governance frameworks, Singapore's sandbox research on agentic risk, and the regulatory questions that are still very much up in the air.
