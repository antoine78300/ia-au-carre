Maya: Good morning and welcome to AI Squared! It is Saturday, the sun is out, the coffee is hot, and honestly, we have some absolutely wild stuff to get into today. Ryan is here with me, we are keeping it relaxed this morning, but trust me, the news from this week is anything but chill. Buckle up.

Maya: Alright, let's just dive right in with the big discovery of the week. And Ryan, I have to say, when you sent me that headline last night, I actually read it twice because I was convinced I had misread it.

Ryan: Yeah, so hold on to your seats. Claude, the AI model developed by Anthropic, independently discovered, on its own, a gene-editing mechanism in the same class as CRISPR. And it did it in twenty-one hours.

Maya: Twenty-one hours. For anyone who might not fully grasp what that means, we are talking about the same category of technology as CRISPR, which is arguably the most significant medical breakthrough of the last thirty years. The kind of discovery that entire research teams spend years chasing.

Ryan: Exactly. Claude identified an enzymatic system that was previously unknown, buried in the DNA of bacteriophages, which are viruses that infect bacteria. This mechanism has been named ART. And the key thing to understand here is that Claude did not just sort through existing data or summarize research papers. It found something genuinely new.

Maya: And Anthropic did not stop there?

Ryan: Not even close. Anthropic also announced the opening of a wet lab, meaning an actual physical laboratory, in the San Francisco Bay Area, specifically designed to let AI agents interact with real lab equipment. We are not talking about computer simulations anymore. We are talking about robots and AI agents physically handling samples and instruments in the real world.

Maya: So AI is stepping off the screen and walking into the lab.

Ryan: That is exactly it. And Dario Amodei, the CEO of Anthropic, went even further in his public statements. He is predicting that AI could help cure most major diseases within five to ten years.

Maya: Five to ten years. In medicine, that is practically tomorrow.

Ryan: It is an incredibly short timeline, yes. And we do need to pump the brakes just a little, because other analyses remind us that even though AI agents are doing remarkable things in the early phases of pharmaceutical research, the clinical reality is still really complicated. A 2024 study shows that drug candidates discovered by AI are hitting Phase 1 success rates between eighty and ninety percent, which is way above historical averages.

Maya: That is a massive difference.

Ryan: It is, but Phase 2 success rates, meaning when you move into human trials, are still hovering around forty percent, which is pretty comparable to what we saw before AI came into the picture. So AI is accelerating and improving the discovery process, but it is not erasing the clinical complexity that comes after.

Maya: So basically, AI is brilliant at finding drug candidates, but after that, it is still human biology that has the final say.

Ryan: Perfectly put. And speaking of using AI to accelerate science, that vision is now official US government strategy. The Department of Energy just released a report called the Genesis Mission, which establishes a formal federal framework for applying agentic AI to national research in chemistry, physics, materials science, and energy.

Maya: So this is not just private labs doing their own thing anymore. This is the US government officially saying AI is part of the plan for national scientific research.

Ryan: That is right. It is a political and institutional stamp of approval for what has been happening in labs for the past few years. We are moving out of the experimental phase and into a deliberate, coordinated federal strategy.

Maya: That is fascinating stuff. Okay, we are shifting gears now because our next story smells a lot less like a laboratory and a lot more like a theme park.

Ryan: Oh yes, we are going from one extreme to the other here.

Maya: In Zhuhai, China, a theme park just opened its doors. And this is not your average theme park. It is the world's first large-scale embodied AI theme park. It is called Chimelong Spaceship.

Ryan: And when we say large-scale, we are talking about more than three hundred AgiBot robots deployed across over a hundred interaction points throughout the entire park. Guided tours, food service, retail, guest services. These robots are not there just to look cool. They are actually working.

Maya: So you show up, and it is a robot that tells you where the restrooms are.

Ryan: Exactly. And what is really remarkable about this announcement is what it represents in terms of where the industry is. AgiBot delivered its twenty-thousandth robot at the time of this opening. Twenty thousand units. That is industrial-scale production, not prototypes.

Maya: We have left the demo era behind.

Ryan: Completely. What is happening in Zhuhai is a live demonstration that AI robotics can operate in real-world environments, at scale, under unpredictable conditions, with thousands of visitors doing whatever they please.

Maya: Because a theme park is basically the perfect anti-laboratory. You have got kids running everywhere, adults asking strange questions, noise in every direction.

Ryan: That is exactly it. It is an extraordinary real-world stress test. If the robots hold up well there, that is genuine proof that the technology has matured.

Maya: Alright, we are continuing our global tour of AI with a topic that hits pretty close to home for a lot of people, and that is music.

Ryan: The numbers here are staggering. On Deezer, in June 2026, more than fifty percent of daily uploads were AI-generated. We are talking about ninety thousand tracks produced entirely by machines being uploaded every single day.

Maya: Every single day. That is mind-blowing. That is more than the entire music industry was producing in a year, not that long ago.

Ryan: And yet those tracks represent only about one to three percent of actual listening. People are not really listening to this music. It exists, it is out there, but nobody is consuming it.

Maya: So AI is creating this massive mountain of content that essentially nobody is listening to.

Ryan: Which raises a real question about the value of creative output when it becomes trivially easy to produce. A study referenced by the National Library of Medicine shows that the human connection to musical experiences generates a sense of meaning and lasting emotional satisfaction that AI alone simply cannot provide. It is not just about audio quality. It is about emotional resonance.

Maya: When you listen to an artist who you know poured their heart into writing a song, you might not hear it in the notes themselves, but you feel it.

Ryan: Exactly. And the industry has started pushing back. The IFPI, the RIAA, the Recording Academy, and SAG-AFTRA announced in July 2026 a voluntary framework that draws a line between two categories. On one side, what they are calling AI-generated music, where AI is responsible for all the major creative elements. On the other, AI-assisted music, where there is substantial human creative input and AI only touches certain elements.

Maya: So kind of like a label rating how much humanity is actually in the music.

Ryan: And the IFPI is going further by requiring this transparency for global charts. If you want to chart, you have to be upfront about what you used.

Maya: That is just common sense, honestly. Like nutritional labels on food.

Ryan: The comparison really does hold up. We want to know what is in what we are consuming, and that includes what we are listening to.

Maya: And on a slightly different note, there are also artists out there asking really interesting questions about our relationship with AI that go beyond who created what.

Ryan: Yes, there is a fascinating art installation called Echoes of the Machine, which has been running since 2025 and continues through 2026. It explores co-creation between humans and AI through listening, imitation, and feedback loops. And what is really interesting is that this installation does not treat AI as a tool. It treats it as a relational partner.

Maya: Meaning we are not in the AI-as-a-hammer mode anymore, where you swing it and it makes a hole. This is something more like a genuine dialogue.

Ryan: Exactly. And that approach is going to be front and center at the Fak'ugesi Festival in Johannesburg this coming October. The core idea being that the technology itself becomes the subject of inquiry, not just a means to an end. You interrogate the machine rather than simply use it.

Maya: That is really a philosophical stance.

Ryan: It is. And it connects to a much bigger question about what actually emerges in the relationship between humans and AI agents when you genuinely put them in conversation with each other.

Maya: Which brings us perfectly to our favorite part of the show. The what if.

Ryan: The moment where we let ourselves think out loud a little.

Maya: So here is my question. Claude discovers a gene-editing mechanism in twenty-one hours. Three hundred robots are running a theme park. AI is composing ninety thousand tracks a day. What if we are living through a transformation so fast that our institutions simply do not have the tools to keep up with it?

Ryan: That is a real question. The Genesis Mission framework from the Department of Energy is one institutional response, but it is a response that takes months to put in place and years to produce real effects. Meanwhile, an AI can make a major scientific discovery in less than a day.

Maya: And the music framework is voluntary. It is not a law. It is not binding on anyone.

Ryan: Which brings us to a fundamental tension. The more capable AI becomes of acting quickly and autonomously, the more urgent the questions of oversight, validation, and governance become. A molecule discovered by AI in twenty-one hours still needs years of clinical trials before it reaches a patient. But you have to wonder how long that safety net holds.

Maya: What if the real question is not whether AI is going to replace humans, but whether our collective decision-making structures are agile enough to guide something that moves faster than anything we have ever dealt with before?

Ryan: That might actually be the real race here. Not a race between humans and machines. A race between the pace of AI and the human capacity to collectively define what we want to do with it.

Maya: A race against ourselves, when you get down to it.
