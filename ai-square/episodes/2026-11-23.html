Full transcript

Maya: Welcome to AI Squared, the podcast that breaks down artificial intelligence every single day from every possible angle. Today we have a packed show: tech giants setting aside their rivalry to fight fragmentation, Google laying the groundwork for a secure agentic infrastructure, and humanoid robots taking over research labs. Buckle up, let's get into it.

Maya: Ryan, we're kicking things off with some pretty big news that could genuinely reshape the future of AI agents. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft — direct competitors — deciding to work together. That's not something you see every day, right?

Ryan: It really isn't, and it's hard to overstate how significant this is. The alliance is called the Agentic AI Foundation, or AAIF, and the mission is straightforward: prevent the agentic AI ecosystem from fragmenting the same way the mobile internet did in its early days, where incompatible ecosystems couldn't talk to each other. The core idea is to establish open protocols so autonomous agents can interact with one another regardless of who built them.

Maya: So what exactly are these four giants actually bringing to the table?

Ryan: Real, battle-tested open-source technology. Anthropic contributed the MCP protocol, which now has ten thousand active servers running on it — that's a massive footprint. OpenAI brought AGENTS.md, a standard for describing agents. And Coinbase, which is also part of this initiative, contributed x402, a payment protocol for agents that has already processed a hundred and thirty-eight million transactions. So this isn't some vague letter of intent — this is a coalition building on existing, proven infrastructure.

Maya: What's striking to me is that these companies go after each other hard every single day, and yet here they are choosing to collaborate. Is this altruism or just cold hard pragmatism?

Ryan: Pure pragmatism, no question about it. If every player builds their own silo of agents that can't talk to anyone else's, the whole market grinds to a halt. Enterprise customers don't know which platform to bet on, developers have to rewrite everything for every platform, and ultimately everyone loses. By establishing common standards now, these players are making sure the agentic AI pie grows big enough that everyone gets a meaningful slice. It's exactly the same logic that drove the creation of web standards back in the nineties.

Maya: Alright, let's move on to tools and frameworks, and we've got two major stories to cover here. Let's start with Google, which just announced something they're calling Agent Compute Engine. Ryan, what exactly is this?

Ryan: It's the governance infrastructure Google is offering for running AI agents securely inside enterprise environments. At its core, Agent Compute Engine is built around several key components. First, an Agent Gateway that controls traffic and access policies. Then an Agent Registry, which keeps track of all the agents and tools that an organization has actually approved. And here's where it gets really interesting: each agent gets its own identity through a standard called SPIFFE, and the credentials — the access keys — are never stored inside the agent's own sandbox. They're injected from an external gateway.

Maya: Why does that detail about credentials actually matter?

Ryan: Because it addresses one of the most classic vulnerabilities in agentic architectures today. If an agent gets compromised — say, through a malicious prompt injection — and it's sitting on its own access keys, the attacker walks away with everything. By separating the agent's identity from its access rights, Google dramatically shrinks the attack surface. This is isolation designed for real production environments, not for demos.

Maya: And there's something new on the observability side too?

Ryan: Yes, Google is integrating OpenTelemetry, which has become the de facto standard for distributed monitoring. That lets engineering teams track what agents are actually doing, trace their actions, and debug when things go sideways. This announcement puts Google in direct competition with AWS and Azure, which both have their own agentic offerings — Bedrock AgentCore for AWS and Azure Foundry Agent Service for Microsoft — but Google is differentiating on that credential isolation approach.

Maya: We also have another tools story today, smaller in scale but worth talking about. a2sys announcing plans to make their Agent Studio free. What is Agent Studio?

Ryan: It's a development environment specifically built for creating AI agents. What's notable here isn't really the free pricing itself — it's the philosophy behind it. a2sys is drawing from what they call the DeepSeek Harness philosophy, with this principle that everything is a plugin. The goal is to avoid monolithic software and instead favor a modular architecture where every component — the language model, the tools, the orchestration layer, the interface — can be swapped out or recombined independently. It's an open-source positioning that puts them in direct competition with tools like OpenAI's CLI and Claude Code.

Maya: Now let's get into the research side of things, and honestly this one is a little unsettling. Ryan, can you walk us through what we're learning about LLM security in agentic contexts?

Ryan: Yeah, and this is a story that really deserves a closer look. Two recent security papers, AutoPI and PIPETTE, reveal something pretty troubling: red-teaming techniques based on reinforcement learning — meaning the automated methods used to find model vulnerabilities by attacking them — keep rediscovering the same types of attacks over and over again. And more importantly, traditional adversarial training just isn't enough to make an agent robust against prompt injections.

Maya: What does that actually mean for someone deploying an agent in production right now?

Ryan: It means that if you train your agent to resist known attacks, you're not necessarily making it resistant to unknown ones. And in an agentic system where the agent can read emails, access databases, execute code — the consequences of a successful prompt injection go way beyond an inappropriate response. The agent can be manipulated into taking real actions in the real world. It's exactly what the paper's title captures: LLMs are bad at keeping secrets, and agents make that problem worse.

Maya: And there's no silver bullet emerging from this research?

Ryan: No silver bullet. What these papers point toward is the need for architectural approaches — like what Google is doing with credential isolation — that limit what an agent can do even if it is compromised. It's the principle of least privilege applied to AI agents. We keep coming back to it.

Maya: Let's close out with industry impact, and we've got two very different stories that together show just how wide the agentic AI footprint is becoming. First up: Danaher and Microsoft partnering to automate scientific experimentation in the lab. Ryan, how ambitious is this?

Ryan: Extremely ambitious. Danaher is an industrial conglomerate that specializes in scientific and medical instruments. The partnership with Microsoft is aimed at building lab instruments driven by AI agents running on Microsoft Foundry. The idea is that a researcher expresses a scientific intent — I want to test the effect of this compound on these cells under these conditions — and the agent translates that into a full experimental plan, physically controls the instruments, interprets the results, and proposes corrections in real time.

Maya: So the robot is actually running the experiment, not just analyzing the data afterward?

Ryan: Exactly. We're talking about a complete chain: from the physical instrument all the way through to the data, from the workflows down to the physical execution of corrective actions. This is no longer AI assisting a researcher after the fact — this is AI conducting the experiment autonomously. The architecture runs on Azure and Microsoft Foundry, which makes it a concrete real-world application of cloud agentic platforms in an industrial setting.

Maya: And then in a completely different direction, Nvidia announcing at GTC Berlin a major expansion of its Isaac platform for humanoid robotics. What's the headline change here?

Ryan: What's changing is that Nvidia is formalizing a full-stack open-source strategy for robotics. Isaac now includes Agile Robots' humanoid platform Agile ONE SN, a new runtime called Isaac OS, an updated version of Isaac Lab for simulation-based training, and data resources for robotic learning. This is no longer Nvidia just supplying GPUs and isolated infrastructure — this is Nvidia planting its flag across the entire chain: hardware, simulation, AI training tools, and partnerships with physical robot manufacturers. At this pace, the platform will be doing everything short of making you coffee.

Maya: And to wrap up, we've got a data point today that really captures the tensions playing out across professional industries right now. A Wolters Kluwer report on US accounting firms finds that eighty-eight percent of professionals are using generative AI on a daily basis, and sixty-eight percent are already using agentic AI. And yet only thirteen percent of firms actually meet the required threshold for technological maturity. Ryan, how do you read those numbers?

Ryan: It's a really telling snapshot of a dynamic we're seeing everywhere right now. Tool adoption is way ahead of the infrastructure needed to support it responsibly. Professionals are using AI because it's available, because it's useful, and sometimes because their competitors are already doing it — but the processes, the governance policies, the output validation systems, none of that has kept pace. Thirteen percent maturity against eighty-eight percent daily adoption is a massive gap. And in a field like accounting, where mistakes carry direct legal and financial consequences, that gap has very real implications.

Maya: Is this what you'd call the adoption paradox?

Ryan: That's exactly what it is. And it's not unique to accounting — it's cutting across pretty much every professional services sector. Technology moves faster than organizations do. Which is exactly what makes the governance frameworks we've been talking about throughout this episode so important, whether that's Google's Agent Compute Engine, the AAIF protocols, or frameworks like the one mEinstein is proposing for evaluating consumer intent in agentic commerce.

Maya: Right, and we didn't actually get a chance to dig into mEinstein. Quick rundown?

Ryan: mEinstein lays out a four-step framework for evaluating a consumer's intent before an AI agent completes a transaction on their behalf: context, intent, readiness, and authorized action. The core idea is that an agent detecting a goal in a consumer's behavior should not automatically be granted permission to act on that consumer's behalf. It's an important distinction that sets the foundation for more nuanced governance of agentic commerce.

Maya: That's a perfect note to end on. What today really drives home is one central question: how do we govern agents that are becoming increasingly capable of acting in the real world? The AAIF working on common standards, Google isolating credentials, researchers sounding the alarm on prompt injections — all of these threads converge on the same challenge: governing autonomous agents. This isn't a theoretical debate anymore. It's the operational challenge of right now.

Thanks so much for spending part of your day with us here on AI Squared. See you tomorrow for another episode, with all the AI news that never, ever stops. Talk to you then.
