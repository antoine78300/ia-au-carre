Full transcript

Maya: Good morning and welcome to AI Squared, the podcast that breaks down artificial intelligence in your everyday life! It is Saturday, you are probably still in your pajamas, coffee or tea in hand, and we are right here with you. Ryan and I have been digging through everything that happened in the AI world this week to bring you only the stuff that actually matters. So get comfortable, let's get into it!

Ryan: Good morning Maya, good morning everyone! I am ready to go, double espresso in hand, and trust me, this week's news does not need any caffeine to keep you wide awake.

Maya: Alright Ryan, lay it on me. Where do we start?

Ryan: We start with something that genuinely stopped me in my tracks this week. A researcher at Oxford University, Fei Hao, is proposing that we draw a clear line between two major classes of AI entities. On one side, you have physical robots, machines that live in actual bodies in the real world. On the other side, a brand new category he calls "resident personal agents," things like Meta Muse, xAI's Grok Bot, or OpenAI's Dots. And the distinction he draws is honestly fascinating.

Maya: So what exactly is the difference? Because right now we are swimming in terminology and it can get overwhelming fast.

Ryan: Right, so here is the core idea. These resident agents do not just complete a task and shut down. They run continuously in the background, they remember your long-term goals, and they work on your behalf over time. Fei Hao actually gives them a specific name: he calls them "cognitive symbionts." The whole point is that these are not tools you pick up and put down whenever you need them. They are intelligences that embed themselves into your daily life on a permanent, ongoing basis.

Maya: Cognitive symbiont... okay that sounds like it is straight out of a sci-fi novel. But what does it actually look like in practice?

Ryan: Picture an AI that knows you have been working toward a big professional project for the past three months. It has tracked how your priorities have shifted over time. It anticipates what you need before you even ask, and you never have to explain yourself from scratch every single day. Not a chatbot you restart from zero every morning, but something that genuinely travels with you through time. That is what the resident concept is all about. And Fei Hao argues this category deserves its own serious academic conversation, completely separate from the debate around robots.

Maya: So what do you make of it? Does the distinction actually hold up?

Ryan: I think it does, and here is why it matters. In a lot of public conversations about AI, we lump everything together: humanoid robots, chatbots, background assistants. But the stakes are genuinely different for each. A robot that does your dishes is one thing. An AI that knows your life goals and is making decisions on your behalf while you are asleep, that is an entirely different conversation we need to be having.

Maya: And speaking of robots, we actually have some fresh news this week from Ant Group, the Chinese fintech giant!

Ryan: Yes, LingBot 2.0! So Ant Group, through their subsidiary Robbyant, launched what they describe as a full-stack system for physical robots. And what makes it interesting is their approach: a single AI brain capable of running across different interchangeable robot bodies. They call it the "one brain, multiple embodiments" principle. One mind, many possible physical forms.

Maya: So the AI can literally hop from one body to another?

Ryan: That is the idea. The system handles 3D spatial perception, environmental prediction, motor control, and in theory all of that can adapt to different physical platforms. Think of it like plugging the same brain into an industrial arm, then into a delivery robot, then into something else entirely.

Maya: That sounds pretty impressive. But I did come across something that pumps the brakes a little on all the excitement, right?

Ryan: Oh, you are talking about the CoRL 2026 conference recap from Austin! Yeah, and honestly it is a breath of fresh air. The ChinaTalk team who attended put out a pretty candid assessment. The title of their piece is literally "Robots Still Suck." And they lay out how, despite real progress on benchmarks, hardware, and reinforcement learning, robots remain fundamentally unreliable when it comes to actual real-world tasks. Commercial adoption is still way behind what the marketing hype would have you believe.

Maya: So robots can crush it in the lab but still struggle once they hit the real world.

Ryan: Exactly. And it is worth saying out loud, because we tend to confuse academic progress on very controlled benchmarks with what actually works in a warehouse or someone's kitchen. Those two things are moving at very different speeds.

Maya: Alright, let's stay in tech but shift gears. There is a story this week that genuinely caught me off guard, and it is about OpenAI and their robotics strategy.

Ryan: This one is a really bold bet. OpenAI has dissolved its dedicated robotics team. And before anyone panics, they are not walking away from robotics. They are folding those researchers back into their general research groups. And the logic behind it is essentially this: we do not need an AI purpose-built for robots. Our generalist models, starting with GPT-6 Astra, can directly control physical bodies.

Maya: Is that a risky move, or is it actually obvious to people who are deep in this space?

Ryan: It is a clear bet, and it is one that others are willing to make. Wang Xingxing, who runs Unitree, a well-known robot manufacturer, said that testing GPT-6 back in October made him genuinely more optimistic about this direction. The idea is that generalist models are becoming powerful enough to understand the physical world without needing a specialized architecture built just for robotics. If that turns out to be true, it completely reshapes how robotics research is organized.

Maya: Okay, let's shift to a topic that is really close to my heart this morning, AI and medicine. And the news here is nothing short of remarkable.

Ryan: Remarkable is the right word. This week we got major announcements from basically every big name in the global pharmaceutical industry: GSK, Bristol Myers Squibb, Novartis, Eli Lilly, Novo Nordisk, AstraZeneca, Sanofi. Every single one of them is saying they have integrated agentic AI across their entire drug development pipeline.

Maya: And when we say the entire pipeline, what does that actually cover?

Ryan: Everything. From initial molecule design, to candidate drug screening, to risk identification. And some of the concrete numbers being thrown out are genuinely jaw-dropping. Sanofi says they cut at least a year off the development timeline for a drug called lunsekimig. Novartis says their AI tools identified five new therapeutic targets for kidney diseases.

Maya: And the number that really floored me was the one from Novo Nordisk.

Ryan: Yes! Novo Nordisk gets really specific about it. Their process used to run through eight development cycles, each one lasting around eight weeks. With agentic AI, they are down to three cycles, each reduced to four weeks. Do the math: you go from sixty-four weeks down to twelve. That is an absolutely staggering leap.

Maya: And against that backdrop, Anthropic going public this week makes complete sense.

Ryan: Totally. On November nineteenth, Anthropic pulled off what is being called the largest IPO in history, listing on the Nasdaq under the ticker ANT. The total raise, counting the IPO and a simultaneous private placement, comes in at over one hundred and nineteen billion dollars. It is a mind-bending number.

Maya: And Dario Amodei, the CEO, was pretty direct about where they are headed in healthcare.

Ryan: He said that within five to ten years, AI could fundamentally transform drug discovery and clinical trials. And they already have a partnership with Genmab to build AI agents specifically for biotech research and development. So this is not just vision-casting, there are actual projects already underway.

Maya: Now, there is another science story from this week that is a lot more unsettling. A team used AI to generate viruses.

Ryan: Yes, and this one deserves a serious conversation. Samuel King and his team used AI to generate three hundred bacteriophages. Those are viruses that target bacteria, not humans directly. Sixteen of them turned out to be viable and were successfully tested in the lab. The results were published in Science back in the summer of 2026.

Maya: So AI designing viruses that actually work.

Ryan: The research is framed as a breakthrough for developing new treatments against antibiotic-resistant bacteria, and that is a genuinely important area of medicine. But obviously, the same capability that lets you design beneficial viruses could theoretically be turned in a much darker direction. Biosecurity researchers are sounding the alarm: if AI can design viable biological agents, the guardrails against misuse become absolutely critical, and they need to be in place now.

Maya: It is one of those discoveries that makes you realize AI amplifies everything at once. The breakthroughs and the dangers, side by side.

Ryan: Exactly. And this is an area where regulatory decisions cannot afford to lag behind, because the technology is not waiting around for anyone.

Maya: Let's talk about creativity and culture, because this week artists pushed back in a big, organized way, and not just behind closed doors in the music industry.

Ryan: At the ARIA Awards in Australia, which is basically Australia's version of the Grammys, Tim Minchin, who a lot of musical theater fans will know well, delivered a really powerful speech about protecting human creativity and copyright in the age of generative AI. And more than just the speech, he launched a formal national campaign with other Australian artists called "Not AI's to Take."

Maya: I mean, the name says it all!

Ryan: Pretty hard to misread that message, yes. The campaign is pushing back against the unauthorized use of copyrighted works to train generative AI models. And what stands out is that this is no longer just individuals speaking up. You are seeing organized, structured, nationally coordinated mobilization.

Maya: And Australian musicians are not alone here. We also had Nick Cave and Max Richter speaking out in November.

Ryan: Right. But this is a campaign, with a name, an organization, and clear staying power. And meanwhile, over in the US, Florida State University is launching a full course on music and AI next spring, the first of its kind at that school.

Maya: How are students responding to all of this?

Ryan: It is really split, and that split is telling. Some students see delegating musical creation to an AI as a form of cheating, a kind of creative shortcut that feels fundamentally dishonest. Others are genuinely excited to explore the possibilities. And the professors themselves are coming at it from wildly different angles depending on their backgrounds. This course is basically a mirror of the tension an entire generation is working through right now: what actually makes something authentically human?

Maya: And that is the perfect bridge to our "What If" segment of the day. Ryan, what is your big forward-looking thought this week?

Ryan: Here is what has been living in the back of my mind. This morning we talked about resident AI agents tracking your long-term goals, AI massively accelerating drug discovery, AI designing viable viruses, and AI shaking up the whole idea of creative authorship. So here is the question I want to sit with: ten years from now, what does the concept of human effort actually mean?

Maya: That is a big one. Unpack it for us.

Ryan: We place enormous value on effort. We value the fact that a medication took a team of researchers years of hard work. We value the fact that a song was written by someone who went through something real, who actually lived it. If AI compresses that time, the eight cycles Novo Nordisk cut down to three, the years of drug development squeezed into a few months, does the outcome carry the same weight? Can we separate the value of the result itself from the value of the process that produced it?

Maya: And with music it gets even more emotional.

Ryan: It does, because we tend to listen to a song differently when we know the artist lived it. When we find out it was generated in seconds by a model, something shifts in how we relate to it, even if the notes are exactly the same. Maybe the real question is not "can AI create something beautiful?" Maybe it is "are we actually willing to feel something for what it creates?" I do not have the answer. But I think all of us are going to have to wrestle with that question very soon.

Maya: That is exactly the right note to end on today. And honestly, that is what I love about this show. We do not pretend to have all the answers.

Ryan: Which is a good thing, because if we did, we would run out of material pretty fast!

Maya: On that note, thank you so much for spending your morning with us. Take some time to enjoy your Saturday. Go outside, take a walk, pick up a book, preferably one written by an actual human being. We will be back with a brand new episode of AI Squared tomorrow. See you then!

Ryan: See you tomorrow everyone!
