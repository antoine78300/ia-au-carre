Full transcript

Emma: Hey everyone, welcome to AI Squared, your daily podcast on artificial intelligence! Quick heads-up before we dive in: Maya and Ryan are out today because of a technical hiccup on Microsoft's end, so Liam and I are stepping in to hold down the fort. They'll be back as soon as things are sorted out on that side. In the meantime, get comfortable, it is Sunday, pajamas and coffee are absolutely acceptable, and we have got a packed episode full of genuinely fascinating stuff.

Liam: Hey everybody! And honestly, for a Sunday, the AI world did not get the memo about taking a day off, so neither did we. Let's get into it!

Emma: So Liam, let me just throw it right at you to kick things off: what was the single thing that stopped you in your tracks this week?

Liam: No hesitation. Hands. Atlas's hands.

Emma: A robot's hands?

Liam: Exactly. Boston Dynamics just unveiled the next-generation hands for their Atlas robot, and they're calling them the GR3. And the detail that changes everything is the number of degrees of freedom.

Emma: Okay, hold on, degrees of freedom. Can you break that down for listeners who are enjoying their Sunday morning coffee and really do not want to think about mechanical engineering right now?

Liam: Sure. It is basically the number of independent movements a joint, or a set of joints, can make. The more you have, the more agile the hand is, and the more precise and complex the movements it can pull off. The previous generation of Atlas hands had seven degrees of freedom. The GR3 hands have thirteen.

Emma: That is almost double. That is a massive leap.

Liam: It really is. And Boston Dynamics points out something interesting: these hands are designed to be anthropomorphic, meaning they are modeled after the human hand, but slightly larger and stronger than an average human hand.

Emma: So this is not just a flashy demo for a YouTube video?

Liam: Not at all, and this is where it gets really serious. The whole point is object manipulation in real, uncontrolled environments. Up until now, industrial robots worked beautifully inside highly structured factories, where every object is always in exactly the same place, always in the same position. But the moment you take them out of that perfect setup, things fall apart. With thirteen degrees of freedom, you start to have hands that can grasp a wide variety of objects, adapt on the fly, and not send everything crashing to the floor just because something is slightly out of place.

Emma: It is the jump from the lab to the real world.

Liam: Exactly. Boston Dynamics says it outright: this is a critical step toward moving robotic agents from the prototype stage to actual real-world production manipulation. And what really gets me excited here is that for a long time the conversation was all about AI intelligence, large language models, reasoning capabilities. But the actual bottleneck for robots operating in the physical world was almost always dexterity.

Emma: The brain was there but the hands could not keep up.

Liam: Perfect way to put it. And now we are starting to see both advancing at the same time.

Emma: And that is actually a great segue into what comes next, because we have got quite a few more robotics stories this week coming from all over the world.

Liam: Yeah, and what strikes me is just how global this conversation has become.

Emma: So let's move into our second block: experiments and projects worth watching. And we have got two very different stories here. The first one comes out of China, with BYD.

Liam: BYD, which most people know as an electric vehicle company.

Emma: And apparently they are not stopping there. What did they announce?

Liam: BYD filed a patent for their first in-house humanoid robot. The design was officially unveiled through a publication from China's National Intellectual Property Administration on October 9th. But what makes this genuinely interesting is not just the fact that the robot exists. It is where they plan to deploy it first.

Emma: I would assume their factories, right?

Liam: Nope. Their car dealerships.

Emma: Oh, interesting.

Liam: BYD salespeople might soon have a robot colleague on the showroom floor. The idea is to use it for customer service, demonstrating vehicle features, walking customers through infotainment systems. Standardized, face-to-face tasks with real customers.

Emma: That is a really strategic deployment choice. Instead of jumping straight into mass production lines, they are starting with their own retail network.

Liam: That is exactly it. They control the environment, they know their dealerships inside and out, they know which tasks are repetitive. It is a smart way to test in real-world conditions without taking on massive risk. And it sends a powerful signal to consumers: BYD is not just a car manufacturer anymore, it is a technology company in the fullest sense.

Emma: And staying in China on the robotics beat, we also have news about Geek Plus.

Liam: Yes, and Geek Plus is way under the radar for most people but it is a really significant player in the industry. They specialize in industrial robotics, and they just invested fifty million yuan, roughly seven point five million dollars, to acquire a twenty percent stake in Beijing Shenyi Robotics Technology.

Emma: What is the interesting angle there?

Liam: It is the strategic shift it reveals. Geek Plus does not want to just be an equipment supplier anymore. Their stated goal is to become an embodied intelligence platform. Which means moving away from robots that execute fixed programs, toward systems that can actually perceive their environment, understand it, and make decisions in an autonomous and adaptive way.

Emma: The difference between a Roomba vacuum and something much, much more sophisticated.

Liam: Yeah, that works well. The Roomba follows a program. What we are talking about here is a robot that recognizes that the warehouse floor plan changed this morning and just figures it out on its own. And we also have some academic validation of this whole trend from the Beijing Institute of Technology.

Emma: The university, not a company.

Liam: Right. The IN2Bot team from the School of Automation won two international championships in embodied AI. The first was in language-guided bimanual manipulation with vision and touch, and the second was in autonomous navigation, both at the IEEE International Conference on Robotics and Automation.

Emma: Language-guided bimanual manipulation with vision and touch. So that is a robot using both hands at the same time while seeing and feeling what it is touching?

Liam: Exactly. And guided by language, which means you can give it instructions in plain natural language. It is the convergence of several different technologies all at once.

Emma: And this came out of a university.

Liam: Which confirms something important: Chinese robotics research in embodied AI is genuinely world-class right now. And the connection between universities and industry over there is extremely tight.

Emma: Alright, let's shift into our third block, AI and society, and here we are stepping completely away from robotics and into music. And honestly, this was the story that hit me the hardest personally this week.

Liam: Same for me, and I say that as someone who usually geeks out on technical architectures. But this one raises very human questions.

Emma: So let's set the scene. Deezer, the music streaming platform, just released some pretty staggering numbers. Forty-four percent of the tracks being uploaded to their platform are AI-generated.

Liam: Almost half.

Emma: And in June 2026, we are talking about ninety thousand tracks uploaded per day, all AI-generated. That is a huge number.

Liam: And the wild twist is that ninety-seven percent of listeners cannot tell them apart from music made by humans.

Emma: Which is fascinating in itself. Our ears just cannot tell the difference.

Liam: But our conscience can. And that is where the BBC data gets really compelling. Despite AI being technically invisible in music, more than fifty percent of people surveyed say they care whether something was made by a human or not, and that they want humans to be involved in the creative process.

Emma: So we cannot tell, but we still want to know, and we still prefer the human.

Liam: There is a premium for authenticity emerging out of this. Some people are starting to call it the human premium. The perceived value of something created by a human goes up precisely because AI can now replicate everything technically.

Emma: And that connects directly to the other music story we have this week, INDUSTRY.AIFF.

Liam: Yes, launched on October 9th by Pat Villaceran, the founder of CITY JAM. The concept is a music acceleration platform that explicitly positions the original sound of human artists as a critical asset, a value in itself, in the face of the saturation of AI-generated music.

Emma: And what does the platform actually do in practice?

Liam: It combines several things: education, professional development, live sessions, and commercial opportunities for emerging artists. It is an artist-centered business model, positioned as a direct alternative to generative AI music production.

Emma: And this brings us back to those Deezer numbers, which are kind of brutal when you sit with them. Ninety thousand AI tracks a day, but only one to three percent of those tracks generate any real streams.

Liam: Which tells you that volume does not equal engagement. You can flood the platforms with AI music, but people are ultimately not listening to much of it. And that paradoxically creates an opportunity for human artists who know how to stand out.

Emma: That is almost reassuring.

Liam: It is complicated though. The problem is that inside that ocean of content, getting discovered is becoming harder and harder for a human artist. Which is exactly why platforms like INDUSTRY.AIFF are trying to carve out a dedicated space.

Emma: Alright, it is time for our favorite segment. The what if. Liam, what is your open question for today?

Liam: What if physical dexterity becomes the next great challenge, even bigger than intelligence itself?

Emma: Say more.

Liam: We have spent years talking about artificial intelligence as the central problem to solve. Can AI reason, can it create, does it truly understand. And we have made spectacular progress on all of those fronts. But this week, with Boston Dynamics and those thirteen degrees of freedom, with BYD putting humanoids on the showroom floor, with Geek Plus pushing toward robots that adapt to their surroundings, what we are seeing is that the real bottleneck for changing the physical world might actually be the ability to reliably manipulate real objects.

Emma: Because the physical world is so much less predictable than a digital environment.

Liam: Exactly. In the digital space, AI is already everywhere and it performs incredibly well. But in the physical world, an object that falls over, an uneven surface, a product sitting at an unexpected angle, any of that can bring the whole thing to a halt. What if the real challenge over the next few years is not making models smarter, but giving embodied agents hands that are nimble enough to actually operate in our world?

Emma: And that raises a real societal question too. Because if robots can reliably handle complex physical tasks in warehouses, in dealerships, in restaurants...

Liam: We are entering territory where a whole lot of skilled manual jobs are in the picture. And that loops us right back to the human premium idea we talked about with music. Is it going to apply to physical work too? Will people want a human barista making their coffee even if a robot can technically do it just as well?

Emma: Some people will absolutely say yes. And others will call that pure sentimentality.

Liam: And the answer is probably going to look very different depending on the culture, the generation, the context. That is what makes it so fascinating. The technology moves forward, but the social responses are genuinely unpredictable.

Emma: And on that note, that wraps up this Sunday edition of AI Squared. A huge thank you to everyone who tuned in this morning, whether you are fully awake or still in full lazy Sunday mode.

Liam: Ultra-dexterous robot hands, humanoids on the car lot, and a human premium rising in the music world. Sunday is definitely a solid day to sit with all of that.

Emma: We will be back with more AI tomorrow. Take care of yourselves, and see you tomorrow!

Liam: See you tomorrow, everyone!
