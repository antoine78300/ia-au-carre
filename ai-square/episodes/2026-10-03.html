Maya: Good morning and welcome to AI Squared! It's Saturday, you're probably still in your pajamas, coffee's brewing, and we are right here doing what we do, bringing you a packed batch of AI news to kick off your weekend on the right note. I'm Maya, and as always, Ryan is here with me. Hey Ryan!

Ryan: Hey Maya! Saturday morning, I'm on my second cup already, because honestly this week had some stories that genuinely made my jaw drop.

Maya: Perfect, because that is exactly what we're here for. So let's dive right in with the biggest wow moment of the week. Ryan, what's the story that blew you away the most?

Ryan: Okay, fair warning, this one sounds like science fiction, except it's just science. Researchers at Chalmers University and the University of Gothenburg in Sweden have developed what they're calling an AI scientist. An autonomous agent that can design its own scientific experiments, plan them, run them, and validate the results, all without any human involvement.

Maya: Hold on. No human involvement? Like, start to finish?

Ryan: Start to finish. This is what researchers call full loop closure. Imagine a lab that wakes up in the morning, decides what it wants to test that day, runs the experiments, looks at the results, and draws its own conclusions. And this has been operationally validated, not just theorized on paper.

Maya: That is mind-bending. And what field are we actually talking about here?

Ryan: Biology. This AI agent can generate and validate genuinely new biological discoveries. Professor Ross King, who leads the computer science side of the project, says these autonomous labs will allow biological systems to be studied in a systematic way that goes far beyond what's humanly possible today.

Maya: So what does the human researcher actually do in all of this? Just watch?

Ryan: That's the real question. Right now, humans supervise, set the big-picture direction, and validate whether the discoveries are actually useful. But the hands-on bench work, the day-to-day experimental grind, that gets handed off to the machine. And what's fascinating is that this could potentially free researchers up for the really high-level creative thinking, forming bold hypotheses, rather than grinding through repetitive experiments over and over.

Maya: And this isn't the only example of that kind of shift this week, right?

Ryan: Right. There's also Schrödinger, a company well known in the world of computational pharmacology, and they're deploying an AI agent called Bunsen. Bunsen is basically the pharmaceutical cousin of that Swedish AI scientist. It autonomously orchestrates drug discovery workflows and actually delegates complex tasks to human researchers.

Maya: Okay, that's a fascinating flip. The machine is delegating to the humans, not the other way around.

Ryan: Exactly, and that is a genuine paradigm shift. In the traditional model, the human researcher tells the computer what to calculate. With Bunsen, the AI agent runs the whole workflow and hands certain tasks to humans because it needs them. Schrödinger is positioning itself directly as a player in the agentic pharmaceutical R&D space.

Maya: Wild times we're living in. Alright, let's move on to our next big block, the experiments and projects that have people either excited or seriously scratching their heads. Ryan, you were keeping an eye on robotics this week?

Ryan: Yeah, and it was a busy week on that front. Faraday Future, a company most people know as a somewhat turbulent electric vehicle maker, showed up at the IROS 2026 conference with something pretty ambitious. They're calling it a One-Brain Multi-Form architecture, built around a four-component embodied AI ecosystem.

Maya: One-Brain Multi-Form sounds like a Marvel supervillain name.

Ryan: A little bit, yeah. But the idea behind it is that a single AI brain can drive very different physical forms, a humanoid robot, a car, an industrial system. The four components are a brain, devices, a data factory, and solutions. Faraday wants to take this approach well beyond humanoid robots and into vehicles and industrial productivity.

Maya: And beyond Faraday, are there other players making moves in embodied AI?

Ryan: Several. First there's Ropedia, a startup founded in Singapore in 2025, and they're launching an academic program to give researchers and universities access to their HOMIE Gen2 platform. The idea is to collect real-world multimodal data, movements, interactions, navigation, the kind of data that is absolutely critical for training robots. They're already supporting more than twenty robotics companies across Asia.

Maya: And Vietnam is getting in on this too, right?

Ryan: Yeah, this one genuinely surprised me. Vietnam held its very first embodied AI conference on October 2nd in Hanoi, bringing together companies, universities, and research institutes. They're planning to use the Hoa Lac Hi-Tech Park for robotics demonstrations and sandboxes focused on mobile robotics and smart manufacturing. It's a strong signal that embodied AI is becoming a real question of technological sovereignty on a global scale.

Maya: This is no longer a club for a handful of wealthy countries.

Ryan: Not even close. And to round out the picture, over in Stuttgart at the Humanoid Robots Summit 2026, three European industrial players, Vector Informatik, PEM Motion, and FMC3 Robotics, signed a memorandum of understanding to jointly develop a standardized data infrastructure for embodied AI in industry.

Maya: Standardization is usually a sign that a sector is hitting its maturity phase.

Ryan: Exactly. When people start pushing for common standards, it means they're thinking about large-scale deployment and they want systems from different manufacturers to actually be able to talk to each other.

Maya: Love it. Now we get to the chapter that always turns up the heat, AI and society, culture, creativity. And this week there's a pretty surprising lineup of people ending up on the same side of the argument.

Ryan: Yeah, and it's a pretty explosive mix. On one side you have an author, Etienne Guereau, who just published a book called The Student Is the Master, and he's calling generative AI applied to music an intellectual fraud and what he describes as mega-plagiarism. He's preparing a video to call out what he sees as the saturation of tonal grammar and the hijacking of copyrighted material.

Maya: Mega-plagiarism is a strong charge.

Ryan: Very strong, and it connects to a broader creative pushback against the replacement of artistic jobs. And then on the other side, and this one is genuinely unexpected, Pope Leo XIV. On October 1st, he called on the Catholic Church to stand alongside artists against generative AI, saying that algorithms lack a divine spark.

Maya: The Vatican coming out against generative AI in music. Did not see that coming.

Ryan: Nobody saw that coming. And it's not just a throwaway comment. The Vatican is preparing a formal symposium for 2027 and an encyclical on culture before Easter 2027. An encyclical is the most solemn document a pope can publish. The fact that the oldest institution in the world is taking this seriously enough to put it in writing at that level says a lot.

Maya: It's fascinating to see such different voices converging. A critical author, a pope, musicians. And yet the music industry itself is also making moves this week.

Ryan: Right, and this is where it gets really interesting, because the market is trying to respond to these tensions in its own way. Stability AI just confirmed it raised 76 million dollars in a Series B round, and the remarkable detail is that Universal Music Group, Warner Music Group, and Sony Music Group all participated directly with equity investments. This is the first time all three majors have invested simultaneously in the same AI company.

Maya: All three majors at once? These are the same companies that were first in line to complain about their catalogs being scraped.

Ryan: And that's exactly the tension here. What convinced the majors is that Stable Audio 3.0, their music generator launched in May 2026, uses only licensed music data sourced from AudioSparx and Creative Commons. Zero unauthorized content. They're investing in something they themselves approved.

Maya: And running alongside that, there's this debate about separate charts for AI music?

Ryan: Exactly. Nicholas Gunn, founder of Blue Dot Music, is documenting the emergence of two distinct models on streaming platforms. One model with labeling that flags content as AI-generated, and one artist-centered model without labeling. The debate is getting intense around whether AI music should have its own charts, separate from music made by humans.

Maya: It's kind of like the Paralympics versus the Olympics. You create separate categories so the competition stays meaningful.

Ryan: That's an interesting analogy. Except in this case we don't even have a clear definition yet of what fair actually means in this context.

Maya: Alright, we've made it to my favorite part of the show. The what if. Ryan, with everything we've covered this week, where does your head go?

Maya: Say more.

Ryan: This week we saw an AI scientist running experiments on its own in Sweden, and a pharmaceutical agent telling humans what tasks to handle. We saw artists and a pope defending something irreducibly human in creative work, while at the same time the major labels invest in an ethically built music AI. And we saw industrial players standardizing to roll out robots on factory floors.

Maya: It's getting hard to tell who's the tool and who's the one using it.

Ryan: Exactly. What if the real question isn't whether AI will replace humans, but how we collectively decide which spaces we want to keep human? Not because the machine can't do it, but because we choose that certain acts only have meaning when a human does them. Maybe artistic creation. Maybe the act of care. Maybe the ethical choice.

Maya: And this week we're seeing very different kinds of actors start to try to draw those lines. Researchers, industries, religious institutions, musicians.

Ryan: And what strikes me is that nobody agrees on where the line should go. Which suggests we're going to be living with this debate for a long time. And honestly, that might be a good thing. A society that stops debating these questions is a society that has already given up its right to choose.
