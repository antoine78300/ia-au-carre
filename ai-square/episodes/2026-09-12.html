Full transcript

Maya: Good morning and welcome to AI Square! It is Saturday, the sun is out — or at least we're hoping it is wherever you're listening — and we are so glad you're spending part of your morning with us. Ryan and I grabbed our coffees, sat down, and went through everything that happened this week in the world of AI, and honestly, we were spoiled for choice. Robot dogs paying for your groceries, hidden proteins, startup drama... let's get into it!

Ryan: Good morning Maya, good morning everyone! Yeah, it has been a genuinely packed week. And if I had to sum up the overall vibe in one word, I'd say "embodied." Because the big theme right now is AI stepping off the screen and into the physical world.

Maya: Embodied AI — we're hearing that phrase more and more. But let's kick things off with something that totally stopped me in my tracks this morning. A robot dog that does your shopping and pays for everything itself. Ryan, tell me this is real.

Ryan: It is real, and it is actually up and running. Alipay, the Chinese payments giant, just announced a partnership with a company called Gaode Momentum. Together they have deployed what they are calling Dog Leg Pay — yes, that is the actual name — on an autonomous robot dog. The idea is that a quadruped robot can move through a space, identify products, pick them up, and complete a financial transaction entirely on its own. No human involvement whatsoever.

Maya: Wait, so the robot is basically pulling out its own wallet?

Ryan: Exactly, except the wallet is an AI agent connected directly to Alipay's payment systems. And what is fascinating here is not just the fun novelty of a robot dog running errands. It is what this represents conceptually. We are embedding financial services directly into robotic bodies. AI is no longer just making decisions — it is acting, moving, paying.

Maya: And this landed the same week as another story in the exact same vein, right? Kinetix AI raising 75 million dollars?

Ryan: Yes, and that is what tells you this is not a one-off thing. Kinetix AI is a startup specializing in what the industry calls embodied AI. They just locked in 75 million dollars to develop robots that can intelligently interact with their physical environment. And in that same week, we learned that China just released a major strategic report announcing plans to multiply its computing capacity by seven by 2028, and projecting the embodied AI market at one trillion yuan. That is a staggering number.

Maya: So we are genuinely in a global race to see who ends up with the smartest, most autonomous robots?

Ryan: Absolutely. And that race comes with real friction, because another story this week pokes right at that sore spot. A Chinese humanoid robotics startup called JoyIn — backed by financial giant Ant — is accusing none other than OpenAI of copying their AI model.

Maya: Wow. Okay, what exactly is the accusation?

Ryan: JoyIn's CEO, Guo Renjie, is claiming that his team had presented their framework in California just weeks before OpenAI published something he says looks remarkably similar. He is pointing to similarities in the design, the technical architecture, even visual elements inspired by space. And he is specifically calling out the concept of model distillation — which is essentially when you train one model on the outputs of another model to capture its intelligence.

Maya: That is an extremely serious allegation.

Ryan: Very serious. And it lands in the middle of an already intensely charged tech rivalry between China and the US. What makes this particularly interesting is that it is the first time we are really seeing this kind of accusation go in this direction — a Chinese company calling out an American one, rather than the other way around, which is what we usually hear. It shows that this competition is genuinely running both ways now.

Maya: Has OpenAI responded?

Ryan: Not officially, at least not as of when we recorded this. But this is absolutely one to watch. Okay, can we shift to something a little more uplifting?

Maya: Please! Because I have a story from the creative world that genuinely put a smile on my face. Google Flow Music and Nigeria. This one is a feel-good story.

Ryan: It really is, and it deserves a moment. Google has launched a platform called Flow Music, built on their Lyria 3.5 model, that lets you generate studio-quality music from a simple text description. You describe what you want to hear, and the model creates the music. But what makes this story really interesting is not the technology itself — it is who Google partnered with to roll it out.

Maya: The Nigerian AI film festival, NAIFF!

Ryan: Exactly. The Naija Artificial Intelligence Film Festival in Lagos. And on September 19th, there will be a live workshop where African creators can get hands-on with the tool. And that right there is a real shift. For a long time, these creative AI tools were unveiled in the US or Europe, at expensive tech conferences. Here, Google is going directly to an emerging market, working with local creators, and giving them access to the same tools.

Maya: And that raises such a fascinating question about what this means for African music specifically. Because African musical traditions — the rhythmic structures, the harmonies, the oral traditions — they are extraordinarily rich.

Ryan: Absolutely. Does Lyria 3.5 actually know how to capture afrobeat, Nigerian highlife, South African amapiano? We don't know yet. But the fact that Google is moving in this direction is significant. And it leads me right into another cultural story from this week, one that is a little more complicated.

Maya: Apple Music and the Made With AI labels?

Ryan: That's the one. Apple Music has decided to roll out a labeling system that clearly flags when a track was created in a substantial way by generative AI — tools like Suno or Udio, for example. But here's the thing — this is not a ban. Apple is not saying this music does not belong on our platform. They are saying you have the right to know what you are listening to.

Maya: And that is a really interesting position because it is neither pro nor anti — it is just transparent.

Ryan: That is exactly it. You can debate endlessly whether it is enough, whether it stigmatizes AI music, whether it is fair to listeners. But there is something fundamentally healthy about the idea that when you consume content, you know what you are consuming. And this is a call Apple made on their own, without waiting for a law or a regulation to force their hand.

Maya: Fair point. Alright, we have covered robots, music, geopolitical tension. But Ryan, you flagged a science story this week that genuinely blew your mind.

Ryan: Yeah, and honestly this might be the most consequential story of the week in terms of real-world impact on humanity, even though it is getting way less attention than the robot dog paying for groceries.

Maya: The hidden proteins?

Ryan: The hidden proteins. Researchers at the Sylvester Comprehensive Cancer Center used AI to analyze 214 million predicted proteins in the human proteome — that is the complete set of proteins our body can produce. And buried in all of that, they found proteins that had never been identified or studied before. Completely unexplored. And not only did they find them, they actually figured out what those proteins do.

Maya: For listeners who are not biologists, why do proteins matter so much?

Ryan: Proteins are essentially the molecular machinery of your body. They do everything — they relay signals between cells, they fight infections, they repair tissue. So when you discover proteins nobody knew existed, it is like finding parts of an engine you did not know were in there. It can explain diseases we did not understand. It can open up entirely new treatment pathways. And for cancer research specifically, the potential here is enormous.

Maya: And what is AI's actual role in this? Because 214 million proteins is a number that is hard to even wrap your head around.

Ryan: That is exactly where AI becomes irreplaceable. A human researcher could not analyze 214 million proteins in an entire lifetime. Neither could a whole team of researchers. AI can identify patterns, structural similarities, probable functions — at a scale and speed that is simply beyond human capability. And this fits into a bigger trend we saw play out this week.

Maya: Jeff Dean and Discovery Loop!

Ryan: Exactly. Jeff Dean, who was Google's chief scientist for years — he stepped down last month — he is now raising funds for a startup called Discovery Loop. The target valuation is around 50 billion dollars. And his goal is to automate scientific discovery itself using AI. Not automate one piece of the scientific process. Scientific discovery as a whole.

Maya: That is one seriously ambitious vision.

Ryan: It is almost dizzying when you think about it. The idea that AI becomes an active partner in solving humanity's biggest challenges — disease, climate change, energy. And on that note, Swiss researchers we mentioned briefly this week — they trained AI models to analyze NASA climate data and generate weather forecasts at extreme speed. We are talking about the potential to predict natural disasters in ways that could save thousands of lives.

Maya: So we have got AIs finding hidden proteins, forecasting storms, accelerating drug discovery... This kind of naturally brings us to our What If moment!

Ryan: Ha, we just got there on our own! Alright, let's go — What If — because we are right in the middle of something here.

Maya: The question I have been sitting with all morning is this. On one side, you have AI moving into the physical world — embodied robots, dog-shaped payment systems, humanoids. On the other side, you have AI diving into the infinitely small — hidden proteins, molecules, climate data. Are these two directions eventually going to meet somewhere?

Ryan: That is a great question. And I think yes, and probably sooner than we expect. Picture a medical robot — like CARES 4.0, which is being launched at a Hong Kong symposium in about ten days — a multimodal AI system built for healthcare. That is exactly the convergence point: an AI agent that understands complex biological data and can physically interact with a patient at the same time. Embodied AI in service of the most fundamental science there is.

Maya: And that brings us right back to the question of trust, doesn't it? Because now we are talking about AI agents making autonomous decisions — financial, medical, scientific. That is an enormous amount of responsibility.

Ryan: Immense. And that is actually why I find the Apple Music move interesting in this context. It is a tiny thing compared to surgical robots. But it is the same underlying principle: transparency as the foundation for trust. We tell you what the AI did. You decide what to do with that. And if we can apply that principle at the scale of autonomous robots and medical systems, we might actually have a solid foundation for building this relationship between humans and AI.

Maya: And what if the real legacy of this whole era turns out to be not the robots or the proteins, but the way we learned — or failed to learn — to trust these systems?

Ryan: What if. What if.

Maya: And on that note, that is a wrap for today! Thank you so much for hanging out with us this Saturday morning — we hope we gave you plenty to think about, and maybe a few conversation starters for the rest of your day. Take care of yourselves, and we will see you tomorrow for another episode of AI Square. See you tomorrow!

Ryan: See you tomorrow everyone!
