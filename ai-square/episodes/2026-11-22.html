Maya: Good morning and welcome to AI Squared, the daily podcast that breaks down artificial intelligence with curiosity and a whole lot of enthusiasm. It is Sunday, and you might still have your coffee in hand — hold onto it, because what we have for you today is absolutely worth your full attention. I am Maya, and joining me as always is Ryan, our resident AI analyst, for this weekend edition. We are rested, we are fired up, and honestly, the news this week had us both floored. Let's get into it.

Ryan: Hey Maya, hey everyone! Really glad to be here for this Sunday. And yeah, the coffee timing is spot on, because we are kicking things off with a big one.

Maya: So Ryan, give it to me straight. What was the story this week that genuinely blew your mind?

Ryan: Okay, no hesitation here — I'm taking you straight to outer space. So there's a teenager from California named Matteo Paz, and this kid built, entirely on his own, from home, an AI system capable of analyzing astronomical data from NASA. And that system detected one and a half million flickering objects sitting in the agency's archives.

Maya: Hold on. One and a half million objects. From a laptop?

Ryan: From a laptop. Not from some billion-dollar research facility. From his personal computer at home. And NASA officially recognized the breakthrough. The institution publicly acknowledged what this kid pulled off.

Maya: That is wild. So what exactly are these flickering objects?

Ryan: They are astronomical objects whose brightness changes over time — stars that pulse, potentially highly energetic phenomena, candidates for all kinds of discoveries. The thing is, NASA's archives are absolutely massive, and there is simply no way to analyze all of it by hand. And here comes an autonomous AI on a home computer, flagging one and a half million signals that nobody had ever catalogued before.

Maya: So basically, the barrier to entry for doing real cutting-edge science just collapsed?

Ryan: That is exactly it. And that is what makes this story so much bigger than just a feel-good headline. For decades, high-level scientific discovery required institutions, budgets, full teams. And now we are seeing that a well-designed AI agent can democratize access to fundamental research. It is no longer reserved for labs with nine-figure budgets.

Maya: And the astronomy community is actually taking this seriously? They are not brushing it off because it came from a teenager?

Ryan: Apparently not at all. Astronomers are paying attention, and honestly, that makes total sense. What matters is the quality of the detection, not the credentials of the person who made it. And maybe that is the real takeaway here: AI as a genuine equalizer in scientific research.

Maya: I love that story. Okay, let's stay in science territory because you also mentioned something earlier about Nvidia and IBM mixing AI with quantum computing?

Ryan: Yes, and this one is a totally different scale — we are going from a bedroom laptop to supercomputers. Nvidia and IBM announced a collaboration to use AI to correct errors in quantum computing calculations. And it opens the door to some very real, very concrete applications.

Maya: Because the big problem with quantum computers has always been that they make a lot of errors, right?

Ryan: Exactly. Qubits are incredibly sensitive to interference, which causes constant errors. And up until now, correcting those errors ate up an enormous amount of resources. The idea here is that AI steps in to eliminate those errors far more efficiently. And the real-world results are pretty impressive: a company called Terray Therapeutics managed to extract 76 percent of chemical reactions directly from scientific diagrams using this pipeline. Another company, Therna, doubled its accuracy in predicting protein expression. And Argonne National Laboratory is projecting a 1.6 times performance boost with twice the energy efficiency.

Maya: So AI is not replacing quantum computing — it is fixing it so that it actually becomes usable?

Ryan: That is a great way to put it. It is really an orchestration play: AI plus quantum plus the classical processors we already know, all working together in a pipeline. And that combination creates discovery capabilities that simply did not exist before.

Maya: Fascinating stuff. Let's step away from cosmology and supercomputers for a second and talk about some projects that are a little more, let's say, human-scale. Because there were a couple of experiments this week that are really worth pausing on. Ryan, you mentioned something earlier about a robot that explores faces?

Ryan: Yeah, so this one — I will be honest, when I first read it I had to go back and read it twice to make sure I had it right. The University of Nottingham, working with an art collective called Blast Theory, developed something they call the Facial Rover. It is a system that lets a person control a microscopic camera over their own face using a haptic controller and a conversational chatbot.

Maya: So I can explore my own face like it's some kind of lunar landscape?

Ryan: That is exactly the image. The camera is mounted on a soft robotic arm, and you guide it with your fingers through the controller. And while you're doing that, you can have an actual conversation with the AI system that accompanies you through the whole exploration.

Maya: But like... why? Is this art? Is it science? Both?

Ryan: Both, and that is precisely what makes it so interesting. It sits right at the intersection of artistic research and what researchers call embodied AI — AI that is embedded in a very physical, very intimate interaction with the human body. It raises questions about how we relate to our own faces, about intimacy, about what it even feels like to look at your own skin at a microscopic scale. And technically speaking, it is a demonstration of multimodal orchestration: soft robotics, haptic feedback, natural language dialogue — all happening in real time.

Maya: And on a less poetic, more industrial note, there was also Walden Robotics in the news this week?

Ryan: Yes, and this one is a significant development for manufacturing robotics. Walden Robotics is a startup that was founded in January 2026 — so barely ten months ago. Their first robot was deployed in March, which is two months after they were founded. And what sets them apart is their approach to continuous learning.

Maya: What does that mean exactly?

Ryan: So traditionally, most industrial robots are trained upfront, in controlled environments, and then deployed. Walden flips that model entirely: their fleet of robots keeps learning directly on the production floor, in real conditions. The robots are getting better while they are actually doing the job.

Maya: It's kind of like an employee who grows by doing the work rather than spending six months in training before they ever step onto the floor.

Ryan: Perfect analogy. And that changes everything in terms of adaptability. But — and this is important — there is a significant reality check in this week's broader industrial robotics news. A report notes that despite 48 billion dollars raised in the sector this year alone, actual adoption on factory floors is still stuck. And not for the reasons you might expect.

Maya: Not because of the robots themselves?

Ryan: No! The problem is data. The data collection infrastructure inside factories is just not there. Systems do not talk to each other, quality tracking is inconsistent. Basically, you can have the most sophisticated robot in the world — but if the production data is not clean and interoperable, it cannot learn effectively.

Maya: So the real bottleneck in the robotics revolution is... the data plumbing?

Ryan: Exactly. And that is one of the most sobering lessons of 2026: AI technology is moving fast, but it keeps running into infrastructure that was never designed with it in mind. Forty-eight billion dollars raised, and the main obstacle is still system interoperability. That is genuinely humbling.

Maya: Alright, let's talk about something that is close to my heart: creativity. Because there was a lot of material this week on the relationship between AI and human creators. And honestly, the picture is a lot more nuanced than you might think.

Ryan: I would even say more optimistic than the dominant narrative. A report out this week estimates that AI multimedia generation tools — what people are calling Gen Media AI — could create 920 billion dollars in annual economic value globally. That is a staggering number.

Maya: 920 billion. That is basically a trillion dollars.

Ryan: And what is really interesting is the mindset of creators themselves. In that same study, 55 percent of respondents see AI as an amplifier of their creativity rather than a threat or a replacement.

Maya: So more than half of creators see AI as a tool that makes them better, not as something that is coming for their jobs.

Ryan: And the report draws a really interesting historical parallel: this is exactly what happened with Photoshop in the nineties, with nonlinear video editing. Tools that initially scared professionals, and ultimately expanded their creative capabilities in ways nobody anticipated.

Maya: And speaking of expanding creative capabilities, there is this project out of UCL in London that really caught my attention. The Centre for Creative Intelligence?

Ryan: Yes! University College London is developing an approach to multimodal generative AI that goes way beyond the text prompt. The idea is that the AI understands creative intent expressed through sketches, gestures, movements, visual references — not just written instructions.

Maya: So I could draw something rough and messy and the AI would understand what I am actually trying to create?

Ryan: Exactly. And that connects directly to what Synthesia is doing — a company co-founded by Professor Lourdes Agapito, who is part of that same center, now valued at 4 billion dollars after a 200 million dollar raise in January 2026. They create professional videos with photorealistic avatars, and their whole interface relies on exactly that kind of multimodal understanding of creative intent.

Maya: And in the middle of all this, there is also a voice out there saying: hey, even with AI everywhere, do not stop making beautiful things yourself.

Ryan: Yes, and this was a cultural commentary piece I found really well-observed. The argument is that in the face of this flood of AI-generated content in 2026, maintaining intentional human creation becomes more valuable than ever, not less. It is not anti-AI — it is the idea that the two can coexist, and that deliberate human creativity holds a kind of value that sheer volume simply cannot dilute.

Maya: There is something almost quietly rebellious about it — keep making beautiful things just for the sake of making them.

Ryan: And honestly, there is something really comforting in that idea.

Maya: Okay, we have made it to my favorite part. The what-if. Ryan, where are you taking us today?

Ryan: So the story of Matteo Paz and his astronomy AI got me spinning on a question that I have not been able to shake all week. What if the next wave of major scientific discoveries does not come primarily from institutions, but from thousands of individuals armed with autonomous AI tools?

Maya: A distributed science, kind of like how open source distributed software development?

Ryan: That is exactly the parallel. But there is another layer that this week's news brings up, and it pumps the brakes on the excitement a little. A study from the Santa Fe Institute reveals that scientists themselves do not actually agree on what science is — and the disagreements among scientists are larger than the disagreements between scientists and the general public.

Maya: Wait. Scientists do not agree on what science is? That is almost dizzying.

Ryan: And it raises a very serious concern: if we are building AI agents to conduct autonomous scientific discovery, and those agents are calibrated on one particular vision of what the scientific method is, we risk making other equally valid approaches invisible.

Maya: So AI could create a methodological bias at a global scale without anyone consciously deciding to do that?

Ryan: Right. It is not malice — it is a side effect of unintentional standardization. And this is exactly the kind of question we need to be asking now, while we are still building these systems, not after the fact. What are we choosing to optimize for? And based on whose definition of scientific truth?

Maya: That is the kind of question that does not have a clean answer, and that is precisely why it matters. Ryan, thank you.
