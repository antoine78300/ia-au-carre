Maya: Good morning and welcome to AI Square! It's Saturday, the sun is out, the coffee is hot, and we decided to talk artificial intelligence anyway — because honestly, this week was way too good to wait until Monday. Ryan is here with me, also in full weekend mode, and we are very happy to break it all down for you.

Ryan: Hey Maya, hey everyone! Yeah, relaxed mode is definitely on, but the brain is still running at full speed because this week was genuinely packed. We've got music, robots, voice tech, science... it's a solid lineup.

Maya: Alright, let's just dive right in. The big story of the week touches on music, and I'll be honest, it kind of blew my mind. Sony, Universal, Warner — the three absolute giants of the global music industry — have put 76 million dollars into Stability AI. These are the same labels that, just two years ago, were taking AI companies to court. And now they're cutting checks. Ryan, what exactly is going on here?

Ryan: It's a pretty stunning reversal, you're right. For a long time, the major labels treated AI like an existential threat. They saw their catalogs being scraped without permission, their artists being imitated, their revenue potentially gutted. And now they've done a complete one-eighty. Instead of fighting from the outside, they're buying in. They want a seat at the table where the decisions get made.

Maya: But why Stability AI specifically? Because that's a company that has had a pretty rough few years.

Ryan: Totally fair point. Stability AI has been through a lot of turbulence — founder departures, governance questions. But they're still a serious player on the technical side, especially around audio and music generation. And more than anything, the labels are watching what's happening across the space. Suno, for example, now has two million paying subscribers and around 300 million dollars in annual recurring revenue. That is massive for a music generation tool. ElevenLabs, on the voice side, is clearing 500 million dollars in annual revenue. Those numbers get people's attention real fast over at Universal, Sony, and Warner.

Maya: So basically their thinking is, if this train is leaving the station no matter what, you might as well be on it?

Ryan: And ideally be the one driving it. And there's something really interesting happening with Udio, another music generation tool, which is working on a joint platform with Universal Music. We've gone from an adversarial relationship to a co-creation relationship. The question is no longer "is AI going to kill music" — it's "how do we monetize this together."

Maya: Which does raise a pretty real question about where artists fit into all of this. Because we have another story connected to this, and it's a bit more complicated. Saudi musicians debating their place in the AI era. And Spotify mass-removing AI-generated tracks because there are just too many of them.

Ryan: Yeah, and that's the fascinating flip side of this whole thing. On one hand you have billions of dollars flowing into AI music generation, and on the other you have a platform like Spotify that is literally drowning in auto-generated content to the point where they have to start cleaning house. And caught in the middle of all that, you have actual artists — whether they're in Saudi Arabia or anywhere else — asking themselves a completely legitimate question: what is my value here? What is it that stays genuinely human about what I create?

Maya: And there's no easy answer to that.

Ryan: No, and that's exactly why this debate is going to stick around for a long time. The copyright question is wide open, and so is the question of artistic identity. If an AI generates a song based on a specific artist's style, does that artist have some ownership over it? Did something get taken from them? These legal and philosophical questions are nowhere near being resolved.

Maya: Alright, let's move into the experimentation segment, and I have something to share that personally has me very curious. There's a tool called Voicebox that lets you clone voices locally, with no cloud involved. Ryan, can you break down why that actually matters?

Ryan: Sure. So voice cloning has been around for a while. ElevenLabs does it really well. But with all of those services, you're sending your audio data to remote servers. Someone out there is storing your voice, analyzing it, processing it. And that raises pretty obvious privacy concerns. Voicebox, as covered by Korben, lets you do all of that directly on your own machine, without your data ever leaving your computer.

Maya: That's a huge difference for a lot of use cases.

Ryan: Absolutely. Think about healthcare professionals who want to create personalized voice messages for patients. Companies handling sensitive information. Journalists working on delicate stories. Or honestly just regular people who don't want their voice archived somewhere in a data center they've never heard of in a location they don't know. Having sovereignty over your own biometric data — and your voice absolutely is biometric data — that is a real and meaningful issue.

Maya: And it opens up creative possibilities too, right?

Ryan: For sure. Content creators, podcasters actually, indie game developers or tabletop roleplayers who want to build out voice characters without a studio budget. The fact that it runs locally also makes it accessible even with a limited internet connection. That's genuine democratization of the technology.

Maya: Another experimentation story from this week, and this one comes from the world of science — I think it's really beautiful. Researchers have developed a system where microscopes and AI collaborate in real time. What does that actually look like in practice?

Ryan: So picture this: you're a biologist looking at cells. Normally you spend hours watching, capturing images, analyzing them, adjusting your microscope, starting over. It's slow, it's tedious, and there's a lot of dead time where nothing is really happening. This new system these researchers built puts AI in the loop continuously. The microscope captures, the AI analyzes immediately, and it can suggest or even automatically adjust the microscope's parameters to get a better look at whatever was just detected.

Maya: So it's like a real-time collaboration between the instrument and the artificial intelligence.

Ryan: Exactly. And what's really elegant about this approach is that it erases the gap between the scientist and the data. Instead of a workflow where you collect, then analyze, then interpret, then go back to the instrument — everything happens in a continuous stream. That can dramatically speed up biological discoveries, especially in fields like oncology or microbiology where catching the right details at the right moment is absolutely critical.

Maya: It's almost like the microscope itself becomes intelligent.

Ryan: Right, and this is a broader trend in scientific instrumentation. AI doesn't replace the scientist — it upgrades the instrument. The next generation of labs is probably going to look a lot like this: equipment that thinks in part and has an ongoing dialogue with researchers in real time.

Maya: Let's keep going with the society and science segment, and we have two big ones here. First up: China and humanoid robots. The numbers are pretty wild.

Ryan: Wild is the right word. In 2025, Chinese companies had deployed around 1,000 humanoid robots in industrial settings. In 2026, the target is 10,000. A tenfold increase in one year. And this isn't just a race to automate factories. There's a data strategy behind it.

Maya: Walk us through that logic, because it's not immediately obvious.

Ryan: The number one problem in developing intelligent humanoid robots right now is data. Teaching a robot to navigate a real environment, to handle objects, to adapt to unexpected situations — that requires enormous amounts of real-world motion data. We have simulations, sure. But data collected in actual factories, with real physical constraints, real mistakes and corrections — that is irreplaceable. By deploying robots at massive scale in manufacturing plants, China is generating that data at a level nobody else can match.

Maya: So it's a long-term strategic play dressed up as industrial automation.

Ryan: Exactly. And it's brilliant in how it's designed. You're solving two problems at once. You're automating your industrial production, which has immediate economic value. And you're building the data infrastructure that will let you dominate robotic AI five or ten years from now. We also saw Isaac 0.5 drop this week — that's the model from Perceptron AI, released as open-weight, 36 billion parameters, capable of understanding video, reasoning about physical environments, and controlling robots. That's a genuine technical step forward in this space.

Maya: And for professions outside of manufacturing, there was also a story this week about AI and tax returns. Seventy percent of accountants are worried about security vulnerabilities. Are they right to be worried?

Ryan: It's a legitimate concern, but it needs some nuance. AI can genuinely add value in tax preparation — it's great at checking data consistency, flagging discrepancies, automating repetitive data entry and sorting tasks. But a tax return contains some of the most sensitive information that exists about a person or a business. Income, assets, transactions, liabilities. Handing all of that over to a system whose inner workings and security you don't fully control is a real risk.

Maya: So the human accountant still has a future?

Ryan: For judgment calls, interpreting edge cases, legal liability, and client trust — absolutely yes. What changes is the nature of the work. Less manual data entry, more intelligent review and strategic advising. It's a repositioning, not an extinction.

Maya: Alright, we've made it to our favorite part of the show — the What If. And this week I want to connect two things we've been talking about: music and robots. Because I've had a question rattling around in my head since I read these stories.

Ryan: Let's hear it.

Maya: What if ten years from now, the most-listened-to creators are neither humans nor AIs working alone, but an ongoing, inseparable collaboration between the two? Not "a human using an AI tool," but a genuinely mixed creative entity where you can no longer say who came up with which idea or produced which sound?

Ryan: That question genuinely fascinates me. And I think we're getting there faster than most people realize. Even today, with tools like Suno and others, some artists aren't producing a song anymore — they're iterating with an AI across hundreds of variations to find the right direction. At what point does the AI become a co-author? The legal system is nowhere near ready for that.

Maya: And that connects directly to the major labels investing in Stability AI. If tomorrow a massive hit comes out of a human-AI collaboration, who collects the royalties?

Ryan: That's exactly where those 76 million dollars from Sony, Universal, and Warner start to make complete sense. They're not just funding a technology — they're buying a position in defining the rules of tomorrow. Whoever is sitting at the table when the legal and economic standards for hybrid creativity get set will have the most influence over how it all shakes out.

Maya: And meanwhile, thousands of independent artists — Saudi musicians, Korean musicians, French musicians — don't have a seat at that table.

Ryan: That's the real democratic risk here. The technology is getting democratized, the tools are becoming available to everyone. But the rules of the game risk being written by a handful of very powerful players. And maybe that's where the real societal debate needs to happen. Not "is AI going to make music" but "who gets to decide how that music is valued, protected, and shared."

Maya: Now that is a great question to sit with for the rest of your Saturday. Ryan, thank you — this was a fantastic conversation this morning.

Ryan: Always a pleasure. These conversations never get old.

Maya: And thank you all so much for listening to AI Square all the way to the end. I hope we gave you some things to chew on with that second cup of coffee. We will be back with a brand new episode tomorrow, because yeah, we never really stop around here. See you tomorrow!
