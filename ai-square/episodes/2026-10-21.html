Maya: What if the watchdogs of finance started using the same weapons as the institutions they oversee? And what if a Chinese autonomous AI tool hit dozens of companies across Asia with zero human hands on the keyboard? Welcome to AI Squared, the podcast breaking down the artificial intelligence reshaping our world, every single day. It is Wednesday, October 21st, 2026, and there is a lot to unpack tonight.

Ryan: Hey Maya. And yeah, today we are getting into something pretty fundamental: the question of who actually controls AI agents, and what happens when nobody is controlling theirs.

Maya: We are jumping right in. Our big story today comes out of London. The Financial Conduct Authority, the FCA, which is the UK's top financial regulator, just announced that it wants to become what it's calling an agentic regulator. Ryan, what does that actually mean in practice?

Ryan: This is a pretty landmark declaration in the world of financial regulation. What the FCA is essentially saying is: the markets we oversee are increasingly using autonomous AI agents to make decisions. So to supervise them effectively, we are going to adopt those same agentic AI technologies ourselves to sharpen our oversight capabilities. That is a major paradigm shift.

Maya: So the regulator wants to move as fast as the players it's regulating.

Ryan: Exactly. And what is genuinely new here is that this is the first time a regulatory authority of this size and stature has made a formal public declaration of an agentic self-transformation strategy. We are not talking about using a chatbot to handle emails. We are talking about embedding autonomous AI agents into the core of how markets get supervised.

Maya: And that opens up a dizzying question: who supervises the supervisor's AI agents?

Ryan: That is exactly the right question to be asking. And the timing of this FCA announcement is striking, because it landed right alongside another story that illustrates precisely why overseeing AI agents has become a genuine emergency. I am talking about Artex.

Maya: Right, let's get into that. CrowdStrike just released new details on this tool. What exactly is Artex?

Ryan: Artex is an autonomous AI-powered pentesting tool of Chinese origin, built on a large language model. Now for listeners who are not in the security world, pentesting, short for penetration testing, is normally something security teams do to find vulnerabilities in their own systems. But Artex is being used offensively. CrowdStrike first documented attacks in South Korea back in September and October, targeting the financial sector. And now that same tool has hit 80 Japanese companies.

Maya: Eighty companies. And all of it operating autonomously, no human pulling the trigger on each individual attack.

Ryan: And that is what makes this particularly alarming. The agent identifies targets, hunts for vulnerabilities, exploits them, methodically and at scale. And we are seeing a confirmed geographic expansion: South Korea first, then Japan, consistently going after regulated financial sector targets. This is real-world proof that offensive AI agents can be deployed at scale in an organized way.

Maya: And that connects directly to what Microsoft just announced with its Guardian Agents, which we will get to in our industry segment. But first, let us talk frameworks. AWS just published something interesting about how agents discover their tools.

Ryan: Yes, AWS Agent Registry, and this is a meaningful technical step forward. Up until now, when you deployed an AI agent, you pre-configured it with a fixed list of available tools, things like MCP servers for those familiar with the space. That is a static setup. What AWS is now offering is dynamic discovery at runtime.

Maya: Break that down for someone building agents day to day. What does that actually change?

Ryan: It means the agent receives a request, and in real time it checks a registry to see what tools are available at that exact moment. It can spin up what they call ephemeral agents, assembled on the fly based on what the task needs. And the system automatically handles multi-protocol chains, both MCP and A2A, without the developer having to anticipate every possible configuration upfront. It is a shift from static assembly to dynamic orchestration.

Maya: So agents get more adaptive, but potentially also less predictable.

Ryan: That is the constant tension. And on the frameworks front, we also need to talk about Security Onion 3.3.0, which just dropped its major release. It is a SIEM platform, a security analysis tool used by cybersecurity teams.

Maya: What is new in this version?

Ryan: What stands out is that Security Onion now has autonomous AI agents built directly into the platform, through what they are calling Agent Studio. Security teams can build their own custom agents with persistent memory and multi-site orchestration. And critically, none of it depends on external APIs. Everything runs natively, on-device. This is the first time a mainstream SIEM platform has offered that out of the box. The underlying model is Gemma 4 31B.

Maya: And there is also Databricks pushing a new memory system for agents, called MemCurrent.

Ryan: Right. And MemCurrent is solving a very concrete, real-world problem. When an AI agent is working on a long task, every new request often forces it to reload the entire context from scratch. That is slow and expensive. MemCurrent is a persistent memory architecture that keeps the agent's state between requests, built for long-running workflows.

Maya: Like the agent can finally remember what it was doing yesterday.

Ryan: That is a great way to put it. And it is already live inside Genie, Databricks' conversational data interface for enterprise. These are the technical building blocks that make agents genuinely usable in production on complex, multi-step tasks.

Maya: Let us move into our research segment. We have two stories today that are pulling in pretty different directions. The first one is about OpenAI and legacy programming languages.

Ryan: OpenAI is launching GPT-6 Astra, and it is a specialized coding model, but not focused on Python or JavaScript where there is already strong coverage. This one targets legacy languages: COBOL, Fortran, Visual Basic. These languages are running a huge chunk of the world's critical infrastructure, banks, healthcare systems, government agencies. But there is far less training data available for them.

Maya: And that is a very real, very practical problem. There are billions of lines of COBOL keeping banking systems alive right now, and hardly anyone left who knows how to read them.

Ryan: Exactly. GPT-6 Astra is a direct response to that reality. The goal is to extend AI capabilities into these legacy systems that underpin essential services around the world. It also continues a pattern we are seeing more and more: specialized foundation models rather than one single model trying to do everything.

Maya: And speaking of specialization, the second research story today is one that genuinely blew my mind. Researchers at Mass General Brigham predicting cancer survival from a photo of someone's face.

Ryan: This is the FAHR model, developed by researchers at Mass General Brigham. It was trained on millions of facial images. And it has two components: FAHR-FaceAge, which estimates a person's biological age, meaning not their calendar age but the actual age of their cells, with an accuracy of plus or minus five years. And then FAHR-FaceSurvival, which uses that biological age estimate to predict survival risk for cancer patients.

Maya: Hold on. We are talking about just a regular photo?

Ryan: Just a photo. No blood work, no biopsy. One facial image. The authors say this is the first demonstration that an AI model can predict cancer survival directly from a photograph. The underlying idea is that the face reflects biological markers of cellular aging that are genuine prognostic indicators.

Maya: That is equal parts fascinating and ethically enormous.

Ryan: Absolutely. And it is a perfect example of how specialized biological foundation models are opening up possibilities that nobody was even imagining a few years ago. Along those lines, Sinopia Biosciences just received a two and a half million dollar grant from the NIH to develop a specialized AI foundation model in metabolomics for drug discovery. That is the first major federal validation of this type of model for pharmaceutical discovery.

Maya: Let us head into our industry impact segment. And the theme today is pretty clearly governance and control of AI agents in the enterprise. Microsoft made a big announcement with its Guardian Agents.

Ryan: The framing of this announcement is striking. Microsoft is introducing Guardian Agents, a monitoring system for AI agents deployed inside companies. And the comparison they draw is very direct: they are saying the risks posed by autonomous AI agents are comparable to insider threats, the kind of risk you would associate with a malicious employee inside your organization.

Maya: Satya Nadella himself said that frontier models should no longer be automatically trusted. That is a pretty bold statement from the CEO of Microsoft.

Ryan: Extremely bold. And the concrete recommendation that comes with it is that companies need to draw a clear line between two things: intelligence on one side, meaning what the AI model knows and can analyze, and decision authority on the other, meaning what the AI agent is actually allowed to do. Being smart does not automatically mean being allowed to act without oversight.

Maya: And that question of agent control also shows up in Omnissa's Elara suite.

Ryan: Yes, and this takes a very specific angle. Elara includes AI agents dedicated to detecting shadow AI, meaning AI applications being used inside a company without going through official security policies. So you have AI agents monitoring other unauthorized AI agents. It is a bit of a hall of mirrors, and it illustrates just how layered and complex agentic governance inside enterprises has become.

Maya: And to round out the business picture, we cannot skip over the doubleAI fundraise: 200 million dollars with Nvidia and Dell coming in as investors.

Ryan: And what is driving that valuation is their Maxwell model, which just set a world record in chess at 63 moves deep. The previous record was 60. That is not a trivia footnote. Chess is a classic benchmark for evaluating multi-step reasoning. A model that can calculate 63 moves ahead is demonstrating deep planning capabilities, which is exactly what you want in complex autonomous AI agents. Nvidia and Dell are not putting 200 million dollars behind a chess demo. They are betting on what that reasoning capacity means for agents running in real production environments.

Maya: And that really does bring everything full circle for today's show. We have AI agents attacking, agents defending, agents watching other agents, and regulators who want to become agents themselves. Ryan, if you had to sum up the central thread of today in one sentence, what would it be?

Ryan: The central question is no longer whether AI agents can act autonomously. They already are, offensively with Artex, defensively with Guardian Agents, at the regulatory level with the FCA. The urgent question now is: who decides what these agents are allowed to do, and how much human oversight is actually in the loop?

Maya: A question that does not have a universal answer yet, and that is exactly why it matters that you tune in every day. Ryan, thanks so much for walking us through all of this.

Ryan: Thanks Maya.

Maya: And thank you for sticking with us through the whole show. AI Squared is here every day to help you make sense of a news cycle that never slows down. We will see you tomorrow for another deep dive into the AI that is changing everything. Talk to you then.
