Emma: Hey everyone, welcome to AI Squared, the podcast that breaks down artificial intelligence every single day of the week, weekends included! I'm Emma, and it's your weekend team here this morning bringing you a more laid-back, curious, and honestly pretty fun edition of the show. So get comfortable, because we've got some really exciting stuff to share with you today.

Liam: And I'm Liam, and I can absolutely back that up. Laid-back and curious is exactly the right vibe, because this week AI has been serving up some genuinely fascinating stories that we couldn't wait to get into with you.

Emma: So where do we even start, Liam? Because I feel like we're spoiled for choice this morning.

Liam: We start in the music world, and fair warning, this one is stirring up some serious noise right now over in the UK.

Emma: Oh yeah, I saw this come across my feed! There was a big conference?

Liam: Exactly. It's called Beyond The Music 2026, a major gathering of UK music industry leaders all wrestling with one burning question: how should the music industry position itself in the age of generative AI? And the takeaways are pretty clear: regulation, accountability, and guardrails.

Emma: Regulation, sure, we hear that all the time. But what really caught my attention when you walked me through this earlier was this concept of "Fair GPT." What is that exactly?

Liam: So this is honestly the most original idea that came out of the whole event. A guy named Clive Dickens, who runs a company called Meliora, came up with something simple but pretty clever. He wants to redefine what GPT actually stands for. Right now everyone associates GPT with large language models, that's just the cultural shorthand at this point. But he's proposing that GPT should mean "Fair GPT," as in models that are fairly governed, fairly sourced, and fairly trained.

Emma: Hold on, fairly trained, what does that actually mean in practice? Like, artists whose work was used to train AI models would get... paid?

Liam: That's exactly it. The idea is to build a framework where if your music catalog helped feed an AI model, you have a say in how it's used, and potentially a right to compensation. It's still a proposal, still a principle at this stage, but it really captures the direction the UK music industry wants to push things.

Emma: There's almost something ironic about it when you think about it. AI fed off human creativity, and now humans have to negotiate to get back a piece of what they unknowingly gave away.

Liam: That's a pretty accurate way to put it, yeah. And within that same debate, you've got two very different personalities who kind of illustrate just how divided the industry is right now. On one side you've got Gene Simmons, the legendary bassist from Kiss, taking a very pragmatic stance, basically telling musicians: get on top of generative AI, because if you don't, it will get on top of you.

Emma: Which is a little rich coming from someone who spent forty years painting his face to go on stage.

Liam: And then on the other side you've got Boy George, who has already fully leaned in and confirmed he's written five albums using AI as a lyrical collaborator. Five albums. That's not experimenting anymore, that's just how he works now.

Emma: And that actually leads right into a study we wanted to talk about, because it adds a really interesting wrinkle to all of this.

Liam: Yes, this comes from cognitive science researchers and was published on Singularity Hub. Their conclusion is that while AI can generate ideas fast, human teams are actually more creative.

Emma: Okay that feels counterintuitive, right? You'd expect it to be the other way around.

Liam: And that's what makes it so fascinating. AI is extremely efficient at producing large volumes of ideas quickly, but there's a structural problem underneath that: when everyone is using the same AI tools, the outputs start to look the same. The researchers call it style convergence. Everyone ends up pulling in the same creative direction without even realizing it.

Emma: So if everyone asks ChatGPT or some competing tool to write melancholic pop lyrics for 2026, we're all going to end up with... the same melancholic pop song for 2026.

Liam: Exactly. And that is precisely where human collaboration holds its edge. Teams bring together different personalities, different reference points, disagreements, contradictory emotions. That friction is what produces something genuinely original. AI, by contrast, tends to smooth everything out.

Emma: That's reassuring and a little unsettling at the same time. Reassuring for human creatives, unsettling because a lot of people don't realize this yet and are just going to hand everything over to AI anyway.

Liam: And that's exactly the core tension that Beyond The Music is trying to address over in the UK. How do you preserve creative diversity in a world where everyone has access to the same toolkit?

Emma: Alright, total subject change now, because up next we've got robotics and science, and there are some genuinely jaw-dropping things happening here too.

Liam: We're stepping into the world of embodied AI. That's the idea of integrating artificial intelligence directly into physical machines, robots, mechanical arms, industrial systems, things that actually exist in the real world and move around in it.

Emma: And we wanted to hit two things here. First, what's going on in Taiwan?

Liam: Right, so at COMPUTEX 2026, a major tech expo, Judy Chang from a company called Techman Robot laid out something pretty remarkable. They've managed to integrate computer vision, the ability to see and analyze the environment, directly into heavy industrial machines. And what that means is those machines can now predict their own failures before any breakdown actually occurs.

Emma: Wait, the machine knows it's about to break down before it breaks down?

Liam: Exactly. This isn't reactive maintenance anymore, this is AI-driven predictive maintenance happening in real time. And Taiwan is really positioning this as its competitive advantage: processing data locally inside the machine, while staying connected to the cloud for more complex decisions.

Emma: And Google also made an announcement in this space?

Liam: They did, and the architecture here is really interesting. Google launched what they're calling Gemini Robotics Embodied Reasoning 2, or ER2 for short. It's a two-level architecture. On one side, a large, powerful model in the cloud handling spatial reasoning, planning, and complex decisions. On the other side, a lightweight model running directly on the robot that handles real-time action execution.

Emma: So the cloud is basically the brain doing the thinking, and the local robot is the hands doing the doing.

Liam: That's a great way to put it. And startups are already building on top of this platform to develop new robotic applications. It's starting to become foundational infrastructure, kind of the way cloud computing became the backbone of web apps.

Emma: And speaking of real-world economic impact, there's something interesting happening in China around all of these new robotics technologies.

Liam: Yeah, and this is an angle that tends to get lost in the AI-and-jobs conversation. In China, the growth of embodied AI isn't just eliminating jobs, it's creating new ones. Roles that didn't exist five years ago are emerging right now: robotic arm trainers, embodied AI operators.

Emma: Robotic arm trainers... that's an actual job title now.

Liam: It's a real job. These are people whose role is to teach robots how to perform precise movements, how to adapt to changing environments. And this isn't lab R&D anymore, these are mainstream positions in factories and warehouses.

Emma: That's fascinating because it flips the dominant narrative a little bit. We spend so much time talking about AI taking jobs, and here we have AI generating new ones, even if they look very different from anything we had before.

Liam: And the question that always hangs over that is: is the rate of job creation fast enough to keep pace with the rate of destruction? We don't have a clear answer to that yet, but the fact that these roles exist and are becoming structured is already a meaningful signal.

Emma: Okay, before we wrap up, we've got a science story that is absolutely wild. Insilico Medicine?

Liam: This is my favorite story of the week, no question. Insilico Medicine is a company specializing in AI-driven drug discovery. And they just announced a truly stunning record: eleven preclinical drug candidates discovered by AI between January and October 2026. Eleven in ten months.

Emma: For those of us who aren't scientists, what exactly is a preclinical candidate?

Liam: It's a molecule that's been identified as potentially therapeutic and is ready to move into the testing phase, before human clinical trials begin. Traditionally, finding just one candidate takes years and costs tens of millions of dollars. Here, AI is identifying eleven of them in under a year.

Emma: And since 2021 they're up to thirty-five total.

Liam: Exactly. And what's really interesting about their approach is that their edge doesn't come from having a uniquely powerful AI model. It comes from integrating the entire pipeline: identifying the therapeutic target, generating molecules, automated experimental validation, all of it inside one closed loop. The orchestration is what makes the difference, not raw computing power.

Emma: So it's less about which tool you're using and more about how you connect all the tools together.

Liam: That's a lesson that would apply across a whole lot of industries, honestly.

Emma: So here's the big-picture question that's been turning over in my head throughout this whole conversation. Are we living through a fork in the road? On one side, an AI that homogenizes, that smooths things out, that converges, like we saw with creativity. And on the other, an AI that opens up possibilities we never had before, discovering new drugs, creating entirely new kinds of jobs.

Liam: And maybe the real question is whether we still have the ability to choose which path we go down. The Fair GPT concept coming out of the music industry is exactly that: an attempt to put guardrails in place, to steer AI development toward something more equitable rather than just letting it happen to us.

Emma: And maybe that's the defining challenge of our moment with AI. Not understanding how it works under the hood, but deciding collectively what we want it to become.

Liam: And for that, you need people who understand the subject well enough to actually show up to that conversation. Which I think brings us right back to why this podcast exists in the first place.

Emma: Exactly! Liam, thank you, and thank you all for spending your morning with us. We genuinely hope this was as fun and thought-provoking to listen to as it was to put together. Take care of yourselves, enjoy the rest of your day, and we'll see you tomorrow for another episode of AI Squared!

Liam: See you tomorrow everyone!
