Maya: Hey everyone, welcome to AI Square, the podcast that breaks down artificial intelligence for you every single day. I'm Maya, and we have a packed show for you today. Researchers are sounding the alarm on existential AI risk, a French company is stepping up with its own agentic framework, and a question that has the entire global financial industry buzzing. Buckle up, let's dive in.

Maya: Ryan, good to have you. We are kicking things off with what I'd call the biggest story in the news cycle right now, and it goes right to the core of AI safety. Jacob Coxon, a 27-year-old researcher who worked at OpenAI and then Anthropic, has just gone public with a serious warning. What exactly is he saying?

Ryan: Hey Maya. So Jacob Coxon is a fascinating and kind of unsettling case, because this is someone who had a front-row seat at the two most influential AI labs on the planet. And what he's saying is pretty chilling. He's directly accusing both OpenAI and Anthropic of downplaying, publicly, the risks their own systems pose to humanity. His phrase has been all over social media. He says they're quote playing with our lives. And this isn't some outside critic lobbing stones from a distance. This is someone who was inside the kitchen and saw how the food gets made.

Maya: And from what I understand, he's not alone here, because other researchers at both labs are speaking out right now too?

Ryan: Exactly, and that's what makes this feel like a genuine inflection point. CNBC is reporting that multiple researchers who are still employed at OpenAI and Anthropic are ramping up their calls to slow down AI development. The thing that really crystallized the tension was the demo last April of a model called Mythos, built by Anthropic. It has advanced cyber capabilities, and that presentation sent shockwaves through global financial institutions. Banks, insurers, investment funds all kind of woke up and said, wait a minute, if a system like this can operate autonomously inside digital environments, what are our guardrails?

Maya: So are these whistleblowers actually moving the needle, or are they just getting ignored?

Ryan: That's the real question, isn't it. Right now the impact is mostly in the media and public discourse. These statements are feeding conversations in regulatory circles, especially in Europe. But the labs are still shipping. What feels different this time is that the critical mass of dissenting voices coming from inside these organizations keeps growing. We've moved from what used to be isolated, individual cases to something that's starting to look like an organized movement. Whether that's enough to actually change the trajectory of development, honestly, nobody can say with any certainty right now.

Maya: We are going to come back to that safety debate in the second half of the show. But first, let's talk tools and frameworks, because there is some very concrete news this morning out of France. Sidetrade is launching a framework called SAFE. Ryan, who is Sidetrade and why does this matter?

Ryan: So Sidetrade is a French company listed on Euronext that operates in treasury cycle management for large enterprises, what the industry calls Order-to-Cash. And SAFE, which stands for Sidetrade Agentic Framework for Enterprise, is their answer to a frustration a lot of companies are feeling right now. They put out a stat that really makes you stop and think: 88 percent of organizations have deployed AI in some form, but 81 percent of them haven't seen a meaningful financial return yet. That's the classic gap between running experiments and actually delivering real-world value.

Maya: And what does the framework actually do in practice?

Ryan: It lets companies deploy AI agents directly inside their financial workflows, and what they're calling a sovereign approach, meaning the data stays under the company's control, decisions are fully traceable, and governance is baked right into the platform. Their flagship agent is called Aimie, and it specializes in accounts receivable, what they call Cash Collection. And this agent is already running in production for clients like Securitas, Accor, and Sodexo. We're not talking about lab tests or pilot programs, this is real deployment at scale.

Maya: And they have some pretty specific financial targets attached to this?

Ryan: Very specific. Sidetrade is targeting an EBITDA margin between 30 and 35 percent by 2030, and SAFE is one of the core pillars of that plan. They're expecting to expand the framework's capabilities in Q4 of 2026. What's really striking here is that this is a French company that isn't just plugging in AI components built somewhere else. They're building their own agentic stack, with their own governance logic baked in from day one. In a world where European digital sovereignty has become a major policy issue, this is definitely a story worth watching closely.

Maya: And speaking of governing AI agents, there's another big development today in the financial sector. Ant International, Visa, and Mastercard are working together on something called Know Your Agent. Ryan, what's the idea behind this?

Ryan: So the idea starts from a simple observation that has absolutely massive implications. In the coming years, a growing share of online purchases won't be made by a human clicking a button. They'll be made by an AI agent acting on behalf of that human. The projections suggest that one in ten users will be delegating their purchases to an AI agent within four years. And that's where traditional payment systems hit a wall. They were designed to authenticate individual people. Suddenly they're facing a question they were never built to answer: how do I know that it's actually the authorized agent of a real customer making this purchase, and not a malicious agent impersonating them?

Maya: And the CEO of the State Bank of India, the country's largest bank, took a really strong public stance on this?

Ryan: He did, and the way he put it is going to stick with you. C.S. Setty says 99 percent accuracy is not good enough. Think about it this way: if an AI agent is processing thousands of transactions a day, a one percent error rate means dozens of problematic transactions every single day. And in banking, one error can mean an unauthorized wire transfer, a fraud event, or a regulatory violation. His position is that you can't just take the logic of classic automation and paste it onto agents that are making autonomous decisions with people's money. He's calling for stronger safeguards, clear accountability, and maintained human oversight on critical decisions.

Maya: All right, let's pivot to the research side, and we're staying in the world of agentic AI risk, but this time through the lens of cybersecurity. Palo Alto Networks just published an analysis that flags something pretty alarming about AI coding agents.

Ryan: Yeah, and this is an area that a lot of companies are seriously underestimating right now. AI coding agents are those systems that help developers write code, debug, and automate technical tasks. They're everywhere now. But Palo Alto Networks is pointing to a fundamental problem: these agents can, in the normal course of doing their job, assume the identity of the user, modify system files, and execute arbitrary commands on the machines they run on.

Maya: Which means they can have a really significant level of access?

Ryan: Exactly. And traditional cybersecurity architectures were built to monitor human behavior at human speed. A person trying to access a thousand files in one minute, that triggers an alert. An AI agent doing the exact same thing as part of its legitimate task, that can fly completely under the radar. Palo Alto's recommendation is to think about agentic security natively, meaning you build the controls right into the platform itself. You monitor what gets installed, what gets executed, and critically, what data leaves the organization. The question is no longer just who has access to what. Now it's which agent is doing what, under whose identity, and with what actual authorization.

Maya: And Zoho also made an announcement in this agentic tools space, but with a pretty different angle?

Ryan: Right, Zoho is coming at this from the opposite direction. Instead of leading with security and constraints, they're focused on accessibility and adoption. Their platform, Zia Agent Studio, lets you build and deploy AI agents with complete transparency, meaning the company can actually see how the agent is reasoning, track token consumption, and understand why it made a particular decision. And to lower the financial barrier to entry, they're offering 30 million free tokens per month. It's an interesting play because it addresses a very real obstacle: a lot of small and mid-sized businesses want to experiment with AI agents but they don't have the budget or the in-house technical chops to build from scratch.

Maya: Okay, we're moving into our industry impact segment, and I want to take a step back and look at everything we've covered today. Ryan, if you had to pull out the defining theme of this news cycle, what is it?

Ryan: What really strikes me is that we are collectively waking up to the fact that moving from AI tools to AI agents isn't just a quantitative upgrade. It's a qualitative break. With an AI tool, you ask it a question, it gives you an answer, and you decide what to do. With an AI agent, it decides and it acts. And that capacity for autonomous action creates new responsibilities across three dimensions at the same time. Security, because these agents have access and capabilities that can be misused or misconfigured. Governance, because you need to know who is accountable for what an agent does, both legally and ethically. And systemic risk, because if these agents are operating at scale in critical sectors like finance, a failure can propagate very, very fast.

Maya: And the industry players you mentioned, Sidetrade, Visa, Mastercard, Ant International, do they seem to get that?

Ryan: Some of them do, and that's genuinely encouraging. The fact that Sidetrade is building sovereignty into its framework from the ground up, that payments giants are working on a Know Your Agent standard before problems actually hit, those are positive signals. What's less encouraging is the gap that whistleblowers like Jacob Coxon are describing: between how fast model capabilities are advancing and how fast the guardrails are being put in place, there's still a significant lag. Anthropic's Mythos model with its cyber capabilities is a concrete illustration of exactly what that looks like. Capabilities arriving fast, and institutions scrambling to figure out how to manage them.

Maya: Does Europe have a particular role to play in all of this?

Ryan: I genuinely think so, and it's not just flag-waving. Europe has the AI Act. It has a regulatory tradition that requires thinking about fundamental rights before deployment happens. The concept of digital sovereignty is taken seriously at the institutional level. And initiatives like Sidetrade's show that you can build industrially competitive solutions while integrating those constraints from day one. The risk is that if Europe regulates too slowly on the most critical issues, like autonomous agents in financial contexts, and too quickly on secondary ones, it misses the balance. But the conversations are happening. They're substantive. And that's already better than a vacuum.
