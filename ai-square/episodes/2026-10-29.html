Full transcript

Emma: Welcome to AI Squared, your daily dose of everything artificial intelligence. Quick heads-up before we dive in: Maya and Ryan, our usual voices, are dealing with a technical issue on Microsoft's end — they'll be back as soon as that's sorted out. In the meantime, Liam and I are holding down the fort! Today is Thursday, October 29th, 2026, and agentic AI is absolutely everywhere: a new Google model that can work for days on end, a security language framework from Cisco, and autonomous agents making their way into telecom networks and Middle Eastern banks. Let's get into it.

Emma: Liam, let's kick things off with the biggest story of the day, and it's coming from Google. What is this Gemini 4 Carbon that everyone's talking about?

Liam: This is genuinely the headline announcement of the last several hours. Google just unveiled Gemini 4 Carbon, their new frontier model — meaning the top of their lineup as of right now. What sets it apart is a score of 59 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index benchmark, version 5.0, which puts it at number one in the world according to data published by VentureBeat. But beyond the numbers, what's really new here is something Google is calling the long horizon.

Emma: The long horizon — what does that actually mean in practice?

Liam: Google is documenting that this model can work continuously on a single task for days at a time. The example they give is diagnosing bugs and developing fixes inside large codebases — we're talking massive software projects. This is not a system that answers a question and stops. This is an agent that works continuously, explores, iterates, sometimes over several days, without constant human intervention.

Emma: And the cybersecurity capabilities — is that a meaningful addition?

Liam: Google is calling those capabilities state of the art. The model is built to operate in environments where threat detection and resilience against attacks are critical. That actually lines up with something else we'll talk about in a few minutes with Cisco's project. One thing worth highlighting though: Gemini 4 Carbon is not openly available yet. Google is talking about a controlled expansion, with no public timeline defined. Compare that with Gemini Argon, which has been available via API since October 13th. Carbon, for now, is limited to a select group.

Emma: So we have the world's number one model... that we can't actually use yet. That's a little frustrating, right?

Liam: It's pretty much standard practice for the most powerful frontier models. Access gets rolled out gradually, partly for safety reasons, partly to calibrate the infrastructure. But it does confirm a trend: long-duration agentic capabilities are arriving in commercial models, not just research labs.

Emma: Let's move on to tools and frameworks, and there's a lot happening here too. We've got two major announcements: Cisco on one side, SECO on the other. Liam, let's start with Cisco, because Project Quarry is genuinely original in its approach.

Liam: Absolutely. Cisco has open-sourced a project called Project Quarry, and it's pretty unlike anything we've seen before. It's a rules language — a formal language designed specifically to evaluate the behavior of AI agents. It combines two approaches: on one side, machine learning-based judgments, so a form of statistical evaluation, and on the other side, exact deterministic rule-based checks.

Emma: And why does it matter to have both?

Liam: Because AI agents can be attacked in a huge variety of ways. Some attacks are subtle and context-dependent — they require the kind of semantic understanding that only machine learning can provide. Others are more direct and can be caught with precise logical rules. Quarry covers both cases, and it's designed to work across single-turn conversations, multi-turn conversations, interactions via the MCP protocol, and even multimodal inputs — text, images, audio.

Emma: And Cisco references YARA — can you explain that for listeners who might not be familiar?

Liam: YARA is a tool that became an industry standard in traditional cybersecurity for detecting malware. Cisco is explicitly saying that Project Quarry aims to play that same role for AI agent security — to become the go-to language, the de facto standard, for detecting dangerous behavior in agentic systems. That's a bold ambition, and open-sourcing it is very much part of that strategy: they want the community to adopt it and build on it.

Emma: And on the SECO side, we have Clea 2.0. That's a different kind of challenge?

Liam: Completely different angle. Here we're in deployment territory, not security. SECO is announcing version 2.0 of its Clea framework, which is designed to radically simplify the deployment of AI applications on edge devices — sensors, industrial equipment, connected objects sitting at the edge of a network. The core idea is one-click deployment across fleets of heterogeneous devices, meaning devices from different manufacturers with different architectures.

Emma: Heterogeneous — what does that look like in the real world?

Liam: In any real industrial deployment, you never have all the same hardware. You've got sensors from one vendor, gateways from another, embedded compute from a third. Clea 2.0 takes a hardware-agnostic approach and orchestrates all of it in a unified way. And to drive adoption, SECO is offering free early access through April 30th, 2027. It's a classic land-and-expand play — build the user base before monetizing.

Emma: Great. Now let's get into the research side, and here we have a document published by Endor Labs that touches on regulatory compliance. I know that can sound dry, but this one is genuinely concrete and practical.

Liam: It really is, and it's actually a formal first in this space. Endor Labs published what you could call a compliance mapping for what they refer to as the ADLC — the agentic development lifecycle. The concept is straightforward: take the identified risks for AI agents, and line them up against existing regulatory frameworks.

Emma: And which regulatory frameworks are we talking about?

Liam: Three main ones. First, the NIST SSDF, which is a US framework for secure software development. Second, the OWASP Top 10 Agentic 2026, which lists the ten primary risks in agentic systems, referenced as ASI01 through ASI10. And third, the European Union AI Act, in its version updated by the Digital Omnibus 2026, which came into force on July 27th, 2026.

Emma: And there's an important date for teams building AI systems in Europe?

Liam: There is. The specific obligations for so-called high-risk systems — those listed in Annex III of the Act — have been pushed back to December 2nd, 2027. That delay is a window of opportunity for companies, but it is not a reason to put the compliance work off. Quite the opposite — that's exactly why a mapping like Endor Labs' is useful right now: to start aligning agentic development practices with what regulation is going to require.

Emma: And why do they call it a first?

Liam: Because it's the first time we've seen a formal mapping that connects the specifically agentic development lifecycle to European regulation in its post-Digital Omnibus form. Before this, teams had to do that alignment work themselves, manually, on a case-by-case basis. Now there's a shared reference.

Emma: Perfect. Let's get to the industry impact section, and the picture here is striking. We've got telecom, banking, and a partnership between two enterprise software giants. Liam, where do we start?

Liam: Let's start with the most structurally significant partnership: ServiceNow and OpenAI. ServiceNow is one of the most widely used enterprise operations management platforms in the world. And they're announcing an expansion of their partnership with OpenAI that allows OpenAI agents to directly access ServiceNow workflows through what they're calling the ServiceNow Action Fabric.

Emma: What does that change in practice for a company?

Liam: It changes a lot. Until now, an AI agent could give you an answer, suggest an action. Now it can execute that action inside the enterprise system — inside real workflows, tickets, business processes. And ServiceNow is baking in governance capabilities to oversee that autonomous execution. The press release calls it supervised autonomous execution of business actions. The big takeaway here is the formalization of what people are starting to call agent-to-ERP orchestration: agents that directly drive enterprise management systems.

Emma: And on the telecom side, we've got an announcement out of the UAE.

Liam: Yes. The operator du, operating in the United Arab Emirates, is announcing with Comarch Communications the deployment of autonomous AI agents inside its network service operations — what's called the OSS, or Operations Support System. These are the systems that technically manage telecom networks in real time. Deploying this in commercial production with autonomous agents making goal-oriented decisions is a strong signal: we are past the pilot phase and into real-world operations.

Emma: And finally, a Middle Eastern bank using agentic AI for outbound calls?

Liam: Exactly. Al Masraf, a bank in the MENA region, is launching an outbound calling platform powered by a bilingual AI agent. The agent can qualify the banking needs of prospective customers, speed up response times, and free up human sales teams to focus exclusively on higher-value personalized advice. What's notable here is the bilingual dimension, tailored to the Middle Eastern context, and the fact that agentic AI is entering financial services for automated outbound sales in this region.

Emma: What's striking across all these examples is that agents aren't just advising anymore — they're acting. ServiceNow, du, Al Masraf: in all three cases, the agent is executing something in the real world.

Liam: That is exactly the shift that defines 2026. And that's why the security topics we covered with Cisco, and the compliance work from Endor Labs, are not theoretical exercises. When an agent can trigger an action inside a telecom network or qualify a customer for a financial product, the stakes become very real, very fast.

Emma: Liam, if you had to take away one thing from today, what would it be?

Liam: I'd say it's the convergence of power and responsibility. We have Gemini 4 Carbon pushing the limits of what a model can do on its own for days at a time. We have agents going live in production in critical industries. And at the same time, the ecosystem is seriously building out the tooling — security with Quarry, compliance with the Endor Labs mapping. It's not a coincidence that all of this is happening together: agentic maturity is calling for governance maturity to match.

Emma: Couldn't have said it better. Liam, thanks so much for the analysis. And thank you all for listening all the way to the end. AI Squared is your daily companion for keeping up with a world that just keeps accelerating. We'll see you tomorrow for another episode. Talk then!
