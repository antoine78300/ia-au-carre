Full transcript

Maya: Hey everyone, welcome to AI Squared, your daily dose of everything you need to know about artificial intelligence. Today is Tuesday, October 6th, 2026, and we have a packed show for you. We are talking about a massive 501-billion-parameter model that wants to push back against Chinese AI, a Google security agent hunting down vulnerabilities at serious scale, and the big trust problem companies are running into with AI agents in the workplace. Let's dive in.

Maya: Ryan, let's kick things off with the biggest story of the day, and honestly it's a pretty striking one. Reflection AI, a startup founded by former Google DeepMind researchers, just unveiled a model called Beam. And the numbers are kind of jaw-dropping: 501 billion parameters.

Ryan: Yeah Maya, this one is definitely worth unpacking. Reflection AI was founded in 2024, they raised 4.7 billion dollars at a 25-billion-dollar valuation. And Beam, their flagship model, is what we call an open-weight model, meaning the model weights will be publicly available so that anyone can host it themselves.

Maya: And the goal behind all of this, they are being pretty upfront about it?

Ryan: Very upfront. Misha Laskin, the CEO of Reflection AI, said it straight out: the strategy is to reduce the dependence of Western companies and governments on Chinese models, especially for self-hosted inference. Beam is clearly positioned as a sovereign alternative to models like GLM-5.2, which comes out of China.

Maya: So when can people actually get their hands on it?

Ryan: An early access version is available right now. The full weights are scheduled for the end of October 2026. So it is a two-phase rollout, with a gradual ramp-up to full availability.

Maya: What I also find really interesting here is the geopolitical backdrop it highlights. AI is clearly becoming a sovereignty issue, not just a technology race.

Ryan: Absolutely. And that actually connects to some governance news we are covering today as well. Singapore put forward an ISO/IEC standard back in April 2026 for testing generative AI systems. The White House published a federal legislative framework in March 2026, pushing for a coherent national policy rather than a patchwork of state-by-state laws. And at the UN General Assembly in 2026, developing nations made a strong case for a bigger seat at the table in global AI governance.

Maya: It almost feels like every player, public and private, is rushing to plant their flag at the same time.

Ryan: Exactly. And what is striking is how well Beam fits into that picture. A massive open-weight model designed specifically to be self-hosted is basically a direct, practical answer to the dependency concerns that all these governance frameworks are trying to address through regulation.

Maya: Alright, let's shift over to our tools and frameworks segment. And here we have a few stories that all orbit around the same big theme: how do we get better at monitoring and orchestrating AI agents.

Ryan: Right, and this is really becoming a central issue. We have two announcements that complement each other really well. The first one comes from Snowflake, which is announcing a new feature called Agent Observability, coming soon in private preview. It lets you trace agent interactions end to end, connect their behavior to costs and quality, and use what is called LLM-as-Judge, which means a separate language model that evaluates the quality of responses either in real time or after the fact.

Maya: So you can actually trace a problem all the way back to its root?

Ryan: Exactly. If an agent is misbehaving, you can trace the issue back to the original prompt, the retrieved context, or a bad tool selection. That is a level of debugging that we have not really had natively available before.

Maya: What is the second announcement?

Ryan: This one is even more strategic. Dynatrace, which is a major player in application monitoring, has acquired Arize, a platform that specializes in AI model evaluation. The goal is to bring agent tracing, evaluation, experimentation, and production monitoring all together in a single unified platform. It is a market consolidation move that confirms AI agent observability is now a critical need, not a nice-to-have.

Maya: We are also seeing some news around agent-to-agent coordination, with Work4Flow and LogicMonitor.

Ryan: Yes, this is a partnership that adds what are called agent-to-agent, or A2A, capabilities to ServiceNow. Concretely, LogicMonitor's Edwin AI agent can now interact directly with other agents inside ServiceNow, which is a very widely used IT management platform in enterprise environments. This is what we call multi-agent orchestration, and it is one of the biggest challenges standing between experimentation and real production deployment.

Maya: Let's move into the research side of things. And here we have a really concrete, real-world case study on reducing hallucinations in an actual production system.

Ryan: This is honestly one of the most concrete and encouraging studies we have seen in a while. A large retail organization managed to cut the hallucination rate in their multi-agent system from 15 percent down to 1.5 percent. That is a tenfold reduction.

Maya: And how did they pull that off?

Ryan: The answer is actually pretty counterintuitive. They did not swap out their language model. Instead, they centralized what you might call cross-cutting concerns, things like retry logic, schema validation, versioning, and permission management, into a shared platform layer used by all their agents. The model itself is Llama 3.1, hosted locally through Ollama, with LiteLLM handling compatibility across multiple models.

Maya: So it is the architecture doing the heavy lifting, not the model itself.

Ryan: Precisely. And this system is processing billions of inventory records. This is not a lab prototype, this is a live production system. The takeaway for any team deploying AI agents is that the underlying platform engineering matters just as much as which model you pick.

Maya: Our second research item is a bit more sobering: a VentureBeat survey on how much enterprise companies actually trust their AI agents.

Ryan: Yeah, and the numbers tell a pretty telling story. Out of 140 companies surveyed, only 56 percent currently allow their AI agents to push changes to production based on automated checks alone. Back in July 2026, that number was 75 percent. In less than three months, we are seeing a significant pullback in trust.

Maya: So what is driving that growing skepticism?

Ryan: 91 percent of respondents pointed to limitations that undermine their confidence in automated evaluation. And 27 percent specifically called out a mismatch between the evaluation metrics being used and what is actually happening in production. In plain terms: the tools companies use to measure whether an agent is doing a good job do not always reflect what is going on in the real world.

Maya: Which actually brings us right back to the Snowflake and Dynatrace announcements we were just talking about.

Ryan: Exactly. The market for AI agent observability and evaluation is responding to a genuine need, not just hype. Companies are nervous about letting their agents run fully autonomously, and they need tools that give them real visibility into what is actually happening.

Maya: Alright, we are moving into our industry impact segment, and here we are getting into something that hits right at the heart of cybersecurity. Ryan, tell us about PageBreak, Google's security agent.

Ryan: Sure, and this story is a really vivid illustration of both the potential AI agents have in cybersecurity and the questions that come along with it. PageBreak is an agent developed by Google with one mission: detect XSS vulnerabilities, which stands for cross-site scripting. These are flaws that allow attackers to inject malicious code into websites.

Maya: And the results have been pretty impressive.

Ryan: Very impressive. Since it went into production in January 2026, following a pilot that started in November 2025, PageBreak has identified more than 500 XSS vulnerabilities. The agent is designed to be model-agnostic, though it runs primarily on Gemini 3.1 Pro and Gemini 3.5 Flash.

Maya: So an AI agent can scan critical infrastructure at a speed and scale that no human team could match on its own.

Ryan: That is exactly right. And that is where the security angle gets really interesting when you look at it from two sides. On one hand, agents like PageBreak can find vulnerabilities before bad actors can exploit them, which is obviously a huge win. But on the other hand, those same capabilities could theoretically be turned toward offensive use.

Maya: It is a classic double-edged sword.

Ryan: Always. What is reassuring in this case is that Google is running this agent in a controlled, responsible framework and the vulnerabilities being found are getting patched. But it does raise the question of what happens as these techniques become more widely available, and that is exactly why the governance frameworks we mentioned at the top of the show, from Singapore, the White House, and the UN, are becoming so critically important.

Maya: Let's wrap up the industry segment with two more business-focused announcements. First up, AMD with a platform called AMD Ross.

Ryan: AMD is launching AMD Ross, an agentic AI platform built specifically for embedded systems design workflows. The idea is to automate complex steps in the hardware development cycle, which is a space where engineers spend a huge amount of time on repetitive tasks. It follows the same pattern we have already seen with partnerships like GPT and Synopsys in chip design.

Maya: And Cognizant, the IT consulting giant, also had a big announcement this month.

Ryan: Yes, Cognizant is launching Cognizant Activate, a new business unit. The goal is to make the agentic AI capabilities they have built out for large enterprises accessible to startups and mid-sized companies as well. That is a strong signal: agentic AI is no longer just for the Fortune 500. It is moving down market.

Maya: Alright, we are coming up on the end of today's episode. Ryan, if you had to pull out two big takeaways from today, what would they be?

Ryan: The first one is that the battle over AI sovereignty is very real, and it is playing out both at the model level with Beam from Reflection AI, and at the regulatory level with the initiatives coming out of Singapore, the US, and the UN. The second is that trust in AI agents is still fragile. Companies are pulling back, they want more visibility, more control. And that is opening up a massive market for anything in the observability and evaluation space.

Maya: And I would add that AI security, whether that means finding vulnerabilities with something like PageBreak or governing models to prevent malicious use, is clearly one of the most critical challenges of this moment in AI.

Ryan: No question. And we are really just getting started.

Maya: Ryan, thank you as always for the sharp analysis and great conversation. And thank you all so much for listening all the way through. AI Squared is your daily podcast on artificial intelligence, and we will see you tomorrow for another episode. Talk then!
