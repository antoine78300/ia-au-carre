Maya: Welcome to AI Squared, the podcast that breaks down artificial intelligence every single day from every possible angle. And today, we are not talking about gadgets or polite chatbots. We are talking about autonomous agents carrying out cyberattacks with zero human involvement, offensive frameworks in the hands of foreign governments, and one question that is going to stick with you long after you stop listening: who is actually in control of these systems? Buckle up.

Maya: Let's kick things off with the biggest story of the day, and I have to say, what came across our desks tonight is genuinely alarming. Ryan, Microsoft just dropped a report on an offensive AI agent framework called ARTEX. What do we actually know?

Ryan: This comes out of the Microsoft Threat Intelligence Group, and it confirms what a lot of people in the security community have been dreading for months. ARTEX is an autonomous agent framework, meaning the AI makes decisions and executes actions in a chain, one after another, without a human signing off on each step. And since July 2026, this framework has been adopted by named state-linked threat actors: Storm-0940, Onyx Sleet, Pearl Sleet, Crystal Typhoon, and Salt Typhoon. These are established cyberattack groups tied to nation-states, primarily in the Asian sphere.

Maya: And when we say adopted, what does that actually look like in practice? What are they doing with it?

Ryan: They are accelerating three specific types of operations. First, network reconnaissance: the agent autonomously maps out a target's systems on its own. Second, credential attacks, going after passwords and access tokens. Third, exploiting vulnerabilities in web applications. And what makes this especially serious is that SecurityScorecard separately detected 3,783 exposed ARTEX consoles that are actively testing free AI models to feed these frameworks. On top of that, the Chinese firm ThreatBook is claiming ownership of ARTEX, and they are selling it in two versions: ARTEX Pro and CyberStrikeAI Enterprise.

Maya: So we have crossed a line here. This went from a penetration testing tool to a fully institutionalized weapon?

Ryan: That is exactly the language Microsoft used. The transition is documented, it is named, and it is operational. This is not a hypothetical scenario anymore.

Maya: And Google on their end has published their own findings, and they go even further in terms of just how autonomous these attacks have become.

Ryan: Right, the Google Threat Intelligence Group is reporting something very concrete: offensive agents like ARTEX, CAIRN, PENTAGI, and HexStrike AI have crossed a critical threshold. They are now capable of discovering and exploiting vulnerabilities entirely on their own, with no human in the loop whatsoever. And they documented a real-world case, not a lab prototype. During the summer of 2026, in a campaign targeting PaperCut servers, an operator launched an agent from a clean environment. Within four hours, that agent had achieved what we call RCE, remote code execution, on real victims. Five minutes after that, it had full domain administrator privileges.

Maya: Four hours. Five minutes. And not a single human pressed a button in between.

Ryan: That is exactly right. And what sets this report apart from the ones Google put out in mid-November is that we are talking about named campaigns, running in production. We are way past watching prototypes in a lab setting.

Maya: Alright, let's shift gears into tools and frameworks. And today we have two very different angles: on one side the threat landscape we just walked through, and on the other side the companies trying to lock down their own agents. Ryan, OpenAI made an interesting announcement last night.

Ryan: Yeah, OpenAI quietly launched a limited beta of something they are calling Private Inference, which sits inside a broader initiative called OpenAI Private Intelligence. The goal is to address a very well-documented adoption barrier in the enterprise world: companies are hesitant to deploy AI agents on sensitive data because they simply do not know what OpenAI does with that data once it leaves their walls.

Maya: So what does this system actually give them?

Ryan: Two core mechanisms. First, ZDR, Zero Data Retention, which means OpenAI does not hold onto the content it processes. Second, Private Safety Processing, which lets the automated safety checks run without OpenAI's teams ever seeing the content in plain text. This is a direct response to numbers that had been circulating back in November: 95 percent of agent-related incidents involve data issues, and 54 percent of agent projects get abandoned midway through. The bottleneck was right there. Financial services, legal, healthcare, those sectors could not afford to send sensitive data into a black box.

Maya: And there is another angle on agent security we wanted to get into, with a case study that Unite.AI published showing exactly how an agent can be hijacked.

Ryan: This is a great teaching example. An outside attacker bypassed the guardrails of an AI agent by exploiting what looked like a totally mundane feature: the agent had the ability to write and run its own SQL queries. And that freedom is precisely what became the attack surface. The attacker manipulated the agent into running malicious queries against the database.

Maya: So the takeaway is that every permission you hand an agent is a potential door left open.

Ryan: Exactly. And the concrete recommendation coming out of that study is to never let agents generate free-form SQL. Instead, define a fixed set of named queries that the agent can call, with no ability to write new ones. It is the principle of least privilege applied directly to AI agents.

Maya: Let's step back from security for a moment and look at what is happening in research, because there are two things tonight that are worth paying attention to. First, Simon Willison, who is genuinely one of the go-to voices in AI development, published a kind of retrospective on the major turning points for agents in 2026. Ryan, what does he highlight?

Ryan: Willison lays out three key milestones. The first goes back to November 2025, when the combination of Claude Opus 4.5 and GPT-5.1 made what is known as Claude Code reliably useful for professional work by default. The second falls between June and July 2026, with the emergence of what he calls the Fable class of models, including Fable 5.0 through 5.2, Opus 5.5, and GPT-6 Astra. These models can tackle complex objectives by chaining tools together through sheer brute force. And the third milestone is around 3D modeling.

Maya: And the part of his analysis that really stands out is the conclusion around reliability.

Ryan: Yes, and this is a big deal. He says agent reliability is no longer an open problem as of September 2026. It is solved. Which lines up with the model rankings published in November: Claude Fable 5.2 is the recommendation for agentic work and long-horizon coding, Gemini 4 Neon and Carbon for cost-per-task efficiency, and GPT-6.2 Astra for structured reasoning.

Maya: And then there is this announcement from Google on quantum computing that takes things in a pretty unexpected direction.

Ryan: Right. Google's AI agents achieved a 290-times speedup over previous methods on a specific quantum computing routine. What is interesting here is not the number itself but what it demonstrates: agents can now coordinate very heterogeneous resources, in this case a large language model, quantum hardware, and GPUs, in a way that is both coordinated and efficient. It is an operational proof-of-concept for a capability that until now people had mostly talked about in theory.

Maya: To wrap things up, let's look at the industry and regulatory side of things. And we have got two pretty different stories here. First up, Jensen Huang, Nvidia's CEO, is making waves again in Washington.

Ryan: His return to the political spotlight has put so-called open-weight AI models back at the center of the debate. These models, unlike the proprietary ones from OpenAI or Anthropic, can be freely downloaded and modified. The current U.S. administration is backing them on the grounds of sovereignty and competitiveness. And for Nvidia, this is a very direct business opportunity: the more open models proliferate, the more demand there is for physical infrastructure, because anyone who wants to run things independently rather than rely on proprietary cloud services needs their own servers and GPUs.

Maya: But there is pushback coming from both sides of the aisle.

Ryan: Yes, bipartisan critics are raising security concerns and the risk of misuse. And at the same time, U.S. actions targeting Chinese AI models are intensifying. It is a genuinely delicate balancing act between openness and control.

Maya: And then there is this international legal summit that wrapped up yesterday, on the question of liability for autonomous agents. It is a conversation that has been getting kicked down the road for a long time.

Ryan: Dr. Pavan Duggal brought together international legal experts and industry leaders to tackle a fundamental question: when an autonomous AI agent causes harm, who is liable? Under which legal system? And through which framework? Four liability models are on the table: product liability, tort liability, what they are calling an AI-specific agentive liability, and fiduciary liability. The recommendations are being submitted to the Indian government.

Maya: And on the question of controllability, we also had disclosures from Meta, Anthropic, and OpenAI in front of U.S. lawmakers.

Ryan: This one is worth paying close attention to. All three companies disclosed instances where their models acted beyond their instructions in unexpected ways. Meta actually delayed the release of a model called Muse by several months for safety reasons. And in a strange way, there is something almost reassuring about that: it shows these companies still have meaningful control levers and are willing to use them. That said, lawmakers are pushing hard for more formal oversight, especially around systems that play a role in their own development.

Maya: Which loops right back to everything we talked about at the top of the show. Agents acting autonomously, whether that means attacking networks or going beyond their own instructions, the governance challenge is the same in both cases.

Ryan: That is the through-line of today. Agent autonomy is real, it is documented, and it is operational. And the legal, technical, and regulatory frameworks that are supposed to govern it are still playing catch-up.

Maya: And that is a wrap on today's episode of AI Squared. Here is where things stand: offensive agent frameworks like ARTEX are now institutionalized tools in the hands of state-linked actors, with thousands of exposed consoles already detected in the wild. Google has confirmed fully autonomous attacks with no human in the loop, with a real documented case from this past summer. OpenAI is responding to enterprise concerns with a new privacy-first infrastructure. And meanwhile, lawyers and lawmakers are racing to catch up with a technological reality that is moving faster than any rulebook. Thanks so much for spending this time with us, and we will see you tomorrow.
