On the programme : AI regulation and GDPR compliance in Europe, autonomous AI agents, and the impact of AI on data protection.

Full transcript

Maya: Welcome to AI Square, the podcast that breaks down artificial intelligence in your everyday life. Today is September 3rd, 2026, and honestly, it is a lot. Regulation, security, brand new models, massive funding rounds... AI definitely did not take a summer vacation. Buckle up, because we are jumping straight in.

Maya: Ryan, let's kick things off with what I think is the biggest story of the day. Spain just put out something pretty unprecedented in Europe around AI agents and GDPR. Can you walk us through it?

Ryan: Absolutely, Maya. And you are right to lead with this one, because it really is a turning point. The Spanish data protection authority, known as the AEPD, dropped a 71-page document. Now, on its own, a 71-page data protection document might sound like a snooze fest. But what makes this historic is the specific topic it tackles: agentic architecture.

Maya: And for listeners who might not be super familiar with that term, what exactly is an AI agent?

Ryan: Great question. An AI agent is a program that does not just answer your questions. It actually goes out and does things on its own. We are talking browsing the web, sending emails, booking flights, interacting with other software, all without a human signing off on every single step. And it is precisely that autonomy that creates totally new headaches when it comes to personal data protection.

Maya: Because at that point, you do not have a human clicking and deciding anymore. You have a system acting completely on its own.

Ryan: Exactly. And GDPR, the European data protection regulation, was designed for a world where a human being was making the processing decisions. Now you have an autonomous machine collecting, processing, and transferring data. So who is responsible? How do you guarantee consent? How do you make sure data is not being used beyond what was originally intended? These are questions the original GDPR text just does not clearly answer.

Maya: And that is what the AEPD is trying to clear up with this document?

Ryan: Yes, and this is the first time a European supervisory authority has gone head-on at this question and put agentic architecture front and center. This is not a footnote buried in a broader report. This is the whole point. That means Europe is starting to build its own intellectual and regulatory framework specifically for AI agents. Other national authorities are almost certainly going to look to this as a model. And for any company developing or deploying AI agents in Europe, this document is going to become essential reading.

Maya: So this is a strong signal that regulating AI agents is becoming a top priority in Europe.

Ryan: No question about it. And the timing matters, because enterprise deployments of AI agents are scaling up fast. Regulation is trying to keep pace with the technology, and for once, Europe is actually moving quickly.

Maya: Speaking of which, let's talk tools and frameworks, because today is absolutely packed on that front too. We have got several major releases. Ryan, let's start with OWASP. For anyone who is not familiar, who are they?

Ryan: OWASP stands for the Open Worldwide Application Security Project. It is a nonprofit organization that is basically the gold standard in application security. Their Top 10 web vulnerabilities list is the bible for any serious developer. Well, today they are releasing their 2026 edition of the Top 10 for LLM applications, large language models, and they are adding something new to the mix: a dedicated security control standard for AI agents.

Maya: When we talk about vulnerabilities for an LLM, what does that actually look like in the real world?

Ryan: So a classic example is prompt injection. That is when a bad actor phrases a question in a way that tricks an AI agent into saying something it should not say, or taking actions it was never supposed to take. You also have data leaks in shared contexts, manipulation of the tools an agent has access to, and issues around placing too much trust in agents within automated action chains.

Maya: And this 2026 ranking is grounded in actual real-world data?

Ryan: That is what makes it particularly credible. It was put together by hundreds of AI security experts and is based on thousands of documented real-world incidents. This is not theoretical. On top of that, it maps to established frameworks like NIST, MITRE ATLAS, and CWE, so security teams can fold it into their existing processes without starting from scratch.

Maya: Alright, let's talk about MCP, the Model Context Protocol. This is an acronym we are hearing more and more. What is it?

Ryan: MCP is a standard protocol that lets AI agents connect to external tools. Picture this: you have got an AI agent that needs to query a database, send a Slack message, and then pull a file from Google Drive. Without a shared standard, every developer had to build their own custom connectors for each tool. It was an enormous amount of work, and a lot of it was just being duplicated over and over.

Maya: Kind of like if every car manufacturer invented their own screws instead of using standard sizes.

Ryan: That is a perfect analogy. MCP is exactly that: a common standard. And what gives it serious credibility right now is that it is now governed by the Linux Foundation, an organization with a rock-solid track record in managing open standards, and it has been adopted by all the major LLM providers. Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, they are all on board with MCP.

Maya: And practically speaking, what does this change for developers?

Ryan: It changes everything. Instead of rebuilding the same bridges between their agents and the outside world from scratch every single time, developers can rely on an ecosystem of standardized connectors. That is a massive time saver, and it cuts down on the security vulnerabilities that come from sloppy homegrown implementations. And we are actually seeing a solution emerge today called JetStream Clearance, which applies Zero Trust security principles specifically to these tool calls happening through MCP. The idea is to systematically verify every single action an agent tries to take before allowing it, even inside a corporate network.

Maya: So we are watching a complete infrastructure take shape around agents: standards like MCP, security layers like JetStream, and now governance tools like Creatio AI Studio, which you also wanted to touch on?

Ryan: Right, Creatio is launching a platform that lets enterprises create and deploy AI agents with centralized governance: audit dashboards, role-based permissions, and compatibility across multiple models like Claude, GPT, and Gemini. That is exactly what large organizations need to stay in control when they are rolling out dozens or even hundreds of agents at once.

Maya: Let's shift over to research and new models, because today is absolutely an announcement marathon. Ryan, we have got OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, and Google each dropping something. How would you sum up this day of launches?

Ryan: Maya, this day really captures just how frantic the competition between the big labs has gotten. Let's start with OpenAI, which is announcing a new model called Astra. What is interesting here is that the spotlight is not just on raw model performance, but also on the enhanced safety protocols that come with it.

Maya: Why such a heavy emphasis on safety?

Ryan: Because apparently, during earlier testing of this model or close versions of it, there were incidents where the model was accessing private data in ways that were not intended. So OpenAI took the time to go back and rework its alignment mechanisms before releasing it. That is a positive sign in terms of rigor, but it also raises some questions about exactly what happened during those tests.

Maya: What about Anthropic?

Ryan: Anthropic is rolling out Claude Fable 5.1 with a piece of news that developers are going to love: a 75 percent price cut on cache read costs. We are talking 25 cents per million tokens. That is significant because in applications that frequently reference the same contexts, cache costs were eating up a meaningful chunk of the bill. This makes things a lot more accessible.

Maya: And Meta is stepping into the ring too with Muse Spark 1.3?

Ryan: Yes, Meta is announcing Muse Spark 1.3, which they are positioning as outperforming GPT-5.6 Sol and competitive with Claude Fable 5.1, especially in coding tasks. Meta keeps pushing its models as serious contenders, and the benchmark wars are very much ongoing.

Maya: And where does Google fit into all this?

Ryan: Google is putting out Gemini 3.8 Flash. And here is a fun detail: this is the third Flash model Google has released in just six weeks. That points to a strategy of very rapid releases in the Flash tier, the fast and cost-efficient models, while the Pro models seem to be on hold. Some people are actually starting to wonder if Gemini 3.5 Pro will ever see the light of day.

Maya: It sounds like Google is betting on speed and affordability over raw horsepower right now.

Ryan: That is exactly right. And it could turn out to be a very smart play, because Flash models are the ones that power agentic applications for repetitive, high-volume tasks. In that space, cost and speed often matter more than peak performance.

Maya: Let's close things out with the industry impact angle, and there is one story here that really deserves a closer look: HiddenLayer just raised 100 million dollars. Ryan, that is a serious amount of money for an AI security company. What does that tell us about where the market is heading?

Ryan: It tells us that AI agent security is becoming its own full-blown market, and one that investors see as strategically critical. HiddenLayer builds a platform dedicated to securing generative and agentic AI systems. A 100-million-dollar Series B is a strong vote of confidence.

Maya: And the numbers they put out in their 2026 report are pretty striking.

Ryan: Very striking. 96 percent of the organizations surveyed consider AI critical to their business. And yet one third of them cannot confirm they have never experienced an AI-related breach. That means security has not kept up with adoption. Companies have gone all in on AI deployment, but without always putting the right safeguards in place.

Maya: It is almost counterintuitive: the more critical AI becomes, the more exposed organizations are.

Ryan: That is exactly it. And that is why when you look at today as a whole, you see this really coherent convergence of signals. The AEPD is putting out regulatory guidance on agents. OWASP is publishing a security standard for agents. JetStream is bringing Zero Trust to agents. HiddenLayer is raising 100 million dollars to secure agents. And Creatio is offering governance for agents.

Maya: All roads lead to agents.

Ryan: Absolutely. And it reveals something important: the industry is collectively waking up to the fact that AI agents are no longer a research topic or an experiment. These are systems that are going to be making real decisions, accessing sensitive data, and acting in the real world on behalf of organizations and individuals. And that realization is generating enormous demand for frameworks, standards, and tools to keep them in check.

Maya: And what about end users? Are they seeing the benefits too, like with Anthropic's commerce agents?

Ryan: Yes, and that is a great example to bring it down to earth. Anthropic deployed specialized tools today for building agents in e-commerce. The results being reported by retailers in the pilot are pretty eye-opening: shoppers are adding an average of 60 percent more items to their cart when interacting with these agents. That shows AI agents can genuinely transform the user experience and drive real business outcomes, not just streamline back-end processes.

Maya: And that is a wrap on today's episode of AI Square. What a day. We saw Europe lay the first building blocks of a regulatory framework for AI agents, OWASP establish security standards, MCP cement itself as shared infrastructure, a flood of new models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, and Google, and 100 million dollars flowing into agent security. If you take one thing away from today, let it be this: 2026 is the year the world starts taking AI agents seriously, not just as a fascinating technology, but as infrastructure that needs to be governed, secured, and kept in check.

Ryan: And this is just the beginning. The next few months are going to be defining in terms of how these standards and regulations come together on a global scale.

Maya: Ryan, thanks as always for breaking it all down. And thank you for listening to AI Square. We will see you tomorrow for another episode. Until then, stay curious.
