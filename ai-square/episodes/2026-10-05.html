Full transcript

Maya: Welcome to AI Squared, the podcast that breaks down artificial intelligence in your everyday life. We are kicking things off strong today: AI agents infiltrating banks, cutting-edge mathematics being solved by machines, and some serious questions about who is really in control when automated systems start calling the shots. It is Monday, October 5th, 2026, and you are in exactly the right place.

Maya: Ryan, let's dive right in with a story that is honestly a little chilling. We are talking about an attack on Korean banks. Walk us through what happened.

Ryan: Yeah, this is probably the most alarming development of the week. A system called Atex AI, which is an autonomous pentesting tool built on top of a large language model, managed to break into Korean banking systems. Shinhan Bank is specifically named in the incident. And what this system actually did was exfiltrate personal customer data.

Maya: Hold on, let's back up for a second. Pentesting. For listeners who may not be familiar with that term, what are we actually talking about?

Ryan: So pentesting is short for penetration testing. Normally it is a completely legitimate practice where authorized security teams try to break into a computer system on purpose, to find the vulnerabilities before real attackers do. The problem here is that Atex AI used open-source multi-agent frameworks to do this autonomously and without any authorization whatsoever. This is the first confirmed operational incident of autonomous AI agents compromising a financial institution at this scale.

Maya: And when you say open-source, you mean essentially anyone could potentially get their hands on these same tools?

Ryan: Exactly, and that is what makes this situation especially serious. We are not talking about some secret technology locked away in a nation-state weapons program or only accessible to the most sophisticated criminal organizations. These are publicly available building blocks, combined with a multi-agent architecture that gives them the ability to act on their own. What this validates is something security researchers have been worried about for a while now: agentic risks are no longer theoretical in regulated financial environments. They are real and they are happening.

Maya: Do we have any details on the scope of the personal data that was stolen?

Ryan: What is available right now mentions personal data being exfiltrated without specifying the exact volume. But honestly, the scale is almost beside the point here. What matters is the precedent this sets. An autonomous AI agent punched through the defenses of a commercial bank. That fundamentally changes the threat landscape for the entire global financial sector.

Maya: A wake-up call that every bank out there should be paying attention to. Now we are going to stay on the topic of AI agent governance and security, because there is a closely related story. Ryan, MLCommons published something significant at the start of October?

Ryan: Yes, and you could almost read it as a direct response to exactly the kind of situation we just described. MLCommons, which is a leading organization in AI benchmarking and standards, has published the first formal framework that catalogs twenty-five specific privacy risk vectors tied to autonomous agents.

Maya: Twenty-five risks. That is a lot. How do they organize all of that?

Ryan: Those twenty-five risks are spread across five distinct domains. And one of the most useful things about this framework is that it lays out a six-step method for tracing an incident from the moment it is triggered all the way through to the actual harm it causes. In practical terms, that helps you analyze what went wrong after the fact, but it also helps you anticipate ahead of time how an autonomous agent could end up causing a privacy violation.

Maya: Who is the intended audience here? Engineers? Lawyers? Regulators?

Ryan: All three, honestly, and that is one of its biggest strengths. MLCommons is explicitly bridging a gap that has existed between, on one side, the regulatory guidance coming out of bodies like Spain's AEPD or UK regulators, and on the other side, concrete engineering standards. Those two worlds have not been speaking the same language. This framework creates a common vocabulary between them.

Maya: So it is basically a translation layer between lawyers and developers.

Ryan: Exactly. And in a world where AI agents are making increasingly autonomous decisions, having shared language to identify, name, and trace privacy risks is genuinely meaningful progress.

Maya: Alright, let's shift over to tools and platforms. We have got two big infrastructure announcements for AI agents. Ryan, CoreWeave and DigitalOcean both made moves at the same time?

Ryan: Yeah, and the timing is striking. CoreWeave deployed a platform called Forge, which brings three major capabilities to the table. The first is Agent Lens, an observability system that catches twenty percent more critical errors compared to traditional methods, and does it at one tenth of the usual benchmarking cost. The second is Sandboxes, which are fully isolated environments for each individual agent execution. And the third is a Registry for managing agent versions in a portable way.

Maya: And what is DigitalOcean bringing to the table?

Ryan: DigitalOcean is launching on the same day what they are calling Agent Droplets, at fifty dollars a month. Isolation is handled through a technology called Firecracker, and the platform gives you access to sixteen thousand governed tools.

Maya: Sixteen thousand tools is a pretty staggering number. But what does all of this tell us about where the AI infrastructure market is heading?

Ryan: It very clearly confirms a major underlying shift. Cloud infrastructure providers are no longer just renting out compute power or GPU capacity. They are becoming full-stack platforms for running agents, with governance, observability, and isolation built right in. CoreWeave was primarily known up until now as a GPU rental company. With Forge, they are becoming a player at the agentic layer. That is a major strategic repositioning.

Maya: And when you talk about observability, what does that actually mean in practice for an AI agent?

Ryan: It means having the ability to see what an agent is doing at every single step of its execution. When an agent is making decisions autonomously, if you have no visibility into its internal behavior, you have no idea what happened when something goes wrong. Agent Lens from CoreWeave provides that traceability, and given the banking incident we just talked about, it is pretty easy to see why that matters.

Maya: The connection is crystal clear. We are also talking today about an approach that can dramatically cut the cost of running AI agents. This is the Jev story?

Ryan: Right. Jev is an architecture built around what are called decision models. The core idea is simple but really effective: instead of calling a powerful large language model every single time for every single task, the agent first uses a much lighter decision model to evaluate what it actually needs.

Maya: So if the task is straightforward, it never even calls the big model?

Ryan: Exactly. The decision model assesses the confidence level required and the nature of the task. If it is simple and structured, it handles it directly. It only calls the large model when confidence is low or when you genuinely need to generate complex text. The documented result is a seventy-three percent cost reduction, which works out to a factor of 3.7 compared to a conventional approach.

Maya: Seventy-three percent is a massive number for any company running agents in production.

Ryan: Absolutely. And there is an additional angle worth noting here: these lightweight decision models, which have between 322 million and 421 million parameters, can actually run locally on a device, without touching the cloud at all. That opens up use cases in environments where privacy or connectivity are real constraints.

Maya: Let's move on to research. Ryan, Meta made a pretty spectacular announcement in the world of mathematics.

Ryan: This is an announcement that deserves to be understood for what it really is. Meta announced on October 2nd, 2026 that its Muse Spark models helped mathematicians produce six research papers covering very different areas: probability, differential equations, group theory, optimization, arithmetic physics, and non-associative algebra.

Maya: And these six papers, are they actually addressing real open questions in the academic world?

Ryan: Five of the six, yes. Five of these papers answer questions that were previously open, meaning no one had solved them yet. To be clear, Meta is not solving these problems on its own. The Muse Spark models are working alongside human mathematicians. But AI's contribution to autonomous mathematical discovery has now been validated operationally.

Maya: You used the phrase validated operationally. What does that mean precisely in this context?

Ryan: It means this is no longer a lab demo or an artificial benchmark. These are real research papers submitted within the actual practice of mathematics. Agentic AI is genuinely pushing the frontier of human knowledge forward. That is a real change in status for this type of system.

Maya: There is also a hardware dimension to this Meta announcement, right?

Ryan: Yes, Meta is simultaneously announcing Muse Gadgets, an open-source SDK compatible with ESP32 boards and Linux. That lets outside developers build physical hardware that integrates with the Muse ecosystem. The extension of agentic AI into physical, autonomous hardware integration is starting to take concrete shape.

Maya: Fascinating stuff. On the industry impact side, we have got two topics to cover. Starting with agents that are beginning to make purchasing decisions inside companies?

Ryan: This is a deep transformation in what we think of as supply chains. Software agents can now negotiate with suppliers, optimize procurement choices, and what you might call acquire autonomous economic authority. There is an image being used to describe this that really lands: the software can now effectively fire a supplier, meaning end a business relationship, without a human signing off at every step.

Maya: And what is the governance risk that creates?

Ryan: The core risk is that significant economic decisions are being made inside processes where human oversight is either absent or shows up too late to matter. In a normal scenario, if an agent makes the wrong call on a supplier or acts on poorly configured criteria, the consequences can be substantial before any human is even alerted. Governance around agentic purchasing decisions is a real gap in most organizations today.

Maya: And on the consumer side, Google made some changes to Gemini that hit users directly?

Ryan: Yes, and this is a very clear strategic pivot. In October 2026, Google is restricting free access to Gemini to a single lightweight model. Gemini Pro, which was previously at least partially available to free users, is now locked behind an AI Pro subscription at 19.99 dollars a month. And the new Gemini 4 Argon model, which is launching at the same time, is limited exclusively to verified cybersecurity defenders through a platform called Fairwind.

Maya: So Google is very deliberately segmenting its offering between general consumers, paying subscribers, and now this separate tier of security professionals.

Ryan: Exactly. And the Argon pricing for those professionals is two dollars per million input tokens, ten dollars per million output tokens, with a ninety-five percent discount on cached tokens. This is a pretty aggressive monetization of the free tier, and a market segmentation strategy that looks very deliberate and very structured. The broader question it raises is one of equitable access: whether high-quality AI tools are becoming something only available to users with the financial means to pay for them.

Maya: And on the government side, we had a significant US announcement this week.

Ryan: Trump announced the creation of what he is calling the Super Intelligence Force. It is worth clarifying right away that the term superintelligence as he is using it here does not carry the same meaning it has in AI research, where it refers to a hypothetical system that would surpass human intelligence across the board. This is the president's own terminology.

Maya: What does this actually involve in concrete terms?

Ryan: What is notable in the announcement is that it follows a White House meeting with CEOs from major tech companies. Those executives signed what is being described as a morally binding contract to submit their AI models to independent third-party audits. The stated goal of those audits would be to verify that the models are functioning as intended. It is explicit government pressure for operational agentic governance.

Maya: And that is a wrap on a very packed edition. Ryan, if you had to pull one common thread out of everything we covered today, what would it be?

Ryan: What really stands out to me is the tension between two dynamics that are both accelerating at the same time. On one side, the power and autonomy of AI agents is growing at a serious pace, whether that is in offensive cybersecurity, scientific discovery, or business management. On the other side, the tools to govern, observe, and secure those agents are trying to keep up. The Korean bank attack and the MLCommons framework both landing in the same week is almost a perfect illustration of that tension made visible.

Maya: A tension that is almost certainly going to be at the center of the conversation for months to come. Thank you so much for joining us for this edition of AI Squared. If you are finding this show useful, share it with someone who needs to hear it, and make sure you are subscribed so you never miss an episode. We will see you tomorrow for another deep dive into everything happening in the world of artificial intelligence. Talk to you then.
