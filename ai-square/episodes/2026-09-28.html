Ryan: Absolutely. So we have two separate stories, but the way they connect is honestly chilling. The first one is the discovery by ThreatDown researchers of a botnet they're calling CARBONATO. It runs on Docker containers, and its command-and-control engine, basically the brain directing the entire malicious operation, is an AI agent. A genuine autonomous agent built on a modified open-source framework.

Maya: And the part that really stops you in your tracks is how long it's been running.

Ryan: Exactly. Since October 2024. Eleven months of flying completely under the radar. It is literally self-funded. It receives tasks through Telegram, generates shell commands, and automates both reconnaissance and exploitation with zero human involvement.

Maya: So this is the first real-world proof of a fully autonomous agentic botnet. Not a lab concept, not a proof of concept, an active operation running for nearly a year.

Ryan: And that connects directly to the second revelation, which comes from Dig.Watch, and it covers a Windows implant called CLOSEDQUORUM. This one is even more sophisticated: the implant simultaneously queries multiple LLMs, DeepSeek, Qwen, Mistral, Gemini, and uses a majority-vote system to decide what actions to take. Credential theft, process injection, system persistence. The AI has literally become the primary operational layer of the attack chain.

Maya: What you're describing is a shift from cybercrime-as-a-service to something way more autonomous.

Ryan: That's exactly right. Before, you had humans renting out malicious tools and running them themselves. Now you have AI agents handling reconnaissance, exploitation, and persistence end to end. The human friction is gone from the attack chain. And that changes everything when it comes to speed, scale, and detection.

Ryan: Right, Google just published four agentic frameworks focused on long-form video generation, presented at the COLM 2026 and EMNLP 2026 conferences. The first one is called Co-Director. The core idea is to use what's called a multi-armed bandit algorithm to dynamically orchestrate several specialized agents, each one handling a different aspect of video creation: creative strategy, narrative mode, aesthetic style.

Maya: So practically speaking, what does this actually solve for long-form video? Because that has been the core problem.

Ryan: The fundamental issue is consistency. If you ask a model to generate a video that's several minutes long, characters change appearance between scenes, objects vanish, backgrounds become incoherent. Google's second framework, CANVAS, directly tackles that by adding persistent visual memory. The system keeps track of characters, objects, and locations, and actively corrects the consistency failures that showed up in systems like AutoStudio or even Gemini when dealing with complex scenes.

Maya: So it's a multi-agent architecture with a closed-loop feedback system.

Ryan: Exactly. And it's proof that for complex creative tasks, a multi-agent approach with LLM judgment evaluating and correcting outputs in production is now genuinely viable. That's a real qualitative leap.

Maya: Okay, let's move to the second tool story. SoundHound AI launched something interesting for embedded devices.

Ryan: Yeah, this is OASYS Edge. The concept is straightforward but the technical challenge was massive: getting LLM-powered voice AI agents to run directly on embedded hardware, think vehicles and smart appliances, without needing to talk to the cloud. Until now, the compute requirements made that commercially impossible at scale.

Maya: And what's the real value proposition for device makers?

Ryan: It's a double win. First, you no longer need a constant connection, which is critical in a vehicle out in a rural area or an industrial device in the field. Second, and this is where the architecture gets really smart, a developer builds their agent once on the platform and can deploy it on cloud, edge, or hybrid without rebuilding from scratch. That dramatically cuts down the friction for manufacturers. Commercial rollout is slated for the end of 2026.

Maya: Build it once, deploy it anywhere. That's the core promise.

Ryan: And it's a very attractive promise for the entire industrial ecosystem that wants to integrate AI without having to deal with the complexity of cloud infrastructure.

Maya: Perfect. Let's move into our research segment, and we have a genuinely notable robotics breakthrough here. Ryan, tell us about the work coming out of Skylark Labs.

Ryan: So Skylark Labs, working alongside Carnegie Mellon and UC Berkeley, developed what they're calling Continual Field-Adaptive Models, or CFAMs. And the problem they're going after is one of the most frustrating limitations in robotics: the fact that once a robot is deployed in the field, it can't keep learning without being completely retrained from scratch.

Maya: And retraining a foundation model is brutally expensive.

Ryan: Expensive in compute, in time, and in money. And on top of that there's a technical issue called catastrophic forgetting: when you retrain a model to absorb new experiences, it tends to lose what it already knew. CFAM sidesteps all of that by not trying to modify the millions of parameters in the base model. Instead, every successful new experience gets stored as what they call a situationally retrievable capsule.

Maya: So the robot basically remembers which situations a given approach worked in, and can pull that up when it encounters something similar again.

Ryan: Exactly. And it accesses those capsules on demand based on context. What's especially important is that this works in the most constrained real-world conditions: limited compute, unstable or zero connectivity. A maintenance robot in an isolated environment can keep improving without ever sending its data back home.

Maya: That's a genuine paradigm shift for field-deployed robots.

Ryan: And potentially for a lot of applications beyond robotics: any embedded system that needs to keep learning under low-connectivity conditions. The industrial implications are huge.
