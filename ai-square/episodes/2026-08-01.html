Full transcript

Maya: Good morning and welcome to AI Square! It is Saturday, the sun is out, and we are right here where we always are, coffee in hand, ready to dig into all things artificial intelligence. Because AI does not take weekends off, and neither do we! I am Maya, and as always I have Ryan with me. Ryan, how are you feeling this morning?

Ryan: Honestly, really good! And this week I have got stuff to share that genuinely blew my mind. We are talking big developments, fascinating research, and even a little legal drama in the music world.

Maya: Oh I love when there is drama. Alright, where do we start?

Ryan: We start with robots. And not just any robots.

Maya: Google DeepMind robots?

Ryan: Exactly. So Google DeepMind just unveiled Gemini Robotics 2. And when I say unveiled, I mean this is a serious, substantive announcement, not just a slick YouTube video of a robot doing a little dance.

Maya: Because we have all seen those videos where the robot looks incredible and then in reality it can barely cross a room without falling over.

Ryan: Right, and you are actually hitting on something really important here. The big challenge with humanoid robots has never really been raw intelligence, it is physical coordination. Staying upright, grabbing a moving object, recovering when you start to lose your balance. What we call motor dexterity. And that is exactly where Gemini Robotics 2 is genuinely changing the game.

Maya: So what did they do differently?

Ryan: They developed what they call whole-body control. The idea is that the robot no longer drives its limbs like they are separate independent parts. It thinks and moves in a unified way, the way we do. When you reach up to grab a glass off a high shelf, you are not consciously calculating the angle of your elbow, the rotation of your wrist, your center of gravity. Your brain handles all of that at the same time, seamlessly integrated. Gemini Robotics 2 is getting close to that.

Maya: And what does that actually look like in practice? What can it do now that it could not do before?

Ryan: It can handle the unexpected. That is the key phrase here. Before, industrial robots were great in controlled environments with well-defined repetitive tasks. Now we are talking about a robot that can adapt to a situation it has never encountered before. If you place an object at a weird angle, it adjusts its grip. If it starts to slip, it compensates. And there is also a multi-robot collaboration dimension, where multiple units coordinate their actions on a single task.

Maya: Wait, multiple robots working together? That sounds a little bit like the beginning of a robot army, no?

Ryan: Ha! In the scary science fiction sense, not quite yet. But in the genuinely useful sense, absolutely. Think about a logistics warehouse, or an assembly line, or even complex medical procedures. Having multiple robots intelligently dividing up a task is a massive efficiency gain.

Maya: And there is also a second model in this family, the Gemini Robotics ER 2?

Ryan: Yeah, and this one is the thinker of the group. ER stands for Enhanced Reasoning. This model is more focused on planning. It can understand its environment, anticipate the steps of a complex multi-minute task, and crucially it detects its own mistakes and self-corrects. It is kind of like a robot that keeps a mental checklist and tells itself, step one, step two, oh wait I messed up step three, let me back up and try again.

Maya: Robot self-awareness. That is fascinating. And Wall Street loved the announcement apparently?

Ryan: Alphabet was up three percent after the news. The market clearly sees AI robotics as the next major playing field. This is not conference-stage science fiction anymore, this is serious product development.

Maya: Alright. Let us move on to our experiments and original projects segment because I have been looking forward to this one. There is some touch in the air?

Ryan: Literally! So this is a project I found really elegant in its concept. Two companies are teaming up, Lightwheel AI and ViTai Robotics, and their goal is to give robots a sense of touch.

Maya: Are we talking about actual tactile sensors?

Ryan: We are talking about visuotactile sensors. It is a combination of vision and touch. These sensors can simultaneously see and feel a surface, its texture, its resistance, its temperature in some cases. Lightwheel AI handles the data and simulation side, meaning how you train an AI model on millions of tactile interactions, and ViTai Robotics provides the hardware, the physical sensors themselves.

Maya: It is funny because when we think about AI we always go straight to vision or language, but touch is a sense we have basically completely overlooked.

Ryan: And yet it is absolutely fundamental! Think about how much information you get through touch without even realizing it. You know if a piece of fruit is ripe just by giving it a gentle squeeze. You know if you are about to break something based on the resistance you feel. Up until now robots have been kind of like surgeons trying to operate while wearing oven mitts.

Maya: That is a really helpful image for understanding the problem. And the goal of this partnership is to scale it up? Not just a lab prototype?

Ryan: Exactly. They want to build a large-scale tactile data infrastructure. Because the real challenge of physical AI is not just the model itself, it is the training data. For a robot to learn how to hold a glass without crushing it or dropping it, you need millions of examples of grasping with every possible variation. This partnership wants to build that database of the physical world.

Maya: This is the moment where I make the joke that AI is going to have more sensitivity than some of my exes.

Ryan: I am not touching that one. But technically, it is not out of the question.

Maya: Fair enough. Let us roll into our society and science segment because I have two topics I really care about this week. Pharma and music.

Ryan: Two completely different worlds, and yet AI is right at the center of both of them this week.

Maya: Let us start with the drug discovery stuff because the announcements here are genuinely significant. There is Insilico Medicine on one side and Bristol Myers Squibb on the other.

Ryan: Two different approaches. Insilico Medicine is a startup that specializes in AI-driven drug discovery, and they just launched something pretty bold: a benchmarking platform. Basically a tool to objectively evaluate and compare the capabilities of AI models in pharmaceutical research.

Maya: Bold, why?

Ryan: Because in this industry, everyone claims their AI is the best. But actually measuring that in a rigorous and transparent way means opening yourself up to scrutiny. Insilico is doing exactly that, and they back it up with a real track record: 31 preclinical drug candidates nominated in six years, and one drug discovered entirely by AI that is now in Phase Three clinical trials. That is unprecedented.

Maya: Phase Three is the home stretch before potential market approval, right?

Ryan: That is right. Clinical trials have three phases. Phase Three is the large-scale trials involving thousands of patients to confirm both efficacy and safety. Having an AI-designed drug make it that far is a major scientific validation.

Maya: And what about Bristol Myers Squibb and NVIDIA?

Ryan: This one is about industrial-scale ambition. BMS is a pharmaceutical giant, and they are investing alongside NVIDIA in what they are calling an AI factory. The idea is to build a computing infrastructure with specialized models entirely dedicated to medical research, cutting down discovery timelines, reducing the repetitive manual workload on researchers, and improving the odds that a drug candidate makes it through the different development stages.

Maya: How much time are we potentially talking about saving?

Ryan: Traditional drug discovery averages somewhere between ten and fifteen years. The ambition with AI-driven approaches is to cut that in half, or even by two thirds in certain phases. It is not systematic yet, but the signals are definitely there.

Maya: That is fascinating and honestly we do not talk about it enough. Alright. Music now. And there is drama like you said.

Ryan: There is drama and there is also something genuinely historic happening. Let us start with the lawsuit. GEMA, which is the German music rights management society, just won its case against Suno.

Maya: Suno being the AI music generator.

Ryan: Right. GEMA was suing Suno for copyright infringement, arguing that the model had been trained on protected works without authorization and without any compensation. And the court ruled in GEMA's favor. This is a decision that is going to set legal precedent across Europe.

Maya: So that means AI music companies can no longer just freely help themselves to existing catalogs?

Ryan: That is the strong message coming out of this ruling. And it fits into a much bigger movement. Because at the same time, the major labels and independent labels have agreed on global standards to govern AI music in official charts.

Maya: So actual rules for whether an AI song can show up in the rankings?

Ryan: Exactly. They define three categories. Purely human creativity, legitimate AI-assisted work where an artist is using AI as a tool, and fully synthetic tracks with no meaningful human creative involvement. There are also technical requirements, mandatory metadata to identify where a track came from, and measures against fraudulent streams that are automatically generated to inflate the numbers.

Maya: Because that was also a real problem, people were dumping thousands of AI songs onto streaming platforms just to collect royalties?

Ryan: That is exactly it. And it was distorting the charts, diluting revenue for real artists, and basically creating a kind of large-scale musical pollution. These new rules are an attempt to bring some order back to the whole ecosystem.

Maya: That is a packed week. Alright, we have made it to my favorite part. The what if.

Ryan: Ah, the philosophical moment of the show.

Maya: Exactly. What if all these robots that are learning to feel, to coordinate their bodies, to plan complex multi-step tasks, what if in twenty years we had robot nurses working in hospitals?

Ryan: That is a genuinely serious question. Because what we have been describing today with Gemini Robotics 2, the dexterity, the ability to adapt to the unexpected, the sense of touch with visuotactile sensors, that is almost exactly the skill set you need from a nurse. Helping someone get up, monitoring vitals, administering treatment with precision.

Maya: And in a context of healthcare worker shortages or an aging population, that argument gets very concrete very fast.

Ryan: There is a deep human question underneath all of this, obviously. Do we actually want to be cared for by a robot? But if the alternative is not being cared for at all, that answer can shift pretty quickly. And I think the right question is not robot versus human, it is how does the robot free up time for the human caregiver to focus on what really matters: the relationship, the complex diagnosis, the emotional support.

Maya: The robot handling the repetitive tasks so the human can be more human.

Ryan: That might actually be the real promise behind all the robotics we talked about today.

Maya: That is a great note to wrap on. And if you want to keep exploring all of this with us, you know where to find us. Thank you so much for spending your Saturday morning with AI Square.

Ryan: Do yourself a favor and step away from the screens for a bit this afternoon. Enjoy the day.

Maya: And we will see you right back here tomorrow for a brand new episode. See you tomorrow!
