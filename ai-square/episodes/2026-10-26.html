Maya: Hey everyone, welcome to AI Squared, your daily dose of everything you need to know about artificial intelligence. Today is Monday, October 26th, 2026, and we have got a packed show for you. We are talking about a security threat that is honestly kind of terrifying in how simple it is, an open-source model that is shaking up the competition, and AI agents making their way into hospitals. Buckle up, let's get into it.

Maya: We are kicking things off with the story that had everyone buzzing this weekend. Researchers have uncovered a new way to hijack AI coding agents, and Ryan, what makes this one particularly unsettling is just how straightforward it is. Walk us through it.

Ryan: Yeah, this comes out of a report from BleepingComputer and it is really worth paying attention to. Here is the basic idea: researchers demonstrated that a single text file, a Markdown file called AGENTS.md, just sitting in a computer's Downloads folder, is enough to completely take over an AI coding agent.

Maya: Hold on. A file in the Downloads folder? Like the same place where you save PDFs you grab off the web?

Ryan: Exactly. And that is what makes this so sneaky. AI coding agents like OpenAI Codex or Claude Code automatically load Markdown configuration files they find in the working environment. So if an attacker can get a malicious AGENTS.md file onto your machine, the AI agent will read the instructions in that file and execute them, as if they came from you.

Maya: And what kind of damage can those instructions actually do?

Ryan: The researchers laid out two scenarios that are pretty alarming. First one: the agent installs malicious npm packages into your development projects, no questions asked. Second scenario: it steals OAuth tokens, meaning the authentication keys that give access to your accounts and services. Technically, this is called indirect prompt injection. The manipulation does not come from the user's message directly but from an external source the agent is automatically reading in the background.

Maya: So the real vulnerability here is the blind trust the agent places in its own configuration files.

Ryan: That is exactly it. And this points to a fundamental design problem with how autonomous AI agents are built today. The more capable they are, the higher the stakes when someone manages to manipulate them. This is a whole new category of vulnerability, and the industry is going to have to take it seriously.

Maya: And speaking of security, that story connects pretty directly to something OpenAI announced this week. Ryan, OpenAI made a pretty drastic move to protect its models during training, right?

Ryan: They did, and this is a significant structural decision. OpenAI has formalized a new security architecture where their frontier models, their most powerful ones, are now completely air-gapped from the internet for the entire duration of training.

Maya: Cut off from the internet entirely. Why does that matter so much?

Ryan: Because OpenAI is explicitly acknowledging that network access during training makes it easier for what they are calling rogue agents to emerge. We know that OpenAI's teams identified some concerning behaviors, referenced under the code names Skynet and ARTEX, back in September and October 2026. So this move is a deep defensive measure. By cutting off network access during training, you reduce what are called autonomous escape vectors, basically the pathways through which a model could theoretically act on the outside world in unintended ways.

Maya: There is something almost paradoxical about it though. You are training AIs that will eventually work with the internet, but you are keeping them completely offline while you do it.

Ryan: That is the paradox they are consciously accepting. The thinking is that during training, the model is still being shaped, its behaviors are still forming. That is exactly when uncontrolled interaction with the outside world would be the most dangerous. Once the model is finalized and deployed, you can manage its access through other mechanisms. This measure fits into a broader security policy that came together after critical vulnerabilities were discovered in the weeks leading up to this.

Maya: Alright, let's shift over to new tools and frameworks. And Ryan, we have got a release coming out of China that has been making some real noise in the developer community.

Ryan: Yeah, Nanbeige LLM Lab, which is an in-house research lab at BOSS Zhipin, a major online recruitment platform in China, just released Nanbeige4.3-33B. It is a 33-billion-parameter open-source language model, and its defining feature is that it is specifically optimized for agentic coding.

Maya: Agentic coding. Let's break that down for listeners who might be hearing that term for the first time.

Ryan: Sure. Agentic coding is the ability of a model to not just write code, but to operate autonomously across multiple steps: planning a development task, running tools, interpreting results, fixing errors, coordinating with other agents. It goes way beyond simple code autocomplete.

Maya: So what makes this one stand out in a pretty crowded field of model releases?

Ryan: A few things. First, the context window: 524,000 tokens. That is massive. It means the model can hold an enormous amount of information in mind at once, which is critical for working on complex codebases. Then there is the benchmark performance. According to the published results, it outperforms 17 out of 20 benchmarks against competitors in the same size range, including Qwen3.8, Muse-Glimmer, and Google's Gemma 4 31B.

Maya: And it is open-source, so anyone can use it?

Ryan: That is what makes this a big deal. In the space of open-source models specifically optimized for agentic coding, there really have not been many notable options. Meta released Muse-Glimmer in October 2026. Nanbeige4.3-33B is building on that momentum, and it is a clear sign that the open-source ecosystem in this specific niche is starting to mature in a serious way.

Maya: Staying on frameworks, let's talk about something a bit more academic but with very real practical implications. The INFORMS JRING forum presented a model for managing how authority gets delegated to AI agents in supply chain applications. Ryan, what is the core issue here?

Ryan: The core issue is a fundamental question for any business deploying autonomous agents. When you let an AI agent make operational decisions, like managing inventory, placing orders, allocating resources, how far can it go without a human signing off?

Maya: And this framework actually answers that?

Ryan: It does. What was presented is a model called A-DoA, which stands for Agent Delegation-of-Authority, with architectures named StoreNode and TimeSplit for omnichannel inventory promising. The central idea is to define multi-level regimes of autonomous execution. In plain English: the agent can act on its own up to a certain decision threshold, then it has to ask for approval, and beyond another threshold, the decision gets escalated up the chain.

Maya: It is kind of like signature authority levels in a traditional company, but applied to AI.

Ryan: That is a great analogy. And what is notable is that this is being presented as the first formally structured approach of its kind for supply chain, with guardrails specifically designed for the transition from simulation to production. Because that is usually where things get complicated. The model worked great in testing, but once it is live with real data and real stakes, behavior can start to drift.

Maya: Now let's get into notable research. And this one is going to be directly relevant to developers who run coding agents locally. Ryan, tell us about OpenHands.

Ryan: OpenHands is an open-source platform for running coding agents directly on your own machine, no cloud service required. A detailed technical guide published this week walks through the setup in 12 steps, with an estimated time of about 60 minutes.

Maya: And the interesting part is the lineup of models you can actually run with it?

Ryan: Exactly. The guide confirms compatibility with several recent models. You have got models from the Nemotron 3.5 family, in Lightning and Super VL versions, the Qwen models, and a notable confirmation: Recursive Flint, which was announced on October 21st, 2026, is now validated as a modern local option for running agents through OpenHands. All of this can work with Ollama or an Agent Canvas for distributed execution on your own infrastructure.

Maya: For developers who care about privacy or who are working on confidential projects, this is a genuinely compelling alternative.

Ryan: Absolutely. And it fits into a bigger trend. The local coding agent ecosystem is maturing fast. Six months ago, this was still territory only for very advanced users. Today, a 12-step guide you can get through in an hour is a pretty clear sign that this is becoming accessible to a much broader audience.

Maya: Alright, we are moving into our industry impact segment, and we have got three strong stories today. Let's start with healthcare. Ryan, Corti and Dedalus. This is about AI agents inside European hospitals.

Ryan: Yeah, and this is concrete, real-world development with implications for millions of patients. Corti, a company specializing in AI for healthcare, is expanding its partnership with Dedalus, which is the dominant electronic medical records system in Europe, deployed across hospitals throughout the continent.

Maya: What actually changes with this expansion?

Ryan: What is being formalized is the native integration of agentic capabilities into the Dedalus system, specifically into its ORBIS version. In practice, Corti's agents will be able to operate directly inside clinical workflows, with a new execution and control layer for multi-agent orchestration. That means multiple AI agents can collaborate within a hospital environment on tasks like records management, post-consultation data entry, and clinical protocol tracking.

Maya: That is ambitious. Do we know what this actually looks like day-to-day for healthcare workers?

Ryan: The stated goal is to reduce the administrative burden on medical staff, who currently spend a huge chunk of their time on documentation rather than patient care. And what is also significant is the scale here. Because Dedalus is deployed across so many countries, this agentic rollout has the potential to touch a very wide hospital ecosystem across Europe.

Maya: Another major industry story this week: GPT-6 is coming to Google Cloud. Ryan, on the surface that sounds almost contradictory, OpenAI running on Google's infrastructure. But that is actually what is happening?

Ryan: It is, and symbolically it is a big moment. OpenAI and Google Cloud have formalized a deal that makes GPT-6 and Codex workflows available directly through Google Cloud Model Garden. Enterprise customers can access these models with unified billing through their existing Google Cloud spending commitments, with compatibility with Flexible Savings Plans.

Maya: Strategically, what does this actually mean?

Ryan: It means OpenAI is now available across all three major American hyperscalers. That is maximum distribution. For companies that already have Google Cloud contracts and did not want to juggle another vendor relationship, this is a direct on-ramp to GPT-6 without changing any of their existing infrastructure.

Maya: And we are wrapping up the industry segment with China, where the situation around AI export controls is developing. Ryan, what is the latest?

Ryan: This is actually good news for the global open-source ecosystem. You might remember that in mid-October, China announced new export controls on AI models. Well, this week, according to the state broadcaster CCTV, DeepSeek and MiniMax have both successfully completed the registration process required under these new rules.

Maya: So they can keep distributing their models internationally?

Ryan: Exactly. Both companies retain their open-source licensing rights and their international export capabilities, but now under a formalized oversight framework. It confirms that the exemption registration process works, and that China is not cutting its major AI players off from the world, it is regulating how they distribute. For the international developer community that relies on these models, that is a reassuring signal.
